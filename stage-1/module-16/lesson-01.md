## Why this lesson exists

Chocolate is the one ingredient in the pastry kitchen that is already a finished, engineered product before you touch it: a suspension of sugar and cocoa particles in a fat that crystallizes in six ways. Two bars both labelled "70 %" can behave completely differently in a ganache, and a few drops of steam can turn a bowl of melted chocolate into a stiff paste. This lesson gives you the model you need to read a label, choose the right chocolate or cocoa, store it and melt it without damage, so that ganache (16.2) and tempering (16.3) become predictable.

## You will be able to

- Describe the main steps from cocoa bean to chocolate and say which property of the final product each step controls.
- Read a chocolate label: explain what the percentage measures and what it does not, and estimate fat and sugar content from it.
- Distinguish couverture, ordinary eating chocolate and compound coating, and choose between them for a job.
- Choose natural or Dutch-processed cocoa for a formula according to its leavening system.
- Store and melt chocolate within its temperature limits, explain why a little water makes it seize and a lot of water does not, and rescue seized chocolate.

## From bean to bar

Chocolate starts as the seeds of the cacao tree (*Theobroma cacao*), about 30–50 per pod, embedded in a sweet white pulp. Every step afterwards is a process variable that the chocolate maker controls ([Beckett]; [McGee]).

| Step | What is done | Typical conditions | What it controls in the finished chocolate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fermentation** | Beans and pulp heaped or boxed under leaves or sacking | 5–7 days (2–3 for some varieties); heap temperature rises to ~45–50 °C | Yeasts then bacteria ferment the pulp to alcohol and acetic acid, which kill the seed and trigger reactions that form the **flavour precursors** (amino acids, reducing sugars). Unfermented beans make astringent, flat, "purple" chocolate |
| **Drying** | Sun-dried on mats or trays, or in dryers | 1–2 weeks sun-drying, to ~7 % moisture | Stops mould; lets volatile acids escape. Too fast traps acid; too slow risks mould and off-flavours |
| **Roasting** | Whole beans or nibs roasted | Roughly 110–150 °C for 10–40 min, depending on bean and style | **Maillard reactions** ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)) turn precursors into chocolate flavour and colour; loosens the shell |
| **Winnowing** | Beans cracked; shells blown away from the heavier kernel pieces | — | Gives **nibs**: the kernel, about 50–57 % fat (cocoa butter) |
| **Grinding** | Nibs ground; the fat melts from the heat of friction | Above ~35 °C | Gives **cocoa liquor** (cocoa mass): a fluid paste of fine cocoa particles in melted cocoa butter. No alcohol: "liquor" means liquid |
| **Mixing** | Liquor mixed with sugar, extra cocoa butter, milk powder, vanilla | — | Sets the recipe: the percentage, the fat content, the milk content |
| **Refining** | Paste passed through roll refiners (or ground in a mélangeur) | Particle size down to roughly 20–30 µm | Smoothness. Particles above ~30 µm start to feel gritty on the tongue; very fine particles have more surface to coat and make the chocolate thicker |
| **Conching** | Paste kneaded and aerated in a heated conche | Hours to days; roughly 50–80 °C (lower for milk) | Drives off moisture and harsh volatile acids, coats every particle with fat, lowers viscosity and rounds the flavour. Lecithin is usually added near the end |
| **Tempering and moulding** | Chocolate cooled and seeded to form stable cocoa-butter crystals, then moulded | See [16.3](stage-1/module-16/lesson-03.md) | Gloss, snap, release from the mould, shelf life |

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Two steps make most of the flavour: fermentation creates the precursors, roasting turns them into flavour. Two steps make most of the texture: refining sets particle size, conching sets how well the fat coats the particles. The chocolate you buy is therefore a *suspension*: solid particles (sugar crystals, non-fat cocoa solids, milk powder) dispersed in a continuous phase of fat (cocoa butter and, in milk chocolate, milk fat). Everything in this module follows from that picture.

</div>

## What is in a bar

### The four families of ingredient

| Component | What it is | Typical share | What it does on the bench |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cocoa butter** | The fat of the cocoa kernel; a narrow-melting mix of triglycerides, solid at room temperature, melting just below body temperature | 30–45 % of a dark chocolate (from liquor plus added butter) | Fluidity when melted; snap and gloss when tempered; the fat phase of ganache |
| **Non-fat cocoa solids** | The fine, dry particles of the kernel: fibre, protein, starch, polyphenols, flavour | 15–35 % of a dark chocolate | Flavour, bitterness, colour; absorb water and thicken (important in ganache) |
| **Sugar** | Fine sucrose crystals | ~30–50 % in dark, 40–55 % in milk and white | Sweetness; dissolves in any water present (the cause of seizing) |
| **Milk solids** | Milk powder or crumb: lactose, milk proteins, milk fat | 15–30 % in milk and white | Caramel/dairy flavour; milk fat softens cocoa butter; milk proteins scorch at lower temperatures |
| **Lecithin** | Emulsifier, usually from soya or sunflower | ~0.3–0.5 % | Coats sugar particles so they slide past each other in the fat: lowers viscosity for the same fat content |

### What the percentage means

The percentage on the front of a bar is the **total of cocoa-derived ingredients**: cocoa liquor + added cocoa butter + any cocoa powder. It is **not** the cocoa butter content, and it is not the "cocoa solids" in the sense of the non-fat particles. Almost everything else is sugar.

Two "70 %" chocolates can differ a great deal. Take nibs at 55 % fat:

| | Chocolate X | Chocolate Y |
|---|---|---|
| Cocoa liquor | 70 % | 60 % |
| Added cocoa butter | 0 % | 10 % |
| **Label percentage** | **70 %** | **70 %** |
| Cocoa butter (liquor × 0.55 + added) | 38.5 % | 43.0 % |
| Non-fat cocoa solids (liquor × 0.45) | 31.5 % | 27.0 % |
| Sugar + lecithin + vanilla | ~30 % | ~30 % |

Y is more fluid when melted (more fat relative to particles), better for thin coatings, and in a ganache it brings more fat that the water phase must hold. X is thicker, more intensely flavoured and makes a firmer ganache because its extra cocoa particles absorb water. The total fat is printed in the nutrition panel; use it. This is why you should record the brand and the fat content, not only the percentage, whenever you standardize a chocolate formula.

### Dark, milk and white

| Type | Cocoa-derived (label %) | Milk solids | Sugar | Typical total fat | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dark** (bittersweet/semisweet) | ~50–85 % | 0 (traces allowed) | ~15–50 % | ~30–45 % | Higher % = less sugar, more bitterness, usually more fat and solids |
| **Milk** | ~25–45 % | ~15–25 % | ~35–50 % | ~30–40 % | Milk fat softens it; works at lower temperatures |
| **White** | ~20–35 % (cocoa butter only) | ~20–30 % | ~40–55 % | ~30–40 % | No non-fat cocoa solids, so no brown colour; the most heat-sensitive |

Regulations set minimums. In the EU, for example, chocolate must contain at least 35 % total dry cocoa solids including at least 18 % cocoa butter; white chocolate at least 20 % cocoa butter and 14 % milk solids; and "couverture" at least 31 % cocoa butter. US standards of identity are similar in spirit but defined differently (in terms of chocolate liquor). You do not need the legal details; you need to know that the label percentage and the fat content are two separate facts.

### Couverture versus compound coating

| | Couverture | Eating chocolate (bars, chips) | Compound (coating, "candy melts", some "baking chocolate") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fat | Cocoa butter (+ milk fat), high: typically ≥ 31 % cocoa butter | Cocoa butter, often less | Cocoa butter partly or wholly **replaced by other vegetable fats** (e.g. palm-kernel or coconut) |
| Melted fluidity | High: thin coatings, clean moulding. Some brands grade fluidity on the pack | Variable; chips often contain less fat so they hold shape in cookies | Set by the fat; usually fluid |
| Tempering | **Required** for gloss, snap and release | Required if you want it to set glossy | **Not required**: sets by simple cooling |
| Eating quality | Clean melt just below mouth temperature | Good | Waxy or less clean melt; weaker flavour |
| Use | Tempered work, ganache, fine pastry | Baking inclusions, eating | Quick dips, decorations for which speed matters more than quality |

Compound coatings made with lauric fats (palm kernel, coconut) are **incompatible** with cocoa butter: mixing even small amounts of real chocolate into them, or dipping a centre made with one into the other, softens both and causes bloom. Keep them apart. Chocolate chips are formulated with lower fat (and sometimes stabilizers) so they hold their shape in an oven; they melt thickly and are a poor choice for tempering or glazes ([R1-11](recipes/R1-11-chocolate-chip-cookie.md)).

## Cocoa powder

Cocoa powder is cocoa liquor pressed to remove most of the cocoa butter, then ground. Standard powders keep **10–12 % fat**; "high-fat" powders **20–22 %**.

| Type | Process | pH (in water) | Colour and flavour | Leavening partner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Pressed and ground | ~5–6 (acidic) | Lighter, reddish-brown; sharper, fruitier | Can supply acid for **baking soda** |
| **Dutch-processed (alkalized)** | Nibs or liquor treated with an alkali (usually potassium carbonate) before pressing | ~7–8 (neutral to alkaline); "black" cocoas higher | Darker, from red-brown to near black; milder, rounder; disperses better in liquids | Brings **no** acid: pair with **baking powder**, or add another acid if the formula uses soda |

This links straight to [08.2 Chemical leavening](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md). A cake leavened with soda and natural cocoa uses the cocoa's acidity to generate gas; swap in Dutch cocoa and the soda has less acid to react with: less lift, a soapy or metallic note from unreacted soda, a darker and denser crumb ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)). Swap the other way, natural cocoa into a baking-powder formula, and the extra acid can reduce the powder's effect slightly and lighten the colour. Rule: **when you change the cocoa type, re-check the leavening.** Colour also depends on pH: alkaline batters brown darker and redder, which is why old "devil's food" formulas use extra soda.

Cocoa powder is also a *drier*: its fibre and starch absorb water, typically more than their own weight in a batter. Replacing 10 % of the flour in a cake with cocoa makes it drier unless you add liquid ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)).

## Storing chocolate

| Factor | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | **15–18 °C**, stable | Above ~20–24 °C cocoa butter starts to soften and, with daily swings, recrystallizes into unstable forms at the surface: **fat bloom** |
| Humidity | Dry, below ~50–60 % RH | Moisture dissolves surface sugar: **sugar bloom**; damp chocolate may seize when melted |
| Odours | Sealed, away from spices, coffee, onions, cleaning products | Cocoa butter absorbs odours readily |
| Light and air | Opaque, closed packaging | Light and oxygen oxidize milk fat in milk and white chocolate (stale, cardboard flavour) |
| Refrigerator | Avoid for bars and couverture | Condensation when it comes out: sugar bloom. If you must, wrap tightly and let it warm to room temperature **before** opening |

Stored this way, dark chocolate keeps well for a year or more; milk and white, whose milk fat oxidizes, closer to 6–12 months. Bloomed chocolate is safe and can be melted and retempered ([16.3](stage-1/module-16/lesson-03.md)).

## Melting safely

Melting erases the crystal structure; that is fine, because you will either use the chocolate in a mixture (ganache, batter) or temper it. What you must avoid is **overheating** and **water**.

| Chocolate | Maximum melting temperature | What goes wrong above it |
|---|---|---|
| Dark | **~50 °C** (some makers allow 55 °C) | Thickens, dull, burnt or bitter notes; at worst lumps |
| Milk | **~45 °C** | Milk proteins and lactose clump and scorch: thick, grainy, burnt-dairy taste |
| White | **~40–45 °C** | Same, and fastest: turns thick, yellowish and grainy |

**Bain-marie.** Chop the chocolate (pieces ≤ 1 cm) or use callets. Put it in a dry bowl that sits **over, not in**, a pan with 3–4 cm of water. Bring the water to a bare simmer, turn the heat **off**, then set the bowl on top. The bowl must seal the pan so steam cannot rise round the edge, and must not touch the water. Stir with a dry spatula as it melts; lift it off when two thirds is melted and let the residual heat finish. Wipe the bowl's base when you lift it so no drops fall into the chocolate.

**Microwave.** Plastic or glass bowl, **50 % power, 20–30 s bursts**, stirring well between bursts (the microwave heats unevenly and chocolate holds its shape even when molten inside). Stop when about two thirds is melted and stir to finish. This is the method most professionals use for small quantities because there is no steam.

Check with a probe after stirring. On the bench, the endpoint is fully fluid, glossy, no lumps, at 40–50 °C for dark and 40–45 °C for milk and white.

### Seizing: why a little water ruins chocolate and a lot does not

Melted chocolate is dry particles (sugar, cocoa, milk powder) in fat. A drop of water, a wet spoon or steam does not mix into the fat; it wets the particle surfaces, dissolves a little sugar at the contact points and makes a **sticky syrup that glues particles together** into clumps. The fat is squeezed out, the mixture turns from fluid to a stiff, dull, grainy paste within seconds. That is **seizing** ([McGee]; [Beckett]). A few grams of water per 100 g of chocolate is enough.

Add **more** water, hot and at once, and the picture changes: there is now enough liquid to dissolve the sugar into a continuous syrup phase and wet every particle, so the particles disperse *in* the liquid instead of being glued by it. The fat becomes droplets in that water phase. You have made a sauce, or with cream, a ganache ([16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md)). As a working guide (standard professional practice), a mixture with roughly 20 g of water or more per 100 g of chocolate, added hot and all at once, stays fluid; a few grams seizes it.

| Water per 100 g chocolate | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0 | Fluid melted chocolate |
| ~1–5 g (a drop of steam, a wet spoon) | **Seized**: stiff, grainy paste |
| ~20 g and above, hot, all at once (e.g. cream in ganache) | Smooth, fluid sauce or ganache |

**Rescue.** Seized chocolate cannot be tempered. It can be turned into a sauce or ganache: add **hot** water, milk or cream in 10 g amounts, stirring hard, until it becomes smooth and glossy again. Use it in a batter, sauce or ganache. Adding fat (oil, butter) loosens it somewhat but does not fix the glued particles.

<div class="callout warn">

**Warning:** Flavourings that contain water seize chocolate too: water-based extracts, fruit purée, liqueurs added in small amounts. Add them to the cream in a ganache, or use oil-soluble flavours for pure chocolate.

</div>

## Chocolate in a baked batter

In a brownie, a chocolate cake or a cookie, chocolate is not crystallized; it is melted into a batter and baked. It still does four jobs you can predict:

- **Fat** (cocoa butter): tenderizes like butter, but it is solid at room temperature, so a chocolate-rich brownie firms as it cools and is fudgier when chilled.
- **Sugar**: sweetens and, dissolved, gives the shiny crust (see [R1-38](recipes/R1-38-brownies.md)).
- **Cocoa particles**: absorb water and act as a drier and a weak structure-builder, like extra flour without gluten.
- **Flavour and colour**, including acidity: dark chocolate is mildly acidic, as is natural cocoa.

Melting chocolate with butter first (both fats together, ~45–50 °C) is safe: the butter's 16 % water is present, but butter is a W/O emulsion in which the water is locked in droplets, and there is enough fat to keep the mixture fluid. Adding the eggs and sugar afterwards supplies plenty of water: the batter is past the seizing zone.

## On the bench

1. **Label survey (20 min).** Take three chocolates you can buy: a 55–60 % and a 70 % dark and a milk. From each nutrition panel record fat, sugar and, where given, protein per 100 g. Estimate the non-fat cocoa solids: label % − (fat − any milk fat) ≈ non-fat cocoa, and check that sugar ≈ 100 − label % − milk solids. Rank them by fat content and predict which will be most fluid when melted.
2. **Melting trial (30 min).** Melt 50 g of each by microwave, logging the temperature after each burst. Compare fluidity: pour 10 g of each from a spoon at 45 °C onto parchment and measure the diameter after 30 s.
3. **Seize and rescue (15 min).** To 30 g melted dark chocolate at 45 °C, add 1 g water and stir: time until it seizes. Then add hot water in 5 g lots until it is smooth; record the total water at which it becomes glossy and fluid.
4. **Cocoa pH (optional, 10 min).** Stir 5 g natural and 5 g Dutch cocoa each into 50 g water; test with pH strips.
5. **Bake [R1-38 Brownies](recipes/R1-38-brownies.md)**, noting the chocolate you used (brand, %, fat) on the formula sheet.

## What goes wrong

- **Chocolate turns to a stiff grainy paste while melting.** Water or steam: [Chocolate seized](troubleshooting/chocolate.md?id=chocolate-seized).
- **Melted chocolate is thick, dull or smells scorched.** Overheated, especially milk and white: [Thick, burnt or grainy melted chocolate](troubleshooting/chocolate.md?id=thick-burnt-or-grainy-melted-chocolate).
- **Stored chocolate has grey streaks or white dust.** Fat or sugar bloom from storage: [Grey streaks or haze after setting](troubleshooting/chocolate.md?id=grey-streaks-or-haze-after-setting), [White dusty surface](troubleshooting/chocolate.md?id=white-dusty-surface).
- **A cocoa cake is flat and tastes soapy.** Dutch cocoa swapped into a soda formula: see [08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md).

## Lab notebook

- For every chocolate you use: brand, product name, label %, fat, sugar and protein per 100 g, couverture or not, fluidity rating if printed.
- Melting method, bursts or time, temperature log, final temperature.
- Seize test: grams of water that seized 30 g; grams of hot water that rescued it.
- Spread diameters for the fluidity comparison.
- Storage conditions of your chocolate (temperature, container) and any bloom seen.

## Check yourself

1. A bar says "72 % cocoa". A student concludes it contains 72 % cocoa butter. Correct the statement and say what the other 28 % mainly is.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

72 % is the total of cocoa-derived ingredients: cocoa liquor (fat + non-fat solids) plus any added cocoa butter. Its cocoa butter is typically around 40–45 % and must be read from the nutrition panel. The other 28 % is almost all sugar, plus lecithin and vanilla.

</details>

2. You replace a 70 % chocolate (38 % fat) with another 70 % (46 % fat) in a ganache at the same ratio. Predict two differences.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The higher-fat chocolate has fewer non-fat cocoa particles to absorb water and more fat for the water phase to hold, so the ganache is likely softer after setting and more prone to looking greasy or splitting. When melted, the higher-fat chocolate is more fluid.

</details>

3. A devil's food cake uses natural cocoa and 1 % baking soda (on flour). You only have Dutch-processed cocoa. What will happen if you swap it directly, and what can you change?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dutch cocoa brings no acid, so the soda is not neutralized: less gas, a denser cake, darker and redder colour and possibly a soapy/metallic taste. Replace most of the soda with baking powder (roughly 3–4× its weight as a starting point, then test), or add an acid ingredient such as buttermilk to react with the soda ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)).

</details>

4. Why does a drop of water seize melted chocolate, but 100 g of hot cream poured on 100 g of chocolate gives a smooth ganache?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A small amount of water wets the particle surfaces and dissolves a little sugar into a sticky syrup that glues particles into clumps, squeezing the fat out: a stiff paste. Enough water dissolves the sugar into a continuous syrup phase and disperses the particles and fat droplets in it: a fluid emulsion-suspension (ganache).

</details>

5. You must melt white chocolate for a glaze and have only a bain-marie and a gas hob. List three precautions.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Keep it below ~40–45 °C: water barely simmering then heat off; bowl sealing the pan and not touching the water so no steam reaches the chocolate; stir with a dry spatula; lift off when two thirds melted and let residual heat finish; check with a probe; wipe the bowl base.

</details>
