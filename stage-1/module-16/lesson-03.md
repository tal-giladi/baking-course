## Why this lesson exists

Melt a glossy bar of chocolate and let it set on its own, and it comes back dull, streaky, soft, sticky to the touch and grey within days. The ingredients are identical; only the crystals have changed. Tempering is the procedure that decides which crystals form, and it is the gateway to every piece of chocolate work: dipped confections, décor, moulded bars and bonbons, and a finish on a tart that looks professional. It is also the clearest case in the course of a process controlled entirely by temperature and nucleation.

## You will be able to

- Explain cocoa butter polymorphism and why only form V gives gloss, snap, contraction and bloom resistance.
- Temper dark, milk or white chocolate by the seeding method, with the correct temperatures for each, and verify temper with a test strip.
- Keep chocolate in temper while working, and recognize when it is over-crystallized or out of temper.
- Use tempered chocolate for dipping, discs and décor on acetate, and a simple moulded bar.
- Distinguish fat bloom from sugar bloom, and prevent both.

## Cocoa butter can crystallize six ways

Cocoa butter is a mixture of triglycerides dominated by a few very similar molecules (mostly palmitic, stearic and oleic acids arranged in a particular pattern on glycerol). Because the molecules are so alike, cocoa butter melts over a narrow range, just below body temperature. It can also pack into **six crystal forms (polymorphs)**, numbered I to VI in order of increasing stability, density and melting point ([Beckett]; [SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)).

| Form | Approx. melting point | What you would see if it dominated |
|---|---|---|
| I | ~17 °C | Soft, melts in the hand; forms only with rapid deep cooling |
| II | ~23 °C | Soft, unstable |
| III | ~25.5 °C | Soft, dull |
| IV | ~27.5 °C | Soft, dull, bloom-prone: what chocolate usually forms if left to set on its own |
| **V** | **~33–34 °C** | **Gloss, clean snap, contraction and release from the mould, melts cleanly in the mouth: the target** |
| VI | ~36 °C | Forms slowly from V over months; harder, duller, associated with bloom in old chocolate |

Reported melting points vary by a degree or so between sources and methods; the ordering does not.

### Why form V is the one you want

- **Gloss:** form V crystals are small and uniform and pack densely, so the surface is smooth enough to reflect light like a mirror.
- **Snap:** a dense, continuous crystal network breaks cleanly with an audible crack instead of bending.
- **Contraction:** form V is denser than the liquid and than the unstable forms, so the chocolate shrinks slightly as it sets and pulls away from a mould. Untempered chocolate sticks.
- **Melt:** it melts at ~33–34 °C, just below mouth temperature (~37 °C): solid in the hand for a few seconds, melting cleanly on the tongue.
- **Stability:** a surface of stable form V crystals resists the slow recrystallization that causes fat bloom.

### Why chocolate does not form V on its own

Crystallization needs **nucleation** (first tiny ordered clusters) and **growth** ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)). Unstable forms nucleate **faster**, because their loose packing is easier to achieve; stable forms nucleate slowly. Cool melted chocolate on its own and forms III and IV appear first and take over, slowly converting later and unevenly, which shows as streaks and bloom. Tempering beats the kinetics: it supplies form V nuclei and then holds the chocolate in a temperature window where **form V can exist but the unstable forms cannot**: above ~28 °C (form IV melts) and below ~34 °C (form V melts). That window is why the working temperatures are 28–32 °C.

## Tempering methods

All methods do the same thing: erase all crystals, create or add form V crystals, then remove everything else.

| Method | How | Stage 1? |
|---|---|---|
| **Seeding** | Melt to 45–50 °C; add finely chopped, already tempered chocolate (the seed, full of form V); stir down to working temperature | **Yes: the home standard** ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)) |
| **Tabling** | Melt; pour two thirds onto marble; spread and gather with a scraper and palette knife until it thickens at ~27–28 °C (dark); return to the remaining third; stir to 31–32 °C | Demonstrated; practised in Stage 2 |
| **Cocoa butter seed powder** | Melt; cool to ~34–35 °C; add ~1 % of stabilized form V cocoa-butter powder; stir to working temperature | Optional, if you have it |
| **Machine** | Continuous or batch tempering machine | Stage 2 |

### Seeding, step by step (dark chocolate)

The full procedure, weights and critical control points are in [R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md). The logic:

| Step | Dark | Milk | White | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Melt completely | **45–50 °C** | **40–45 °C** | **40–45 °C** | Erase all existing crystals, including stray unstable ones |
| 2. Add seed | 20–25 % of total weight, as callets or finely chopped tempered chocolate | same | same | Seed brings form V crystals; it also cools the mass |
| 3. Stir down to working temperature | **31–32 °C** | **29–30 °C** | **28–29 °C** | Seed melts partly; form V surviving on seed fragments templates the melt. Stay below form V's melting point and above form IV's |
| 4. Remove unmelted seed, or blend briefly | — | — | — | Lumps are fine to lift out and reuse |
| 5. Test | Sets in ~3–5 min at ~20 °C | same | same | Proves the crystals are there |

**Follow the pack.** These are standard professional ranges. Chocolate manufacturers print their own tempering curve (melt / cool / work temperatures) for each product because milk fat, added cocoa butter and fat composition move the numbers by a degree or two. Where the pack disagrees with the table, use the pack.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Why do milk and white temper lower? Milk fat is liquid at temperatures where cocoa butter is solid and dissolves into it, diluting the cocoa butter and interfering with its packing. The whole crystallization curve shifts down, and the set chocolate is softer. That is also why even a little butter or oil stirred into chocolate for "shine" ruins its snap.

</div>

### Testing temper

Dip the tip of a clean knife, a spoon or a strip of parchment into the chocolate, scrape one side, and leave it on the bench at **~20 °C** (not in the fridge).

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Sets in **3–5 min**, evenly glossy, no streaks, snaps and releases from parchment | **In temper** | Work |
| Still soft or tacky at 5–10 min; sets later with grey streaks or a matte surface | **Not tempered** (too few form V crystals) | Add more seed (5–10 %), stir, re-test; or check the temperature is not above working |
| Sets in under 2 min, the chocolate in the bowl is visibly thickening | **Over-crystallized** (too many crystals) | Warm by ~1 °C (a few seconds of heat, stirring) or stir in a spoon of warm melted chocolate |

### Keeping it in temper

Tempered chocolate is a moving target. As it sits at working temperature, form V crystals keep growing: the chocolate thickens, coats more heavily and eventually sets dull and thick. As it is heated (hairdryer, bain-marie, a warm room), form V crystals melt:

- **Stir every few minutes**, and keep the bowl at working temperature: a heating pad set low under the bowl, a bain-marie at ~35 °C for seconds at a time, or brief bursts of a hairdryer on the surface, stirring immediately.
- **Never exceed working temperature by more than ~1–2 °C.** Dark chocolate at 34 °C has lost most of its seed; at 36 °C it is untempered, however glossy it looks while liquid ([X1-41](experiments/X1-41-tempering.md) variant C).
- **Over-crystallized?** Warm by a degree, or add some warm melted chocolate, and re-test.
- **Lost temper?** Re-melt to 45–50 °C and re-seed. Tempered chocolate that has set can be melted and re-tempered indefinitely.
- Keep water, steam and moist fillings away ([16.1](stage-1/module-16/lesson-01.md), seizing).

## Using tempered chocolate

### The room and the object

Work in a room at **18–22 °C** with low humidity. Objects dipped or coated should be at **room temperature (~20 °C)**, dry: cold centres (from the fridge) set the chocolate too fast into unstable forms and cause condensation; warm centres slow setting and can melt the seed. Let everything set at **~15–18 °C**. Use the refrigerator only briefly (10–15 min) for moulds to help contraction, then return to room temperature. Never let chocolate set in a freezer.

### Three Stage 1 uses

| Use | How | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Dipped items** (candied orange peel, toasted nuts, dried fruit, truffles from [R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md)) | Dry items at room temperature. Dip with a dipping fork or fingers, lift, tap the fork on the bowl rim to thin the coat, scrape the base on the rim, set on parchment or acetate | Thick "feet" at the base (scrape more), streaks (out of temper), cracked coats on truffles (centre too cold) |
| **Discs and décor on acetate** | Spoon or pipe 3–5 g drops onto acetate and tap to spread into 3–4 cm discs (for mendiants, top with nuts and fruit before they set). For sheets, spread 1–2 mm thick with a palette knife; when it has just set but is not hard (~5–10 min), cut shapes with a knife or cutter; cover with parchment and a flat weight to stop curling | The acetate side is mirror glossy; release is clean and complete only when it is in temper |
| **A simple moulded bar** | Polished polycarbonate bar mould, clean and buffed with cotton wool. Fill, tap hard for 30–60 s to release bubbles, scrape the top level, set at 15–18 °C (or 10–15 min in the fridge then out), unmould when the bar has visibly shrunk from the edges | Stuck bars = not tempered or not set long enough; dull patches = dirty mould or water marks |

Silicone moulds are cheap but give a dull finish and do not show contraction; use them for practice only.

## Bloom: two different faults with the same look

| | **Fat bloom** | **Sugar bloom** |
|---|---|---|
| Looks | Grey-white haze or streaks, sometimes in swirls; soft sheen | Dry, white, dusty or rough surface |
| Touch | Greasy; a warm finger melts it away | Dry and gritty; does not smear |
| Water test | A drop of water does not change it | A drop of water dissolves it; it may return as it dries |
| Cause | Poor temper (unstable forms converting); storage too warm or with temperature swings (partial melting and recrystallizing at the surface); slow form V → VI change; liquid fat migrating from nut fillings | Moisture on the surface (condensation when cold chocolate meets warm, humid air; damp storage) dissolves sugar, which recrystallizes as the water evaporates |
| Prevention | Correct temper; stable 15–18 °C storage; no temperature cycling | Store dry; never move chocolate from fridge to open air unwrapped; let it warm in a sealed container |

Both are cosmetic, not a food safety issue. Bloomed chocolate can be melted and re-tempered ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md); [Beckett]).

## Check yourself

1. Why does chocolate need to be heated to 45–50 °C before seeding, when cocoa butter melts at ~34 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

To be sure that every crystal, including stable form V and VI fragments and any unstable crystals left from earlier handling, is melted. Any leftover crystals of the wrong form would act as nuclei for the wrong structure. 45–50 °C gives a margin above the highest-melting form (~36 °C) and through the whole mass.

</details>

2. Your dark chocolate is at 31.5 °C, well seeded, and sets in 4 minutes. You leave it on a warm stove edge for 10 minutes and it reaches 35 °C. It still looks perfect. Is it in temper? What do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Almost certainly not: 35 °C is above form V's melting point, so the seed crystals have melted. Test it to confirm; then either re-melt to 45–50 °C and re-seed, or cool to working temperature while adding fresh seed, and re-test before use.

</details>

3. Why must milk chocolate be worked at 29–30 °C rather than 31–32 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Milk fat dissolves in cocoa butter and disrupts its crystal packing, lowering the melting points of the crystal forms. At 31–32 °C the form V crystals in milk chocolate would start to melt; the working window shifts down about 2 °C. Follow the manufacturer's curve.

</details>

4. Chocolate discs made on acetate come out glossy on day 1, but by day 5 they have grey streaks. The room swings between 19 and 27 °C. Two possible causes?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat bloom from either marginal temper (a mix of forms that converted over days) or storage with large temperature swings, which partly melts and recrystallizes surface fat. Test: a drop of water does not remove it; a warm finger smears it. Store at a stable 15–18 °C and check temper with a test strip next time.

</details>

5. A student keeps bonbons in the fridge and serves them in a warm, humid kitchen. White dusty patches appear. What are they and how do you avoid them?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sugar bloom: condensation on the cold surface dissolves sugar, which recrystallizes as it dries. Store chocolate at 15–18 °C rather than in the fridge, or keep it sealed in a container and let it come to room temperature before opening.

</details>
