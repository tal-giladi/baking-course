## Why this lesson exists

Ganache is two ingredients, chocolate and cream, and it fills more of the pastry kitchen than any other chocolate preparation: glazes, tart fillings, truffles, macaron and bonbon centres, whipped cake fillings. It is also the most common chocolate failure: split, greasy, grainy, too soft to hold, too hard to pipe. Every one of those failures is a predictable consequence of the ratio of fat to water, of the temperature and of how you emulsified it. This lesson gives you the ratio rules and the method, so that you design a ganache for its job instead of hoping.

## You will be able to

- Describe ganache as an emulsion and suspension, and name its fat phase, water phase and dispersed solids.
- Choose a chocolate-to-cream ratio by weight for glazing, filling, piping and truffles, for dark, milk and white chocolate.
- Make a ganache by the hot-cream method with a spatula or a stick blender, with measured temperatures at each stage.
- Diagnose a split or grainy ganache and rescue it.
- Explain what butter, glucose syrup and invert sugar change in texture and shelf life, and flavour a ganache by infusion.

## What a ganache is

When hot cream meets chocolate, the cocoa butter melts, the sugar dissolves in the cream's water, and the mixture reorganizes into three things at once:

| Part | Made of | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous water phase** | Water with dissolved sugar (sucrose from the chocolate, lactose from the cream, any glucose/invert) and dissolved milk proteins | Cream (~58 % water), plus added liquids and sugars |
| **Dispersed fat droplets** | Cocoa butter, milk fat, butter | Chocolate, cream, butter |
| **Dispersed solid particles** | Non-fat cocoa particles, undissolved sugar crystals, milk solids | Chocolate |

So a ganache is an **oil-in-water emulsion** (fat droplets in a sugar syrup) that is also a **suspension** (cocoa particles in the same syrup) ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md); [Beckett]). The emulsifiers are the lecithin already in the chocolate, the milk proteins from the cream, and the fine cocoa particles themselves, which sit at droplet surfaces.

As it cools, the texture sets from three sources: **cocoa butter and milk fat crystallize** inside the droplets (the biggest effect, and why ganache is fluid at 35 °C and firm at 18 °C); **cocoa particles absorb water** and crowd together; and the **sugar-rich water phase** becomes more viscous. More chocolate relative to cream means more fat to crystallize, more particles and less water: firmer.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** A ganache is stable only when its **water phase can hold its fat**. Everything that adds fat (a higher-% chocolate, extra cocoa butter, butter added hot) or removes water (less cream, evaporation) pushes it towards splitting. Everything that adds water phase (a little milk or cream) pulls it back. This is the same phase-volume rule as mayonnaise in [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md).

</div>

## Ratios by weight

Ratios are written **chocolate : cream**, by weight, with whipping cream of 35 % fat. They are starting points from standard professional practice; the exact chocolate moves you within each range.

### Dark chocolate (55–70 %)

| Ratio | Water in finished ganache (approx.) | Texture at 18–20 °C | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 : 1** | ~29 % | Soft, spoonable; fluid at 30–35 °C | Pouring glaze, tart filling, sauce when warm, whipped ganache after chilling |
| **1.5 : 1** | ~23 % | Soft-firm, holds a piped shape | Piped fillings, macaron and tartlet fillings, cake layers |
| **2 : 1** | ~19 % | Firm, rollable, clean-cutting | Truffles, framed and cut centres |
| **2.5–3 : 1** | ~14–17 % | Very firm; with high-% chocolate, near the limit (risk of greasy or grainy) | Only with lower-% chocolate or added liquid/sugar syrups |

Water is estimated as cream × 0.58 ÷ total weight (cream water from [USDA-FDC]); chocolate itself brings almost none.

**The effect of chocolate percentage.** A 70 % chocolate brings more fat and more cocoa particles and less sugar than a 55 % one. The particles absorb water and firm the ganache; the extra fat must be held by less water. So at the same ratio a 70 % ganache is firmer **and** more likely to split. Two practical rules follow:

- Moving from ~55 % to ~70 % chocolate, **reduce the chocolate** by roughly 10–15 % (or increase the cream) for similar firmness.
- For high-% chocolate at firm ratios, add a little glucose syrup or invert sugar: it adds water-compatible solids that thicken the water phase and help it hold the fat.

### Milk and white chocolate

Milk and white chocolates have fewer (milk) or no (white) non-fat cocoa particles, more sugar and milk fat that softens cocoa butter. They set softer, so you need **more chocolate** for the same firmness:

| Target texture | Dark | Milk | White |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glaze, soft filling | 1 : 1 | ~2 : 1 | ~2.5 : 1 |
| Piping | 1.5 : 1 | ~2.5 : 1 | ~3 : 1 |
| Truffle, firm | 2 : 1 | ~2.5–3 : 1 | ~3 : 1 (or more) |
| Whipped (ganache montée, chilled then whipped) | ~1 : 2 | ~1 : 1.5–2 | ~1 : 2–2.5 |

Always test a new chocolate with a small batch (the whole point of [X1-42](experiments/X1-42-ganache-ratio.md)).

## Method: hot cream over chocolate

The target is a mixture that passes through **~35–45 °C** while you emulsify: warm enough that all cocoa butter is liquid and the mixture is fluid, cool enough that fat droplets do not coalesce and milk proteins are not damaged.

| Step | Action | Target / endpoint | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Chop | Chocolate in pieces ≤ 1 cm, or callets, in a deep bowl or a tall jug | Even pieces | Melts evenly and quickly from the cream's heat |
| 2. Heat the cream | Cream (with glucose/invert if used) brought just to a simmer, or to **~80 °C** | Small bubbles at the edge | Enough heat to melt the chocolate; brief heating keeps evaporation low |
| 3. Pour and rest | Pour over the chocolate in one go; tap to settle; **rest 1 min** | Chocolate submerged, softening | Heat moves into the chocolate before you start stirring |
| 4. Emulsify | **Spatula:** stir in small, fast circles at the **centre** until a glossy, elastic "kernel" forms, then widen the circles to take in the rest. **Stick blender:** tilt it in, blade fully **submerged**, run without lifting; move slowly around the bottom | Mixture glossy, smooth, elastic, **~35–45 °C** | The kernel is a small zone where emulsion forms first, like the start of a mayonnaise, then recruits the rest. The blender makes smaller droplets: smoother, more stable |
| 5. Add butter (if used) | Soft butter (~20 °C) in small cubes at **35–40 °C**, blended in | Glossy, no streaks | Hot ganache melts butter into oil that may break the emulsion; cold butter chunks can't be dispersed |
| 6. Use or set | Glaze: use at **30–35 °C**. Piping and truffles: cover with film on the surface and crystallize at **16–18 °C for ~12 h** | See [R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md) | Slow, undisturbed cooling lets cocoa butter form fine, stable crystals: smooth texture |

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** The stick blender is the default in professional kitchens. Keep the head **submerged and tilted** to avoid sucking in air (bubbles show in a glaze and shorten shelf life), and use a container just wider than the blender head. The glossy, elastic look of a well-emulsified ganache (it forms a sheen and "stretches" off the spatula) is the bench endpoint.

</div>

Why not just melt chocolate and stir in cold cream? You can make a ganache that way (melted chocolate at 45 °C plus warm cream, combined by blender) and many professionals do for precise temperature control; but pouring cold cream into warm chocolate in small amounts passes straight through the seizing zone of [16.1](stage-1/module-16/lesson-01.md). If you add liquid to chocolate, add it **warm** and in **at least a third of the total at once**, then blend.

### Crystallizing and holding

- **Glaze (1:1):** strain if needed, blend to remove bubbles, and cool to **30–35 °C** before pouring over a chilled cake or tart. Hotter runs thin and shows fingerprints of the cake; cooler is thick and leaves marks.
- **Piping ganache (1.5:1):** either pipe as soon as it is thick enough to hold a shape (~26–28 °C, it will set in place), or cover, crystallize 12 h at 16–18 °C and pipe then, beating briefly to soften if needed.
- **Truffle ganache (2:1):** pour into a lined frame or tray 1–1.5 cm deep, or a bowl, crystallize 12 h at 16–18 °C, then pipe or scoop and roll.
- **Refrigerating** to speed setting gives a coarser crystal structure and, often, a slightly grainy, dull ganache that is harder to pipe. It works for a tart filling; it is a compromise for truffles.

## Broken ganache

A **split** (broken) ganache looks greasy or oily at the surface, with a curdled or grainy body, often with liquid fat pooling at the edges. It feels oily in the mouth. The fat droplets have merged and escaped the water phase.

| Cause | Mechanism | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Too hot** (cream boiled hard onto a small amount of chocolate; mixture above ~50 °C; reheated carelessly) | Low viscosity, fast droplet collision and coalescence; milk proteins less effective | Cream ~80 °C; emulsify at 35–45 °C |
| **Too much fat for the water phase** | High-% or high-fat chocolate, firm ratio, butter added: not enough water to separate the droplets | Match ratio to chocolate; add glucose/invert; keep butter ≤ ~10–15 % of chocolate |
| **Too little water** (cream reduced by long boiling, infusion that evaporated) | Same as above | Reweigh the cream after infusing and top up to the original weight |
| **Agitation at the wrong time** (whisking hard while it cools, or stirring while it crystallizes) | Air incorporated; forced crystallization; droplets collide as fat solidifies | Emulsify once, at 35–45 °C, then leave it still |
| **Cold additions** (cold butter, cold liqueur) | Local crystallization of fat and poor dispersion | Add at room temperature to a 35–40 °C ganache |

**The fix.** Bring the ganache to **~32–35 °C** (a few seconds in the microwave, stirred, or a bain-marie). Add **5–10 %** of its weight of **warm milk or cream** (~35–40 °C) and blend with the stick blender, head submerged, until glossy and elastic. If that is not enough, start a fresh "kernel": 20–30 g warm milk in a clean jug, add the broken ganache gradually while blending, exactly as you rescue a mayonnaise. The added water gives the fat somewhere to be dispersed again ([09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md)).

**Grainy ganache** has a different cause: either the cocoa butter crystallized coarsely (cooled too fast, stirred while setting, stored cold) or sugar crystallized over days (too little water, no invert or glucose). Warm to 34–35 °C and blend to melt the crystals, then let it set undisturbed at 16–18 °C. Sugar graininess is prevented by formulation, not technique.

## Butter, sugars and shelf life

| Addition | Typical level (on chocolate weight) | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Butter** | 5–15 % | Softer, silkier texture and faster, richer melt in the mouth (milk fat softens cocoa butter). Adds fat: watch the fat/water balance |
| **Glucose syrup** | 5–10 % | Water-compatible solids: thickens the water phase, improves gloss, slows sugar crystallization, lowers water activity |
| **Invert sugar** (or honey, with its flavour) | 5–10 % | As glucose, and more effective per gram at lowering water activity and keeping ganache soft; slightly sweeter |
| **Sorbitol** (Stage 2) | small | Lowers water activity with less sweetness |

**Water activity (a<sub>w</sub>)** is the measure of how "available" the water in a food is to microbes ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)). A simple cream ganache sits roughly around 0.88–0.95: moist enough for moulds and some bacteria. Sugars and glucose/invert bind water and lower it; so does a firmer ratio (less water). Professional bonbon ganaches are formulated to around 0.80–0.85 for weeks of shelf life at 15–18 °C; that is Stage 2 and 3 work. For Stage 1, treat ganache as a **perishable dairy product**: cover it, keep cream ganache for up to 2 days at a cool room temperature (≤ 18 °C) or about a week refrigerated, and freeze for longer ([FDA-FoodCode] for time and temperature control of dairy-based foods).

## Flavouring by infusion

Flavour goes into the **cream**, not the chocolate:

- **Hot infusion:** bring the cream to the boil with the aromatic (vanilla pod, citrus zest, whole spices, coffee beans, tea), cover, infuse 5–15 min (tea 3–5 min, or it turns bitter), strain, **re-weigh and top up with fresh cream** to the original weight, reheat to ~80 °C.
- **Cold infusion:** aromatic in cold cream overnight in the refrigerator (herbs, coffee, some teas): fresher, less cooked flavour.
- **Alcohol and liqueurs:** up to ~5–10 % of ganache weight, added at 35 °C at the end with the blender; they add water (and some lower a<sub>w</sub>), so a firm ratio may need a little more chocolate.
- **Purées** (passion fruit, raspberry): they replace part of the cream as the water phase; acidic purées can split a milk-rich ganache less than you would expect, but they bring less fat, so the ganache is firmer. Stage 2 formulates these properly.

## Check yourself

1. Calculate the approximate water percentage of a ganache of 300 g dark chocolate and 200 g cream (35 % fat, 58 % water). Which texture would you expect at 18 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water = 200 × 0.58 = 116 g; total 500 g; water ≈ 23 %. Ratio 1.5:1: a soft-firm, pipeable ganache.

</details>

2. You have a truffle formula at 2:1 with a 58 % chocolate. You switch to a 72 % chocolate and the ganache splits. Give two formulation changes that could fix it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Reduce the chocolate (e.g. to about 1.7–1.8:1) so there is more water phase per gram of fat; and/or add 5–10 % glucose syrup or invert sugar (on chocolate weight) to strengthen the water phase. Also emulsify at 35–45 °C with a stick blender.

</details>

3. Why does milk chocolate need a higher ratio than dark chocolate for the same firmness?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It has fewer non-fat cocoa particles to absorb water and firm the mixture, more sugar, and milk fat that softens cocoa butter crystallization. It sets softer, so more chocolate (about 2.5–3:1 for a truffle) is needed.

</details>

4. Your ganache looks oily and grainy at 50 °C after you whisked it hard. What do you do, step by step?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Let it cool (stirring gently) to about 32–35 °C. Add 5–10 % of its weight of warm milk or cream (35–40 °C) and blend with a submerged stick blender until glossy and elastic. If still broken, blend 20–30 g warm milk in a clean jug and add the broken ganache gradually while blending.

</details>

5. A pastry chef adds 8 % invert sugar to a piping ganache. Name two effects on the product.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lower water activity (longer shelf life), softer and smoother texture that stays pipeable, delayed sugar crystallization (less graininess over days), slightly sweeter, often better gloss.

</details>
