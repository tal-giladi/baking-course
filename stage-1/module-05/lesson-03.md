## Why this lesson exists

Every preferment in [05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md) used a single organism, baker's yeast, that you bought. A sourdough culture is a small, stable ecosystem of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria that you grow and keep alive yourself. It leavens, acidifies and flavours the bread at once, and how you feed it decides the balance between those three jobs. This lesson gives you a working model of that ecosystem, so that when your starter behaves oddly you can reason about which population is winning and why, instead of following folklore. Start [R1-05](recipes/R1-05-levain.md) the day you read this: a new starter takes one to two weeks.

## You will be able to

1. Describe the two microbial groups in a sourdough, what each produces, and why they coexist rather than compete.
2. Distinguish homofermentative from heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria and explain where lactic and acetic acid come from.
3. Predict how temperature, hydration and feeding ratio shift a levain's speed, acidity and flavour.
4. Establish a starter from flour and water, and explain the "false rise" in the first days.
5. Maintain, store, refresh and rescue a starter, and judge readiness with measurable cues (rise, time to peak, pH, smell).

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning in this course |
|---|---|
| **Starter** (mother, chef) | The culture you keep and feed indefinitely |
| **Levain** | The preferment you build from the starter for a particular bake. In practice people use the words loosely |
| **Feed / refresh** | Mixing some starter with fresh flour and water |
| **Feeding ratio** | Starter : flour : water by weight. 1:2:2 means 10 g starter + 20 g flour + 20 g water |
| **Inoculation** | The proportion of old culture in a feed. 1:2:2 is a higher inoculation than 1:5:5 |
| **Peak** | Maximum rise after a feed. After it, the culture starts to fall back |
| **Hydration** | Water as % of flour in the culture. 100 % is "liquid", 50–60 % is "stiff" |

## The ecosystem

A mature sourdough typically contains around 10⁸–10⁹ lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and 10⁶–10⁸ yeast cells per gram. The bacteria outnumber the yeasts by roughly 10–100 to one. The yeasts are much larger cells, though, and they do most of the leavening ([Arora 2021] reviews three decades of work on sourdough microbiology).

### Lactic acid bacteria

LAB are bacteria that ferment sugars mainly to lactic acid. They tolerate acid well, which is why they end up dominating a system that they themselves acidify. Two metabolic types matter to you:

| Type | From one glucose | Leavening | Effect on flavour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Homofermentative** | 2 lactic acid | None (no CO₂) | Clean, mild, yogurt-like acidity |
| **Heterofermentative** | 1 lactic acid + CO₂ + ethanol, **or** + acetic acid when the cell has an extra electron acceptor available (fructose, oxygen) | Some CO₂ | Lactic plus sharper, vinegary acetic notes |

Sourdoughs that are refreshed frequently are usually dominated by **heterofermentative** LAB. The best known is *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, formerly *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*. (In 2020 the old genus *Lactobacillus* was split into many new genera, so you will see names such as *Fructilactobacillus*, *Lactiplantibacillus* and *Limosilactobacillus* in recent sources.) Homofermentative species such as *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* are common in young starters and in warmer, less frequently refreshed cultures.

**Lactic acid** tastes mild and rounded. **Acetic acid** is volatile: you smell it as well as taste it. It is sharper and more vinegary, and at the same concentration it tastes more "sour". The ratio of lactic to acetic acid, the **fermentation quotient (FQ)**, is how bakers and researchers describe a sourdough's acid balance. A lower FQ means relatively more acetic acid.

### Wild yeasts

Sourdough yeasts are acid-tolerant species. Common ones include ***Kazachstania humilis*** (formerly *Candida humilis* or *C. milleri*) and ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae***, the same species as baker's yeast, though wild strains. Many others appear in particular starters. They produce CO₂ and ethanol, and ester and alcohol aroma compounds, as baker's yeast does, but usually more slowly.

### Why they coexist

The classic example is the pairing of *F. sanfranciscensis* and *K. humilis*. The bacterium strongly prefers **maltose**, the sugar amylases release from flour starch ([05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)). It splits maltose and releases some of the glucose unused. *K. humilis* cannot use maltose but readily uses glucose. So they do not compete for the main sugar. The yeast also tolerates the acid that the bacteria produce. Other exchanges, such as amino acids and vitamins, support the partnership. The general principle holds across starters: the stable community is one where the members occupy different niches, and the acidity keeps outsiders out.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Why is a mature starter so stable? Its pH falls to ~3.5–4 within hours of each feed. Most spoilage bacteria and moulds that arrive with the flour cannot grow at that pH in competition with an established, fast-growing LAB population. The culture re-selects itself every feed. See [SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md) for the microbiology and [SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md) for pH.

</div>

## Acidity: pH and total acidity

pH is a logarithmic measure of hydrogen-ion concentration ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)). A drop from pH 5 to 4 is a tenfold increase in free acidity. Fresh flour and water sit around pH 6. A **ripe levain at peak is typically pH ~3.8–4.2**. A starter left long past its peak drops to ~3.5 or below.

pH is not the whole story. **Total titratable acidity (TTA)** measures the *amount* of acid present, including acid held by buffers. Wholemeal flour, and rye in particular, contains more minerals and proteins that buffer acid (higher ash, [02.1](stage-1/module-02/lesson-01.md)). A wholemeal levain can therefore hold more acid at the same pH, and it tastes more sour than a white levain at the same pH. You will not measure TTA at home; just know that pH alone underestimates the sourness of wholemeal cultures.

## What moves the balance

The yeasts and bacteria respond differently to the conditions you set. That is your control panel.

| Lever | Change | Speed to peak | Acidity | Flavour tendency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Warmer (26–30 °C) | Faster | More acid produced per hour, but a shorter cycle | More lactic, milder, yogurt-like (common practitioner guidance) |
| | Cooler (18–22 °C) | Slower | Acid accumulates over the longer cycle | More acetic, sharper (common practitioner guidance) |
| **Hydration** | Liquid (100–125 %) | Faster | Moderate | Milder, more lactic |
| | Stiff (50–60 %) | Slower | Often more pronounced tang | More acetic (common practitioner guidance) |
| **Feeding ratio** | Low inoculation (1:5:5, 1:10:10) | Slower | Starts less acidic because the old acid is diluted | Milder, more yeast-driven; often a taller rise |
| | High inoculation (1:1:1) | Faster | Starts more acidic | Sharper |
| **Flour** | Wholemeal or rye | Faster (more nutrients, enzymes, microbes) | Higher TTA | Earthier, more sour |
| | White bread flour | Slower | Lower TTA | Cleaner, milder |
| **Salt** | Adding 1–2 % to a levain | Slower | Slower acidification | Used by some bakers to slow a levain in hot weather |

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** The temperature and hydration rules for lactic versus acetic acid are practitioner guidance that is broadly consistent with the microbiology. They are not precise laws. Individual starters differ, and the net sourness of the bread also depends on the levain amount, the bulk time and the retard. Test your own culture in [X1-14](experiments/X1-14-levain.md) rather than assuming.

</div>

Rough working ranges for a vigorous 100 % hydration white-flour starter (practice varies with the culture):

| Feed ratio | At ~24–26 °C, time to peak | At ~21 °C, time to peak |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1:1 | 3–5 h | 5–7 h |
| 1:2:2 | 4–6 h | 6–8 h |
| 1:5:5 | 7–10 h | 10–14 h |
| 1:10:10 | 10–14 h | 14–18 h |

This table is how you schedule: pick the ratio that makes the levain peak when you want to mix. A 1:5:5 feed at 21 °C at 21:00 is ready at breakfast.

## Establishing a starter

You need only flour, water, warmth (24–27 °C is ideal) and 1–2 weeks. You do not need grapes, pineapple juice, honey or commercial yeast. The microbes come in with the flour and, to a lesser extent, from your hands and kitchen. Wholemeal rye or wheat flour starts fastest because it carries more microbes, enzymes and nutrients.

### What happens, and why it looks strange

| Days | What you see | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Flat paste, nothing | Flour microbes waking up; pH ~6 |
| 2–3 | **Sudden vigorous rise**, often 2× or more, foamy; may smell like cheese, vomit, old socks or sour milk | The **false rise**: bacteria that are not sourdough bacteria grow fast at near-neutral pH. They include enterobacteria and *Leuconostoc* and similar gas-producing lactic bacteria. They make gas and unpleasant volatile compounds |
| 3–6 | **Activity stops.** Little or no rise, liquid on top, sour smell. Most people give up here | Accumulating acid (pH falling towards ~4.5 and below) kills off the early bacteria. Acid-tolerant LAB are taking over but are not yet numerous, and acid-tolerant yeasts are few |
| 5–10 | Activity returns, gradually more regular: bubbles through the mass, rise after each feed | Sourdough LAB and yeasts establish. pH drops quickly after each feed |
| 7–14 | Predictable rise, doubling within 4–8 h at 24–26 °C after a 1:2:2 feed, three days in a row; pleasant yogurt, fruit or mild vinegar smell | A stable community. **Ready to bake with** |

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The early rise is not your sourdough. It is a bloom of other bacteria that the rising acidity then eliminates. The quiet phase that follows is normal. Keep feeding on schedule and keep the culture warm. Do not add yeast, and do not throw it away.

</div>

Keeping the starter warm through the quiet phase is the most useful single thing you can do. At 20 °C establishment can take 2–3 weeks; at 24–27 °C it usually takes 7–14 days.

## Maintaining a starter

Once established, you choose a maintenance regime to fit how often you bake.

| Regime | How | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Room temperature, twice daily** | 1:2:2 to 1:5:5 every 12 h at 20–24 °C | Daily bakers; the most vigorous and predictable culture |
| **Room temperature, once daily** | 1:5:5 to 1:10:10 every 24 h | Bakers every 2–3 days |
| **Refrigerator** | Feed 1:5:5, leave 1–2 h at room temperature, refrigerate at 3–5 °C. Feed once a week | Weekly or occasional bakers |
| **Stiff starter in the fridge** | 50–60 % hydration; keeps 1–2 weeks between feeds | Occasional bakers, travel |
| **Dried** | Spread thin, air-dry, crumble, store sealed | Backup. Revive over 3–5 days of feeds |

**Before baking from a refrigerated starter**, give it 2–3 room-temperature feeds (12 h apart, or two 1:2:2 feeds a day) until it reaches its normal peak time again. A cold, acidic starter used directly makes a slow, sour, dense loaf.

**Keep quantities small.** A 10–20 g seed fed with 20–50 g of flour is plenty to maintain and to build a levain from. Large starters just create waste.

**Discard** is the portion you remove before feeding. Use it in pancakes or crackers that are cooked through, or compost it.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Starter and levain are raw flour. Raw flour can carry pathogens such as *E. coli*, and acidity alone does not make it safe to eat raw ([FDA-flour]). Smell your starter freely, but do not taste raw starter or raw dough; taste the baked bread. Discard any starter showing mould (fuzzy growth, or pink, orange or black spots or streaks). Do not scrape and continue. Coloured streaks can indicate bacteria or moulds whose toxins spread beyond what you see. Wash and scald the jar, and restart from a backup or from scratch.

</div>

## Liquid vs stiff levain

| | Liquid levain (100–125 %) | Stiff levain (50–60 %) |
|---|---|---|
| Handling | Stir with a spoon | Knead briefly into a stiff ball |
| Speed | Faster | Slower; more tolerant of timing errors |
| Flavour tendency | Milder, more lactic | Tangier, more acetic |
| Effect on dough | Contributes extensibility | Contributes strength |
| Ripeness cues | Domed, bubbly, rise 2–3× in a jar | Domed ball, cracks across the top, open honeycomb inside |
| Typical use | Pain au levain, most home sourdough | French and Italian traditions, panettone (lievito madre), dough needing strength |

In a formula, a levain is treated like any other preferment: its flour and water are part of the total formula ([05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)). A stiff levain brings less water, so the final-dough water rises to compensate.

## Rescuing a neglected starter

| Condition | Rescue |
|---|---|
| Grey liquid (hooch) on top, smells of alcohol or acetone, weeks in the fridge | Pour off or stir in the hooch. Take 10 g from the middle. Feed 1:5:5 at 24–27 °C every 12 h for 2–3 days until it peaks on schedule |
| Very slow after a holiday | Same, and use 10–20 % wholemeal flour in the feeds for extra nutrients |
| Smells of acetone every time before feeding | It is starving. Feed more often or at a lower ratio, or keep it warmer and use it nearer the peak |
| Mould on the surface | Discard. Do not rescue |
| Rises but never doubles, weak and runny | Feed at a lower hydration (80–90 %) and a warmer temperature; switch to 10–20 % wholemeal. Check that the flour is not bleached |

Detailed entries are in [troubleshooting: sourdough and preferments](troubleshooting/sourdough.md).

## What goes wrong

- **Starter rose on day 2, then died.** It did not die; that was the false rise. See [starter rose strongly on day 2 then stopped](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-rose-strongly-on-day-2-then-stopped).
- **No rise after two weeks.** Usually too cold, too wet or bleached white flour only. See [starter not rising](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-not-rising).
- **Acetone smell, hooch.** Hungry starter. See [starter smells of acetone or nail polish](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-smells-of-acetone-or-nail-polish) and [hooch on the starter](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=hooch-on-the-starter).
- **Mould.** See [mould on the starter](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=mould-on-the-starter).

## Lab notebook

A starter log is a time series. For each feed: date and time, ratio and grams, flour type, water temperature, culture temperature (thermometer next to the jar), jar height at feed and at peak (straight-sided jar, rubber band at the start level), time to peak, % rise, smell descriptors, and pH at peak if you have a calibrated meter. Plot rise % against time for representative feeds. When three consecutive feeds give the same peak time within about an hour, your starter is stable and predictable.

## Check yourself

1. Your 1:2:2 starter peaks in 5 h at 25 °C. You want it to peak in the morning, 10–12 h after an evening feed, with the kitchen at 21 °C overnight. What feed do you give?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lower the inoculation and rely on the cooler temperature. Try about 1:5:5 at 21 °C (roughly 10–14 h). Measure the peak and adjust: if it peaks too early, go to 1:7:7 or use slightly cooler water; if late, go to 1:4:4.

</details>

2. Explain why a heterofermentative LAB can produce acetic acid in some conditions and ethanol in others.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Heterofermentative LAB make lactic acid, CO₂ and a two-carbon product from each glucose. Without an extra electron acceptor, they make ethanol to balance their redox chemistry. With an acceptor such as fructose (reduced to mannitol) or oxygen, they can make acetic acid instead and gain extra energy. Stiff levains and some flours tend to favour this, so they tend to be more acetic.

</details>

3. On day 3 your new starter tripled in volume and smelled like old cheese. On day 5 it barely moves. What do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing different. Day 3 was the false rise from non-sourdough bacteria; the acid they helped create has since suppressed them. Keep feeding on schedule (1:1:1 or 1:2:2 every 24 h, moving to 12 h once activity returns), keep it at 24–27 °C, and wait for regular rises, typically from day 5–10.

</details>

4. Two levains both read pH 4.0 at peak: one from white flour, one from wholemeal. Which will make a more sour-tasting bread, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The wholemeal levain. Its higher mineral and protein content buffers acid, so at the same pH it holds more total acid (higher TTA), and total acid drives perceived sourness.

</details>

5. You find a pink streak on the surface of your starter after a week in the fridge. What do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Discard the whole starter; do not scrape it off. Wash and scald the jar and restart from a dried backup or from scratch. Pink or orange colour can indicate bacteria or moulds that should not be eaten.

</details>
