## Why this lesson exists

A preferment adds a second clock to your bake. The poolish ripens on its own schedule overnight, and the final dough only turns out well if you use the poolish inside its window. This lesson teaches you to plan around that window, read ripeness from the poolish itself rather than from the clock, adjust the final dough to a poolish that is early or late, and then shape and bake the product poolish was invented for: a thin-crusted, open-crumbed bread shaped as bâtards and short baguettes that fit a home oven.

## You will be able to

1. Back-schedule a poolish bake from a target bake time, including the poolish window.
2. Classify a poolish as under-ripe, ripe or over-ripe from four observable cues, and state the correction for each.
3. Calculate water temperature for a DDT using the four-factor formula that includes the preferment.
4. Shape a 35 cm baguette with even thickness and a sealed seam, proof it on a couche, and score it with overlapping cuts.
5. Compare your poolish bread with R1-01 on crumb, crust colour, extensibility and flavour, and connect each difference to a mechanism from 05.1.

## Planning the bake

Work backwards from the time you want bread out of the oven. With R1-04 at a DDT of 24 °C:

| Step | Duration | Clock (bake finishes 13:00) |
|---|---|---|
| Mix poolish | 5 min | 19:00 the evening before |
| Poolish ferments at 20–22 °C | 12–16 h | ripe window 07:00–11:00 |
| Mix final dough, rest, develop | 35 min | 08:00–08:35 |
| Bulk with 2 folds | 1.5–2 h | 08:35–10:30 |
| Divide, pre-shape, bench rest | 35 min | 10:30–11:05 |
| Shape | 15 min | 11:05–11:20 |
| Final proof (oven preheating from 11:20) | 40–60 min | 11:20–12:15 |
| Bake (baguettes, then bâtards) | 25–40 min | 12:15–13:00 |

The poolish window is wide, 12–16 h, and that is the point: it absorbs a late start in the morning. What it will not absorb is a warm night. A poolish at 25 °C instead of 21 °C ferments roughly 1.35–1.4 times faster (using the "×2 per 8–10 °C" rule from [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md)), so a 14 h plan becomes about 10 h and you wake to an over-ripe poolish. Check the forecast and your kitchen temperature before you mix, and use the adjustments in the table below.

| Expected overnight temperature | Poolish yeast (% of poolish flour) | Expected ripe time |
|---|---|---|
| 18 °C | 0.15–0.2 % | 12–16 h |
| 20–22 °C | 0.1 % | 12–16 h |
| 24–25 °C | 0.05 % | 12–14 h |
| 21 °C, short schedule | 0.3 % | ~8 h |

These are starting points from standard professional practice. Your flour, yeast brand and container will shift them. Record your results and correct after two bakes.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Bakeries control the poolish by temperature, not by yeast. A poolish tank or retarder holds it at a set temperature, so the yeast dose never changes. At home your kitchen is the variable, so yeast is the lever you adjust, plus the choice of a cooler or warmer spot. A picnic cooler with a thermometer inside is a cheap, surprisingly stable poolish chamber.

</div>

## Reading the poolish

A poolish has a life curve. It starts as a flat, smooth batter. Yeast grows and gas production rises, so it expands and domes. Then the gluten, which is weak and was never developed, can no longer hold the gas, and sugar starts to run short. The dome sags in the middle and the surface wrinkles. Finally it collapses, thins out, and smells sharply of alcohol and acid.

| Stage | Volume and surface | Smell | Texture when tilted | Use? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Under-ripe** | Up <2×, smooth or with a few scattered bubbles, still convex | Floury, faintly yeasty | Thick, holds together, few strings | Wait 1–2 h at 24–25 °C |
| **Ripe (peak)** | Up 2–3×, domed, surface covered in bubbles, some bursting | Sweet, beer-like, wheaty | Thick ribbons, stringy, airy | Yes, best window |
| **Ripe (just past peak)** | Centre just starting to sink, fine wrinkles, faint ring on the container wall above the surface | Beer-like with a slight sharper edge | Stringy, slightly looser | Yes. Many bakers prefer this point for flavour |
| **Over-ripe** | Well below the high-water ring, flat or concave, broken bubbles, liquid on top | Sharp alcohol, sour, sometimes solvent-like | Thin, soupy, few strings | Use, but correct the final dough (below) |

The **high-water ring** on the container wall is your most reliable single sign. A poolish that has peaked leaves a faint film of batter at the highest point it reached. If you see a ring 1 cm above the surface, it has just started to recede, which is the classic ideal. If the ring is 3–4 cm above a flat surface, it collapsed some hours ago.

### Correcting for an early or late poolish

| Poolish at use | Correction in the final dough |
|---|---|
| Under-ripe, no time to wait | Increase final-dough yeast by ~25 % (3.0 g instead of 2.4 g), raise DDT to 25–26 °C, expect less flavour |
| Just past peak | No change |
| Over-ripe | Lower DDT to 22–23 °C (cooler water), watch bulk from 60 min, end bulk at the low end (+50 %), shape gently, shorten proof. Expect a slacker dough and a slightly paler crust |
| Badly over-ripe (collapsed, soupy, strong acid) | Use it at 20 % PFF instead of 40 %, replacing the missing flour and water in the final dough, or discard and make a same-day dough |

## The final dough

### DDT with a preferment

The preferment is a fourth mass at its own temperature, so it enters the DDT calculation ([01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)):

water temperature = 4 × DDT − flour − room − preferment − friction

Worked example: DDT 24 °C, flour 20 °C, room 21 °C, poolish 22 °C, friction 3 °C (hand mixing a small batch with slap-and-fold). Water = 96 − 20 − 21 − 22 − 3 = **30 °C**. Some bakers ignore the preferment when it is less than ~20 % of the dough weight. At 40 % PFF the poolish is about 46 % of the dough weight, so it matters.

### Mixing: less is more

Poolish dough starts with a head start on hydration and gluten relaxation. It needs **less** mixing than R1-01 to reach the same development, and extra mixing works against you: more mechanical development makes the dough more elastic and tighter, which undoes the extensibility the poolish provided and oxidizes some of the flour pigments, which whitens the crumb and dulls flavour. Stop at moderate development: smooth, cohesive, with a membrane that stretches thin but tears before full transparency. Two sets of folds during bulk finish the job.

### Bulk

Watch bulk from 75 min. With 0.44 % total yeast plus a ripe 40 % poolish, R1-04 often finishes bulk faster than R1-01 despite having half the commercial yeast. The poolish supplies a large, active yeast population and sugar. Use the same end-of-bulk cues as in [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md): +50–75 %, domed, bubbly sides, jiggles as one mass.

## Shaping for a home oven

A Parisian baguette is about 55–65 cm long and 250–350 g. It does not fit a domestic oven, whose interior is typically 40–45 cm wide. A 35 cm baguette of about 250 g keeps the same thickness (5–6 cm across after baking) and so the same crust-to-crumb ratio, which is what defines the product.

### Baguette shaping in four moves

1. **Pre-shape** into a loose cylinder 12–15 cm long, seam down. Rest 20–30 min until it has relaxed and widened slightly.
2. **Fold and seal.** Seam up. Fold the top third down, seal with the heel of your hand. Turn 180°, fold the top third down again and seal. Fold in half lengthways and seal the edge firmly along its whole length. The seam must be sealed or it will open in the oven.
3. **Roll out.** Seam down. Put both hands together on the centre, palms flat, fingers spread. Roll back and forth with light pressure while moving the hands outwards to the ends. Two or three passes. If the dough resists and shortens after each pass, stop, cover, and let it relax for 5 min rather than forcing it. Tearing means too little bench rest, too much mixing, or too little extensibility.
4. **Taper and check.** Slight tapers at the ends. Target 35 cm and even thickness along the length. Uneven thickness bakes unevenly and splits at the thin spots.

### The couche

The couche is a heavy linen cloth. Its job is to support the sides of a long, soft loaf so it proofs upwards instead of spreading. Flour it lightly, place each baguette seam-up, and pinch a pleat of cloth up on each side about 2–3 cm high. Linen releases better than cotton because its fibres hold flour and absorb surface moisture. Never wash a couche with detergent: scrape it, dry it fully, and store it rolled.

### Transfer and score

Use a flipping board to roll the loaf off the couche seam-down onto parchment. Your hands will deform a soft baguette.

**Baguette scoring:** 3 cuts on a 35 cm loaf, each 10–12 cm long, running almost parallel to the loaf's long axis, near the centreline, each overlapping the previous by about one third. The blade is held at about 30° to the surface and cuts 5–6 mm deep. A shallow angle creates a flap that lifts and hardens into an ear. A vertical cut simply opens into a wide, flat scar. Cuts across the loaf (like a sausage) give a very different, more rustic effect and less spring.

## Baking with steam in a home oven

Recall from [04.1](stage-1/module-04/lesson-01.md) and [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md): steam in the first 10–15 min condenses on the cold dough surface, transfers heat quickly, and keeps the skin soft and extensible. The loaf springs and the scores open before the crust sets. Condensed water on the surface also gelatinizes surface starch, which gives the shine and the thin, crackly crust. After 12–15 min the steam must go, so the crust can dry and brown.

Baguettes are thin: heat reaches the centre fast and the product is mostly crust. Bake them hot and short, on a stone or steel that pushes heat into the base: 250 °C (480 °F) with steam for 12 min, then vented at 240 °C (465 °F) for 10–14 min. Bâtards need longer at a slightly lower temperature so the centre reaches 96 °C before the crust over-darkens.

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** Opening the oven to add steam and load bread loses a lot of heat. Load quickly, pour the water, and close the door within about 10 s. If you bake baguettes and bâtards in succession, let the oven and stone recover to 250 °C for 10–15 min between batches. Measure it: an oven thermometer on the stone shelf is the only way to know ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)).

</div>

## Evaluating against R1-01

Cut a bâtard from R1-04 alongside an R1-01 loaf baked on the same day or the day before. The table shows what to expect and why.

| Attribute | R1-01 straight dough | R1-04 poolish | Why ([05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dough feel | Elastic, springs back | Extensible, silky, slightly sticky | Relaxed gluten from the long-hydrated poolish flour and proteases |
| Crumb | Moderately open, fairly regular | More open and irregular, glossy walls | Extensible dough allows cells to expand further |
| Crumb colour | White to cream | Creamier | Shorter mixing: less oxidation of flour pigments |
| Crust | Golden-brown | Deeper, often reddish-brown, thinner | More residual sugars from amylase activity; thin crust from steam and a short bake |
| Flavour | Clean, simple wheat, faintly yeasty | Fuller, sweeter, nutty, lingering | Fermentation by-products from 12–16 h, lower yeast in the final dough |
| Keeping | Stale by day 2 | Slightly better on day 2 | Acids and fermentation products, slightly more moisture retention |

Differences in keeping quality are modest with poolish and much greater with sourdough. Test them in [X1-13](experiments/X1-13-preferments.md).

## What goes wrong

- **Poolish over-ripe on waking.** The night was warmer than planned. See [poolish or biga over-ripe](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=poolish-or-biga-over-ripe).
- **Baguettes tear or shrink back while rolling.** Pre-shape too tight, bench rest too short, or the final dough was overmixed. Rest 5–10 min and continue.
- **Baguettes flat with scores that don't open.** Over-proofed, too little steam, or scores too vertical or too deep. See [poor oven spring](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=poor-oven-spring) and [loaf spreads sideways](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=loaf-spreads-sideways).
- **Pale, dull crust.** Too little oven heat, steam left in too long, or an over-ripe poolish. See [pale crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=pale-crust).

## Check yourself

1. You mix a poolish at 19:00 for use at 09:00. The forecast says your kitchen will be 25 °C overnight. What do you change?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Reduce the poolish yeast to ~0.05 % of poolish flour (12 g of a 1 % solution for 240 g flour), or keep 0.1 % and put the poolish somewhere cooler (a cooler with a cold pack, aiming at 20–22 °C). At 0.1 % and 25 °C it would likely be over-ripe well before 09:00.

</details>

2. At 08:00 your poolish has a ring 4 cm above a flat, bubbly surface with liquid on top and a sharp alcoholic smell. What do you do with the final dough?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It is over-ripe. Use cooler water for a DDT of 22–23 °C, check bulk from 60 min and stop at +50 %, handle gently, shorten the proof, and expect a slacker dough and paler crust. If it is badly degraded, use only half of it and make up the difference with fresh flour and water in the final dough.

</details>

3. Flour 19 °C, room 20 °C, poolish 21 °C, friction 4 °C, DDT 24 °C. What water temperature do you need?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

4 × 24 = 96. 96 − 19 − 20 − 21 − 4 = 32 °C.

</details>

4. Why is a 35 cm, 250 g baguette a fair substitute for a 60 cm one, while a 35 cm, 400 g loaf is not?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The baguette is defined by its thickness and crust-to-crumb ratio, not its length. A 250 g piece at 35 cm has about the same weight per unit length as a 60 cm, ~350–400 g baguette, so it bakes and eats the same way. A 400 g piece at 35 cm is much thicker: a different crust-to-crumb ratio and a longer bake, essentially a small bâtard.

</details>

5. Your baguette scores opened as flat, wide scars with no ear. Name two likely technique causes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The blade was held too vertically (it should be ~30° to the surface), or there was too little steam, so the skin set before the flap could lift. Over-proofing and cutting too deep also flatten scores.

</details>
