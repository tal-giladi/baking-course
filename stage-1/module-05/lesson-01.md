## Why this lesson exists

In [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md) you saw that flavour and dough maturity build with *time*, not with gas. A straight dough can only buy time by slowing everything down, and that stretches your whole day. A preferment separates the two. You ferment part of the flour for many hours in advance, on its own schedule, and then mix a final dough that needs only a normal bulk. This lesson explains what a preferment changes in the dough, gives you the specifications of the four classic types, and teaches you to write a formula that contains one. That last skill is where most home bakers and many recipe books go wrong.

## You will be able to

1. List five things a preferment does to bread, and link each to a mechanism (acids and aroma, enzymes, gluten, keeping quality, schedule).
2. Give the typical hydration, yeast level, time and temperature for poolish, biga, sponge and pâte fermentée, and choose one for a given product.
3. Explain what alpha- and beta-amylase and proteases do during a long preferment, and connect them to crust colour and dough extensibility.
4. Write a formula in three columns (total formula, preferment, final dough) and check that it adds up.
5. Convert a straight-dough formula into a preferment formula while keeping the overall formula identical.

## What a preferment is

A **preferment** is a portion of the dough's flour, water and a leavening culture (commercial yeast, or wild yeast and bacteria for sourdough) that is mixed and fermented *before* the final dough. When it is ripe, it is mixed into the final dough with the remaining ingredients.

The key quantity is the **prefermented flour (PFF)**: the percentage of the *total* flour in the recipe that is in the preferment. A poolish bread with 30 % PFF has 30 % of its flour fermenting overnight and 70 % added fresh in the morning.

## What a preferment does

| Effect | What you see and taste | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Flavour** | Fuller, wheatier, slightly sweet or nutty taste; a faint tang with stiff or long preferments | Many hours of fermentation produce organic acids (lactic, acetic), alcohols, esters and aldehydes. Even with only baker's yeast, some lactic acid bacteria that come in with the flour grow slowly in a long preferment |
| **Enzyme activity** | Deeper crust colour, sweeter crumb | Flour amylases work for 12–16 h on hydrated starch and build up maltose and other sugars ahead of the final dough |
| **Extensibility** | Dough stretches further without tearing; baguettes shape more easily | Flour proteases and the long hydration relax gluten in the preferment portion. A liquid poolish has no mechanical development, so its gluten is soft and loose |
| **Strength** (stiff types) | Biga and pâte fermentée can make a dough stronger | Organic acids at moderate levels tighten gluten. The stiff preferment has developed some structure of its own |
| **Keeping quality** | Crumb stays moist and fresh a little longer; slower mould (strongest with sourdough) | Acids lower pH; more fermentation products and partially broken-down starch slow staling and microbial growth |
| **Schedule** | Mix in the evening in 5 min, bake the next morning in 4–5 h | The slow step happens overnight while you sleep; the final dough runs a normal 1.5–2.5 h bulk |
| **Less yeast in the final dough** | A lower total yeast % achieves a normal bulk time | The preferment adds a large, active yeast population and some sugar; the final dough needs less commercial yeast |

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** A preferment is a way to get the flavour and dough maturity of a long, cool fermentation into a bread that still has a short, controllable final bulk. You pay for it in planning, not in labour.

</div>

## The four classic preferments

| Type | Hydration (of preferment flour) | Yeast (instant, % of preferment flour) | Time and temperature | Salt | Typical PFF | Typical products |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Poolish** | 100 % (a thick batter) | ~0.1 % for 12–16 h. ~0.3 % for ~8 h | 12–16 h at ~20–22 °C | None | 20–40 % (up to 50 %) | Baguette, ciabatta, fougasse, lean rolls |
| **Biga** | 50–60 % (a stiff, shaggy dough) | ~0.2–0.5 % (0.5–1.5 % fresh) | 12–18 h at ~16–20 °C (cooler than poolish) | None | 20–50 % (some Italian breads use far more) | Ciabatta, pane pugliese, rustic Italian breads |
| **Sponge** | 55–100 %, depending on the product | Often all the recipe's yeast: 0.5–2 % of total flour | 30 min–4 h at ~24–27 °C (short, warm) | Usually none | 30–70 % | Pan breads, sandwich loaves, enriched doughs, industrial sponge-and-dough |
| **Pâte fermentée** ("old dough") | Same as the final dough, usually 65–70 % | Same as a straight dough, ~0.5–1 % | 1 h at room temperature, then 8–48 h at 3–5 °C; or a piece saved from yesterday's batch | **Yes, ~2 %** | 15–50 % | Baguette, rolls, any lean bread |
| Levain (sourdough) | 50–125 % | None: a wild culture | 4–16 h, 20–28 °C | None | 10–30 % | Sourdough breads. See [05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md) |

The numbers are standard professional practice (see [Hamelman], [Suas], [Calvel]). Practice varies widely, so read these as the centre of the range and learn what moves you within it.

### Poolish

Poolish is the liquid preferment, associated with Viennese and Parisian bread. It is equal weights of flour and water and a tiny amount of yeast. At 0.1 % of 200 g flour you need 0.2 g of instant yeast, which is below the resolution of most kitchen scales. The engineering fix is a **yeast solution**: dissolve 1.0 g instant yeast in 99 g water (a 1 % solution), stir, and use 20 g of it as part of the poolish water. That 20 g contains 0.2 g yeast. Throw the rest away; do not keep it.

What moves you in the range:

- **Warmer kitchen** (24–25 °C): halve the yeast (~0.05 %) or shorten to 8–10 h.
- **Shorter time available**: raise the yeast. ~0.3 % gives about 8 h at 21 °C; ~0.8–1 % gives a 3–4 h "quick poolish" with less flavour.
- **More PFF** (40–50 %): more extensibility and flavour, but the final dough gets slacker and ferments faster. Reduce final-dough yeast.

### Biga

Biga is the Italian term for a preferment, and in current use it usually means a **stiff** one at 50–60 % hydration. Low water slows everything, and a stiff dough holds more oxygen at its surface and keeps a cooler, slower culture. The result is more acidity, a more "winey" or wheaty flavour, and a final dough that is stronger than a poolish dough. Stiff biga is mixed only until the flour is hydrated. It looks shaggy and dry, and that is correct.

Practitioners generally say that cool, stiff preferments develop more acetic character and liquid, warmer ones more lactic and mild character. The same pattern appears in sourdough ([05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md)).

### Sponge

A sponge is a short, warm preferment that usually carries all of the recipe's yeast. Its main job is to get the yeast fully active and the gluten partly developed before sugar, fat and eggs are added. Those ingredients slow yeast ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)). Flavour gain is modest because the time is short. In industrial pan bread, the "sponge-and-dough" process ferments 60–70 % of the flour for 3–5 h. At home you will meet the sponge in enriched breads (M06).

### Pâte fermentée

Pâte fermentée is literally fermented dough: a piece of finished lean dough, with salt, yeast and all, kept to be added to the next batch. Because it contains salt, it ferments more slowly than a poolish at the same yeast level and can be kept longer in the fridge. It adds flavour and some strength, and it is the easiest preferment to fit into a bakery routine: you keep back 15–20 % of today's dough for tomorrow. At home you mix a small batch the night before, give it 1 h at room temperature, and refrigerate it.

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** Remember to count the salt in pâte fermentée. If 30 % of your flour comes in as old dough with 2 % salt, then 30 % of your salt is already in it. Adding the full 2 % again to the final dough gives 2.6 % overall salt: noticeably salty and slower to ferment.

</div>

## Enzymes: what works while the preferment sits

Flour is not inert. Sound wheat flour carries its own enzymes, and millers often add a small amount of malted flour or fungal amylase to standardize activity ([02.1](stage-1/module-02/lesson-01.md)). In a straight dough these enzymes have perhaps 3–4 h before the oven stops them. In a preferment they get another 12–16 h on part of the flour. Read [SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md) for the underlying chemistry; here is what you need on the bench.

### Amylases: starch to sugar

| Enzyme | How it cuts starch | What it needs | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha-amylase** | Cuts inside starch chains at random (an "endo" enzyme) | Access to starch. Intact granules resist it, and damaged starch from milling is attacked readily | Shorter chains (dextrins), which create many new chain ends |
| **Beta-amylase** | Cuts two glucose units (maltose) off the ends of chains (an "exo" enzyme) | Chain ends, which alpha-amylase supplies | **Maltose** |

The two work as a team. Sound wheat flour has plenty of beta-amylase but little alpha-amylase, so alpha-amylase is usually the rate-limiting step. That is why millers add malt. Yeast takes up maltose and splits it into glucose inside the cell with the enzyme maltase. In a long preferment, amylases keep producing maltose while yeast and bacteria consume sugar. With the right yeast level, a ripe poolish contributes sugars and dextrins to the final dough. Those feed the final fermentation and are left over for **crust colour** through Maillard reactions ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)).

The balance can tip the other way. An over-ripe preferment, or a preferment with too much yeast, runs out of sugar faster than the amylases make it. The yeast population then starves, acids and alcohol build up, and the bread bakes paler. Sourdough runs close to this limit, which is why a long-fermented sourdough can bake pale ([05.4](stage-1/module-05/lesson-04.md)).

### Proteases: softening gluten

Proteases cut proteins, including the gluten proteins. Wheat flour has low protease activity, but in a wet preferment it has hours to act. Together with the simple fact that the poolish flour is fully hydrated and never kneaded, this means the gluten in the preferment portion is relaxed and extensible. When you mix it into the final dough, it makes the whole dough more **extensible** (it stretches further and snaps back less), which is exactly what you need to roll a baguette to length without tearing it.

Too much of this is a fault. An over-ripe poolish, a very high PFF, or a preferment kept too warm contributes so much softened gluten and acid that the final dough is slack and sticky and will not hold a shape. This is the preferment version of over-fermentation.

### The autolyse connection

The **autolyse**, introduced by Raymond Calvel ([Calvel]), is a rest of flour and water, without salt and traditionally without yeast, for 20–30 min before mixing. It lets the flour hydrate fully, lets gluten form without mechanical work, and gives proteases a short head start. The dough mixes in less time and is more extensible. You used a short version in R1-01. A poolish is in a sense a very long autolyse on part of the flour, with fermentation added. That is why poolish doughs need less mixing and feel silky, and why you should be careful about stacking a long autolyse on top of a high-PFF poolish: extensibility gains add up, and the dough can end up weak.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Acidity pulls in two directions. Moderate acidity, as in a stiff biga or a young levain, tends to tighten gluten, which bakers experience as a stronger, more elastic dough. Strong or prolonged acidity, especially combined with protease activity over many hours, weakens the network. On the bench, a stiff preferment strengthens and an over-ripe liquid one slackens. See [SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md) and [SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md).

</div>

## Writing a formula that contains a preferment

This is the most important practical skill in the lesson. The rule that makes it work:

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The **total formula** (also called the overall formula) describes the finished dough as if everything were mixed at once. All baker's percentages in it are relative to the **total flour**, including the flour in the preferment. The preferment and final dough are just a way of splitting that total into two mixes.

</div>

Bakers following Hamelman's and SFBI's presentation write three columns: total formula, preferment, final dough. The preferment column gives its own baker's percentages relative to the *preferment's* flour. The final dough column lists what you add on the day, with the preferment as one ingredient.

### Worked example: 30 % of the flour as poolish

Target: a lean dough with 1000 g total flour, 70 % hydration, 2 % salt, 0.5 % total instant yeast, and 30 % PFF as a poolish at 100 % hydration and 0.1 % yeast.

Step 1. Total formula: flour 1000, water 700, salt 20, yeast 5.0 g.
Step 2. Preferment flour = 30 % × 1000 = 300 g. Poolish water = 100 % × 300 = 300 g. Poolish yeast = 0.1 % × 300 = 0.3 g.
Step 3. Final dough = total − preferment for each ingredient: flour 700, water 400, salt 20, yeast 4.7 g. Add all of the poolish, 600.3 g.
Step 4. Check: 700 + 400 + 20 + 4.7 + 600.3 = 1725.0 g, which equals the total formula weight.

| Ingredient | Total formula % | Total formula g | Poolish % (of poolish flour) | Poolish g | Final dough g |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100.0 | 1000.0 | 100.0 | 300.0 | 700.0 |
| Water | 70.0 | 700.0 | 100.0 | 300.0 | 400.0 |
| Salt | 2.0 | 20.0 | — | — | 20.0 |
| Instant yeast | 0.5 | 5.0 | 0.1 | 0.3 | 4.7 |
| Poolish | — | — | — | — | 600.3 |
| **Total** | **172.5** | **1725.0** | **200.1** | **600.3** | **1725.0** |

Two things confuse people here.

1. **The final dough on its own looks too dry.** 400 g water on 700 g flour is 57 %. That is correct. The poolish brings 300 g of water, and the dough will be 70 % hydration once it is mixed in. Never "correct" the final-dough water by eye before the poolish is in.
2. **Preferment as a percentage depends on the base.** The poolish is 600.3 g, which is 60 % of *total* flour but 85.8 % of *final-dough* flour (600.3 / 700). Some books express preferments on final-dough flour. Before scaling a formula, check which base it uses. PFF (here 30 %) is the least ambiguous number, so write it on every formula sheet.

### The same overall formula as a biga

Swap the poolish for a biga at 60 % hydration and 0.2 % yeast, still 30 % PFF:

| Ingredient | Total formula % | Total formula g | Biga % | Biga g | Final dough g |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100.0 | 1000.0 | 100.0 | 300.0 | 700.0 |
| Water | 70.0 | 700.0 | 60.0 | 180.0 | 520.0 |
| Salt | 2.0 | 20.0 | — | — | 20.0 |
| Instant yeast | 0.5 | 5.0 | 0.2 | 0.6 | 4.4 |
| Biga | — | — | — | — | 480.6 |
| **Total** | **172.5** | **1725.0** | **160.2** | **480.6** | **1725.0** |

The overall dough is identical: same flour, water, salt and yeast. What changed is *where the water was during the night*. That is exactly the variable you will isolate in [X1-13](experiments/X1-13-preferments.md).

### Converting a straight dough into a preferment dough

1. Write the straight dough as a total formula in baker's %.
2. Choose the preferment type and PFF (start at 20–30 %).
3. Compute preferment flour, then preferment water and yeast from the preferment's own percentages.
4. Subtract preferment ingredients from the total formula to get the final dough.
5. **Reduce total yeast.** The ripe preferment is a large, active inoculum. As a starting point, a poolish dough at 30–40 % PFF uses about 40–50 % less total yeast than the same-day straight dough (R1-01 uses 0.8 %; R1-04 uses 0.44 %). Adjust after one bake.
6. If the preferment contains salt (pâte fermentée), subtract it too.
7. Check that the final-dough column plus the preferment equals the total formula.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Bakeries keep the total formula as the master document and generate preferment and final-dough weights from it for each batch size. The total formula lets you compare two breads directly ("both are 70 % hydration, 2 % salt"); the split is just production instructions. Build your formula sheet ([01.4](stage-1/module-01/lesson-04.md)) with the three columns and a check-sum row.

</div>

## Choosing a preferment

| If you want… | Choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| An extensible dough, open crumb, sweet wheat flavour, deep crust | Poolish, 30–40 % PFF | Liquid, enzyme-rich, relaxes gluten |
| A stronger dough, a more complex, slightly tangy flavour | Biga, 20–50 % PFF | Stiff, cool, more acetic, tightens gluten |
| Reliable fermentation in a sugar- and fat-rich dough | Sponge | Gets yeast going before enrichment slows it |
| Flavour with minimal planning, in a bakery with daily batches | Pâte fermentée | Keeps a piece of today's dough for tomorrow |
| Acidity, keeping quality, no commercial yeast | Levain | Wild yeast and bacteria ([05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md)) |

## What goes wrong

- **Preferment over-ripe in the morning** (collapsed, liquid, sharp alcohol smell). The kitchen was warmer than planned or you added too much yeast. See [poolish or biga over-ripe](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=poolish-or-biga-over-ripe).
- **Final dough far too dry or too wet at mixing.** Almost always an arithmetic error: final water computed on final flour instead of by subtraction, or preferment water counted twice. Recheck the three columns.
- **Dough too salty and slow.** Salt from pâte fermentée was counted twice.
- **Dough slack and sticky despite correct hydration.** Too high a PFF, an over-ripe poolish, or a long autolyse on top of a poolish. See [weak, slack dough](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=weak-slack-dough).

## Check yourself

1. A formula lists "poolish 50 %" and the author gives 1000 g flour in the final dough. Why can't you tell how much prefermented flour there is without more information?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

"50 %" may be on total flour or on final-dough flour, and it may be the poolish *weight* or the poolish *flour*. If it means poolish weight = 50 % of final-dough flour, the poolish is 500 g (250 g flour) and total flour is 1250 g, so PFF = 20 %. If it means poolish flour = 50 % of total flour, PFF is 50 %. Ask for PFF or the total formula.

</details>

2. Convert this straight dough to a 25 % PFF poolish (100 % hydration, 0.1 % yeast), keeping total flour 800 g, water 68 %, salt 2 %, total instant yeast 0.4 %. Give the poolish and final dough in grams.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total: flour 800, water 544, salt 16, yeast 3.2 (total 1363.2 g). Poolish: flour 200, water 200, yeast 0.2 g (= 20 g of a 1 % solution); poolish weight 400.2 g. Final dough: flour 600, water 344, salt 16, yeast 3.0, poolish 400.2. Check: 600 + 344 + 16 + 3.0 + 400.2 = 1363.2 g.

</details>

3. Your poolish is flat, has few bubbles, and smells of plain flour after 12 h. What is the likely cause, and what enzyme-related benefit have you lost?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Too little active yeast (weighing error, a dead yeast solution) or a cold kitchen. The amylases still worked, but little fermentation occurred: no acid and aroma build-up and little yeast population to hand on. Give it more time somewhere warmer (24–25 °C) until it is domed and bubbly, or use it and increase final-dough yeast.

</details>

4. Why does a baguette made with 40 % poolish stretch to length more easily than one from a straight dough at the same hydration?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

40 % of the flour spent 12–16 h fully hydrated without mechanical development, while proteases relaxed its gluten. That relaxed gluten makes the whole dough more extensible. Mild acidity and the shorter mixing needed also help.

</details>

5. You make pâte fermentée at 30 % PFF from a dough with 2 % salt. How much salt goes in the final dough, as % of total flour?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1.4 %. The old dough brings 2 % × 30 % = 0.6 % of total flour as salt; 2.0 − 0.6 = 1.4 %.

</details>
