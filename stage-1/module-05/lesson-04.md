## Why this lesson exists

The sourdough loaf is where everything in M02–M05 meets: flour and hydration, fermentation control, shaping, the oven, and now a living culture that you maintain yourself. It is also the bread where diagnosis matters most, because it runs slowly and with many interacting variables, so a failure has many plausible causes. The first half of this lesson runs the loaf. The second half, the bread clinic, trains the skill this course is really about: going from a symptom to a cause using evidence rather than guesswork.

## You will be able to

1. Run R1-06 over two days, with the levain peaking at mixing time and bulk judged by dough cues rather than the clock.
2. Explain why sourdough bulk is slower and harder to read than yeasted bulk, and name the cues that decide it.
3. Adjust the sourness of a loaf in a chosen direction with at least four levers.
4. Explain why sourdough bakes paler than a yeasted loaf and how to compensate.
5. Diagnose a failed loaf from its symptoms and bake record using the troubleshooting database, name the most probable cause, and propose a test that would confirm it.

## How sourdough bulk differs from yeasted bulk

| | Yeasted lean dough (R1-01) | Sourdough (R1-06) |
|---|---|---|
| Leavening organism | Baker's yeast, large dose | Wild yeasts, smaller population, slower gas production |
| Bulk at 24–26 °C | 1.5–2.5 h | 4–6 h at 15 % PFF |
| What else changes during bulk | Mostly gas and gluten | Gas, gluten **and pH**: acid builds and changes dough strength |
| Temperature sensitivity | High | Higher. The ecosystem's balance shifts too, not only its speed |
| Most useful cue | Volume in a marked container | Volume **plus** dome, convex edges, bubbles and jiggle; smell |
| Typical failure | Over-fermentation from a warm day | **Under-fermentation**, from a weak levain or cool dough |

Two practical conclusions follow.

First, **control temperature**. A sourdough at 22 °C instead of 26 °C takes roughly 1.35–1.4 times longer by the rule from [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md). A 5 h bulk becomes about 7 h and is easy to cut short by the clock. That is why R1-06 sets a DDT of 25–26 °C and asks you to find a warm spot.

Second, **read the dough**. Sourdough often looks less dramatic than yeasted dough at the right moment. It rises more slowly, and some of its structure comes from acid-tightened gluten. The combined cues listed in R1-06 step 7 matter more than any single one. In most first sourdough loaves the problem is ending bulk too early, not too late.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** For a first sourdough, a dense crumb with a few big holes near the top almost always means under-fermentation: a weak levain, cool dough, or a bulk ended by the clock. Fix the starter and the temperature before changing anything else.

</div>

## The schedule

| Day | Time | Step |
|---|---|---|
| −1 | 08:00 and 20:00 | Refresh the starter from the fridge, 1:2:2 at 24–26 °C |
| 1 | 08:00 | Build the levain, 20 + 65 + 65 g at 25–26 °C |
| 1 | 12:45 | Autolyse the final flour and water |
| 1 | 13:30 | Add the levain (peaked). Salt at 13:45 |
| 1 | 14:15–15:45 | Four sets of folds at 30 min intervals |
| 1 | ~18:30–19:30 | End of bulk. Pre-shape, rest, shape |
| 1 | ~20:00 | Into the fridge at 3–5 °C |
| 2 | 07:00–11:00 | Preheat 45 min; bake from cold; cool 2 h |

The retard is the flexible element: anything from 8 to 16 h works in a fridge that really is at 3–5 °C. The fixed element is the levain peaking when you want to mix. If your starter is slower, move the levain build earlier or build it overnight at 1:5:5 ([R1-06 variables](recipes/R1-06-sourdough-loaf.md)).

## Controlling sourness

The acid in the finished loaf depends on how much acid-producing culture you add, how long it works, and at what temperature and hydration. Practitioners use these levers:

| To make it more sour | To make it milder | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| More levain (higher PFF, e.g. 20–25 %) | Less levain (10 %) | More bacteria and more acid carried in |
| Levain used past peak | Levain used at or just before peak | Acid keeps building after peak |
| Stiff levain (50–60 %) | Liquid levain (100–125 %) | Stiff cultures tend to more acetic character (practitioner guidance) |
| Cooler bulk (22–24 °C) with longer time | Warmer bulk (26–27 °C), shorter | Cooler conditions tend to favour acetic character (practitioner guidance), and time allows acid accumulation |
| Longer retard (16–24 h) | Shorter retard (8 h) or none | Acid production continues in the cold while gas production slows |
| More wholemeal or rye | White flour | Higher buffering, higher total acidity, more bacterial food |
| Starter kept in the fridge and used after one feed | Starter refreshed 2–3 times at room temperature | A cold, old culture carries more accumulated acid |

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** "More sour" is easy; "more sour and still light" is hard. Extra acidity weakens the dough over long times, so the sourer levers also push towards slack dough and flatter loaves. Change one lever at a time and record the result, or you will not know what did it.

</div>

## Why sourdough bakes paler

In [05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md) you saw that fermentation and amylase activity race each other for sugar. In a sourdough, fermentation runs for 12–24 h in total, and by the time the loaf goes into the oven much of the free sugar has been consumed. The Maillard reaction also runs more slowly at low pH ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md), [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)). Both push towards a paler crust. That is why R1-06 says to bake to colour, and why an **over-fermented** sourdough, in bulk or in a warm retard, bakes noticeably paler. A pale sourdough crust after a full bake is diagnostic evidence of over-fermentation.

A retarded loaf often shows a *deeper* colour and blisters, though. The cold slows yeast sugar consumption while amylases keep releasing some sugar, and surface moisture during the retard contributes to blistering. The two effects coexist; which one wins depends on how far fermentation went.

## Evaluating your loaf

Cut the loaf after at least 2 h. Photograph the crumb with a ruler, the crust and the profile. Score it against the R1-06 expected result:

| Attribute | Target | If off, check |
|---|---|---|
| Height and profile | 9–11 cm tall (boule), rounded, strong ear | Proof level, shaping tension, fermentation |
| Crumb distribution | Open and even from crust to base; no dense band; no single tunnel | Bulk endpoint, shaping, proof |
| Crumb texture | Glossy, moist, springy; not gummy | Bake to ≥96 °C, cooling ≥2 h |
| Crust colour | Deep reddish-brown with a dark ear | Over-fermentation, oven temperature, bake time |
| Acidity | Balanced; wheat first, tang second | Sourness levers above |
| Keeping | Good on day 3 | Should be clearly better than R1-01 |

## The bread clinic

The clinic is a structured diagnosis exercise. You get six failed loaves from M03–M05, each described by symptoms and the baker's notes. There are no photographs; you work from descriptions, as you would from a colleague's notes. For each case:

1. **List the symptoms** precisely: what is wrong, where in the loaf, and how much.
2. **Generate hypotheses.** Open the relevant troubleshooting page and list every likely cause that fits the symptoms: [lean bread: dough and fermentation](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md), [bread: baking, crust and staling](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md), [sourdough and preferments](troubleshooting/sourdough.md).
3. **Use the notes as evidence.** Eliminate causes the bake record contradicts. Promote causes it supports. The most useful clues are temperatures, times and volume changes.
4. **Name the most probable cause** and a second candidate.
5. **Propose a test**: a change to one variable in the next bake, or a comparison, that would distinguish your top two hypotheses.
6. **Write the correction.**

Write your answers in your lab notebook before opening the answers. Time yourself: about 10 min per case.

### Case 1 — The brick

R1-06. The starter came out of the fridge the evening before and was fed once. The levain rose about 40 % in 6 h and was used then. Kitchen 21 °C; dough temperature after mixing 22 °C. Bulk 5 h with four folds; the dough had risen about 20 % and still had flat, sharp edges, but it was getting late. Shaped, retarded 12 h. The loaf is 6 cm tall and tight-crumbed, with a few large holes just under the top crust and a dense, damp layer at the base. It tastes sharply sour.

<details class="answer"><summary>Diagnosis</summary>

**Under-fermented dough from a weak levain and cool bulk.** Evidence: the levain rose only 40 % (it should at least double), the starter had only one feed after refrigeration, the dough was at 22 °C instead of 25–26 °C, and bulk ended at +20 % with flat edges. The large holes under the crust are typical of under-fermented dough (gas collects in a few places in a tight network). The sharp sourness comes from the cold, acid-laden starter. Second candidate: under-baking contributing to the damp base, but the bake record does not suggest it. Test: refresh the starter 2–3 times until it doubles in 4–8 h at 1:2:2, hold the dough at 25–26 °C, and end bulk on cues (+50–75 %). See [dense or gummy sourdough crumb](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=dense-or-gummy-sourdough-crumb), [starter not rising](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-not-rising) and [sourdough bulk far slower than expected](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=sourdough-bulk-far-slower-than-expected).

</details>

### Case 2 — The sticky slice

R1-01, retarded proof, baked in a pot: 20 min covered, 15 min uncovered at 230 °C. The crust is medium golden. The internal temperature was not measured. The baker cut it after 20 min because guests arrived. The crumb is open and well risen, but it compresses into a sticky paste between the fingers and there is a shiny, wet-looking streak just above the base.

<details class="answer"><summary>Diagnosis</summary>

**Under-baked and cut hot.** Evidence: a total bake of 35 min is short for an 860 g loaf (R1-01 specifies 38–45 min), the crust is only medium golden, internal temperature was not checked, and the loaf was cut after 20 min, while starch was still setting and moisture redistributing. Fermentation was fine: the loaf is well risen with an open crumb. Second candidate: a cold pot or a low oven thermostat. Test: bake to ≥96 °C internal, confirmed by probe, and cool ≥1 h; check the oven with a thermometer ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)). See [gummy crumb](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=gummy-crumb) and [raw centre with dark crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=raw-centre-with-dark-crust).

</details>

### Case 3 — The pancake

R1-06 at 78 % hydration. Summer, kitchen 28 °C. Dough temperature 27 °C. Bulk 7 h "to be safe"; the dough rose about 120 % and had many large bubbles on top. At pre-shape it was sticky and spread into a puddle. Shaped anyway, retarded 16 h in a fridge that a thermometer later showed was at 7 °C. When it was turned out, the loaf slumped to 26 cm across. It baked to 6 cm tall, the score barely opened, and the crust was pale in patches. It tastes sharply sour.

<details class="answer"><summary>Diagnosis</summary>

**Over-fermentation in bulk, compounded by over-proofing in a warm retard.** Evidence: 7 h at 27–28 °C is far beyond the 4–6 h at 25–26 °C in R1-06; +120 % with large surface bubbles; a sticky puddle at pre-shape (acid and proteolysis have weakened the gluten); a 7 °C fridge ferments roughly twice as fast as 3–4 °C over 16 h; the pale crust shows sugar depletion. The higher hydration (78 %) made it worse. Second candidate: insufficient shaping tension, but a well-fermented dough would have held its shape at pre-shape. Test: at the same temperature, end bulk at +50 % (probably ~3.5–4.5 h at 27 °C), use 74 % hydration, and put the loaf on the coldest fridge shelf measured at 3–5 °C. See [flat spreading sourdough loaf](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=flat-spreading-sourdough-loaf), [loaf spreads sideways](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=loaf-spreads-sideways) and [pale crust on sourdough](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=pale-crust-on-sourdough).

</details>

### Case 4 — The vinegar loaf

R1-06 with 25 % wholemeal. The baker keeps a stiff starter (55 % hydration) in the fridge and last fed it two weeks ago. It was fed once and used as the levain after 10 h at 20 °C, when it had risen well. Bulk 8 h at 21 °C, retard 20 h. The loaf looks good: tall, open, well coloured. But it tastes and smells strongly of vinegar, and several tasters found it unpleasant.

<details class="answer"><summary>Diagnosis</summary>

**Excess acidity, especially acetic acid, from several levers stacked together.** Evidence: a stiff, cold, old starter used after only one feed (high carried-over acid, and stiff cultures tend towards acetic); 25 % wholemeal (more buffering and more total acid); a cool 21 °C bulk for 8 h and a 20 h retard (time and cool conditions favour acid, especially acetic). Structure is fine, so fermentation was not excessive in the leavening sense. Test: change one lever at a time. First, refresh the starter 2–3 times at 24–26 °C as a liquid culture and use the levain at peak; keep everything else the same. See [loaf too sour](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=loaf-too-sour).

</details>

### Case 5 — The cave

R1-04 bâtards. The poolish was ripe. Bulk was stopped at 60 min at about +30 % because of a school run. The baker floured the bench heavily for shaping, and the pre-shape and shape were loose. Proof 30 min. Each loaf has a huge irregular hole running along under the top crust (the crust has separated from the crumb), a few other large holes, and a tight, dense crumb elsewhere. The scores opened unevenly and one side burst.

<details class="answer"><summary>Diagnosis</summary>

**Under-fermented and under-proofed, with flour trapped in the shaping.** Evidence: bulk stopped at +30 % (target +50–75 %), a 30 min proof (target 40–60 min), and dense crumb between the big holes. In an under-fermented dough, gas concentrates in a few large pockets, and a strong burst of oven spring against an under-proofed skin tears the crust away from the crumb ("flying crust") and bursts the side. Heavy bench flour folded into the loaf creates layers that do not seal, and gas collects along them. Second candidate: shaping without degassing large bubbles; less likely to explain the dense crumb. Test: same process, bulk to +60 %, full proof by the poke test, and flour only the top surface before flipping. See [large tunnel holes or flying crust](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=large-tunnel-holes-or-flying-crust), [dense, tight crumb](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=dense-tight-crumb) and [blowouts on the side](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=blowouts-on-the-side).

</details>

### Case 6 — The ghost

R1-04. The poolish was mixed at 0.1 % yeast and left 22 h because plans changed; the kitchen was 24 °C. At use it was collapsed, thin and smelled strongly of alcohol. The final dough was mixed as normal, bulk took only 60 min, and the dough felt slack. The baguettes were baked at 250 °C with steam and vented at 12 min; the oven thermometer confirmed temperature. After 26 min they were still pale yellow-tan, with little colour on the ears. They taste flat and slightly sour-boozy.

<details class="answer"><summary>Diagnosis</summary>

**Sugar depletion from a badly over-ripe poolish.** Evidence: 22 h at 24 °C is far past the 12–16 h window at 20–22 °C; the collapsed, soupy, alcoholic poolish; fast bulk and slack dough from the large, hungry yeast population and softened gluten; and a pale crust although oven temperature and steam were verified. The oven is ruled out by the thermometer reading, so the crust lacked reducing sugars for Maillard browning. Test: the next time a poolish is over-ripe, split the batch: one dough with the old poolish and one with the old poolish at half PFF, with fresh flour and water making up the rest; compare colour. The correction is planning: reduce poolish yeast or temperature, or mix it later. See [poolish or biga over-ripe](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=poolish-or-biga-over-ripe) and [pale crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=pale-crust).

</details>

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Experienced bakers rarely diagnose from the loaf alone. They diagnose from the loaf **and the production record**: dough temperature, times, volumes, fridge temperature. Most of the six cases would be unsolvable without the notes. That is why your lab notebook records numbers at every step. [18.3](stage-1/module-18/lesson-03.md) turns this clinic into a general diagnostic method.

</div>

## On the bench

1. Bake [R1-06](recipes/R1-06-sourdough-loaf.md) at least twice. Run the schedule table above, and record every temperature, time and volume.
2. Run [X1-14](experiments/X1-14-levain.md) beforehand or between bakes, and use its peak times to plan the levain build.
3. On the second bake, change **one** sourness lever from the table and taste the loaves blind if you can.
4. Complete the clinic and check your answers. Note which troubleshooting entries you needed and which clues you missed.

## What goes wrong

- **Dense, sour first loaf.** Immature or cold levain; see Case 1 and [dense or gummy sourdough crumb](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=dense-or-gummy-sourdough-crumb).
- **Flat, spreading loaf.** Over-fermentation, a warm fridge, or weak shaping; see [flat spreading sourdough loaf](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=flat-spreading-sourdough-loaf).
- **Pale crust.** Over-fermentation, or pulled too early; see [pale crust on sourdough](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=pale-crust-on-sourdough).
- **Too sour or not sour enough.** See [loaf too sour](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=loaf-too-sour) and [loaf not sour enough](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=loaf-not-sour-enough).

## Lab notebook

Starter: refresh history (ratios, temperatures, peak times). Levain: build time, temperature, rise % and pH at use. Dough: autolyse time, DDT calculation and actual temperature, fold times, % rise every hour, time and cues at the end of bulk, notes on pre-shape behaviour. Retard: fridge temperature at the shelf, hours. Bake: oven temperature, time covered and uncovered, internal temperature. Evaluation: crumb and profile photos with a ruler, crust colour, acidity (1–5), keeping on days 1–3. For the clinic: your hypotheses and final diagnoses for each case, and the clues you missed.

## Check yourself

1. Your R1-06 bulk has taken 6 h, the dough is +35 %, and the dough is at 23 °C. Should you shape now?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

No. The dough is under-fermented: +35 % and cooler than the 25–26 °C target, which explains the slow rate. Move it somewhere warmer if possible and continue until the cues say +50–75 %, domed, with convex edges. Check every 30 min. Next time, raise the DDT.

</details>

2. You want a milder loaf without changing the schedule. Name two changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Use the levain at peak rather than after it, and keep the starter as a refreshed liquid culture (2–3 room-temperature feeds before baking). You can also reduce PFF from 15 % to 10–12 % and shorten the retard to 8–10 h, but those change timing slightly.

</details>

3. Two loaves, same formula: A was retarded 10 h at 4 °C, B 10 h at 8 °C. B is flatter and paler. Explain both symptoms.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 8 °C fermentation runs roughly 1.5–2× faster than at 4 °C, so B over-proofed during the retard: the gluten, weakened by acid and time, could not hold the shape (flatter), and the yeast and bacteria consumed more of the sugar that would have browned the crust (paler).

</details>

4. Why is "a few large holes under the top crust and dense crumb elsewhere" a sign of under-fermentation rather than over-fermentation?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

In under-fermented dough the gluten network is tight and gas is limited, so gas collects in a few places rather than in many evenly distributed cells. In the oven the strong, late spring pushes up under the skin and tears the crust away from the crumb. Over-fermented dough tends instead to spread and bake flat with an even, collapsed or coarse crumb.

</details>
