# M02 · Flour, water, salt: the dough system

## Purpose

Lean bread is four ingredients, and three of them — flour, water and salt — form the dough before yeast does anything. This module treats that dough as a system you can measure and control: what flour is and how to read it, how gluten forms and what strengthens or weakens it, where water goes and what hydration does, and how salt and the mixing method set the dough's strength. You finish with test doughs, gluten balls, four controlled experiments and a documented "house flour" that the rest of the bread modules build on.

## Objectives

By the end of M02 you can:

1. Read any flour label — US, French, German, Italian or local — and extract protein, refinement (ash/extraction) and additives; choose a flour for a product by protein range.
2. Explain gluten formation (glutenin, gliadin, water, work, time) and predict the effect of mixing, salt, fat, sugar, bran, acid, oxidation and enzymes.
3. Set hydration for a product and a specific flour, using a test dough, and predict its effect on handling, fermentation, volume, crumb and crust.
4. Choose a mixing method (hand or machine; short, improved, intensive) and a salt timing, with times, speeds and dough-temperature targets.
5. Describe dough behaviour in rheological terms (elastic, viscous, relaxation) and use rest deliberately.
6. Design and run a one-variable dough experiment with controlled temperature and timing.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Endosperm | The starchy inner part of the wheat kernel (~80–85 % of its weight); the source of white flour's starch and gluten-forming proteins. |
| Bran | The outer layers of the kernel (~13–15 %): fibre, minerals, pigments; absorbs water and cuts gluten. |
| Germ | The embryo (~2–3 %): oil, vitamins, enzymes and glutathione; makes whole-grain flour perishable. |
| Extraction rate | Kilograms of flour obtained per 100 kg of wheat; ~70–75 % for white flour, ~100 % for whole wheat. |
| Ash | The mineral residue after burning flour; a measure of refinement. The basis of French T-types (T55 ≈ 0.55 % ash). |
| Hard / soft wheat | Kernel hardness: hard wheat is usually higher in protein and mills into granular flour with more damaged starch; soft wheat into fine, low-protein flour. |
| Gluten | The viscoelastic protein network formed from glutenin and gliadin when flour is hydrated and worked. |
| Glutenin | Large polymeric gluten proteins linked by disulfide bonds; give dough elasticity and strength. |
| Gliadin | Smaller gluten proteins; give dough extensibility and viscosity. |
| Disulfide bond | A strong covalent sulfur–sulfur link between protein chains; the "welds" of the gluten network. |
| Glutathione | A small reducing molecule (from wheat germ and dead yeast cells) that breaks disulfide bonds and weakens dough. |
| Elasticity | A dough's tendency to spring back after being deformed. |
| Extensibility | How far a dough can be stretched before it tears. |
| Windowpane test | Stretching a small piece of dough thin to judge gluten development by translucency and tear shape. |
| Water absorption | The amount of water a flour needs to reach a standard dough consistency; rises with protein, damaged starch and bran. |
| Damaged starch | Starch granules broken in milling; absorbs much more water than intact starch and is readily attacked by amylase. |
| Autolyse | A 20–60 minute rest of flour and water only, before adding salt and yeast, to hydrate the flour and develop gluten passively. |
| Bassinage | Adding part of the water late in mixing, after gluten has developed, to reach high hydration. |
| Stretch-and-fold | Folding the dough over itself in its container at intervals during bulk fermentation to build strength. |
| Viscoelastic | Behaving partly like an elastic solid and partly like a viscous liquid; dough's defining rheological property. |
| Falling number | A test of amylase activity in flour (seconds); low values mean sprouted wheat and a risk of gummy crumb. |
