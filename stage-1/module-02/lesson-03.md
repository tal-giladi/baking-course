## Why this lesson exists

"70 % hydration" is a number on paper. What it *does* depends on how much water your flour can hold. The same 70 % is a stiff, easy dough with a whole-wheat flour and a puddle with a pastry flour. This lesson explains where water goes in a dough, why flours differ, and what changing hydration does to handling, fermentation, oven spring, crumb and crust — so you can choose a hydration for a product and adjust it for a new flour instead of copying a number.

## You will be able to

1. Explain water absorption and list the flour components that absorb water, in order of how much they hold.
2. Predict how protein, damaged starch and bran change the right hydration for a flour.
3. Describe the effect of increasing hydration on handling, fermentation speed, oven spring, crumb and crust.
4. Use an autolyse and bassinage deliberately, with times and quantities.
5. Adjust a formula's hydration for a new flour using a small test dough.

## Where the water goes

Recall from [01.2](stage-1/module-01/lesson-02.md): hydration = total water ÷ total flour × 100. At 70 % hydration a dough with 1000 g flour contains 700 g water. That water is not all in the same state.

| Flour component | Share of white flour | Water it holds (approx., per its own dry weight) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intact starch granules | ~65–70 % | ~0.3–0.45× | Granules absorb water on the surface and swell slightly at room temperature; they gelatinize only in the oven |
| Damaged starch | ~4–9 % of flour (hard wheat higher) | ~1.5–2× | Broken granules in milling; absorb fast and are attacked by amylase |
| Gluten-forming protein | ~8–14 % | ~2× | The largest single driver of absorption differences between white flours |
| Pentosans (arabinoxylans), bran fibre | ~2–3 % in white, much more in whole wheat | up to ~10× | Small quantity, large effect; hydrate slowly |

The rest of the water is relatively free: it lubricates, dissolves salt and sugar, and gives the dough flow. The balance between bound and free water is what you feel as stiffness or stickiness. [SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md) develops the bound/free idea further; [SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md) covers starch.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Water absorption is measured industrially with a farinograph: the percentage of water needed to bring a dough to a fixed standard stiffness. Typical values: cake flour ~50–55 %, all-purpose ~56–62 %, bread flour ~60–66 %, whole wheat ~65–75 %. A useful rule of thumb from these tables: among similar white flours, each extra 1 % protein raises absorption by roughly 1–1.5 percentage points. Standard professional practice; treat as an estimate and confirm with a test dough.

</div>

## Damaged starch

Milling hard wheat breaks some starch granules. Damaged starch matters in three ways:

1. **It absorbs a lot of water quickly** — a flour with more damaged starch "takes" more water at mixing.
2. **Amylase attacks damaged starch** much faster than intact granules, producing sugars for yeast. A moderate level supports fermentation and crust colour in long lean processes.
3. **Too much** releases its water later as enzymes break it down, so a dough that felt fine at mixing becomes sticky and slack during fermentation, and the crumb can become gummy.

Millers control damaged starch by roll settings; hard wheat bread flour typically has more than soft wheat pastry flour. You cannot measure it at home, but you can see its signature: a dough that is correct at mixing and progressively softer during bulk.

## Hydration changes everything downstream

Holding flour, salt, yeast and process constant, here is what raising hydration does. You will verify every row in [X1-04](experiments/X1-04-hydration-series.md).

| Property | Lower hydration (~58–64 %, bread flour) | Higher hydration (~72–80 %) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handling | Firm, smooth, easy to knead and shape; can tear if too dry | Soft, sticky, flows; needs wet hands, bench scraper, folds | More free water lubricates and dilutes the network |
| Gluten development | Needs more work; develops fast once hydrated | Develops with less work, over time; hard to develop by kneading | Mobility of proteins |
| Extensibility | Lower | Higher | Water plasticizes gluten |
| Fermentation speed | Slower | Faster (moderately) | More water available for yeast and enzymes; softer dough expands with less resistance |
| Shape holding / oven spring | Holds shape; spring limited by stiffness | Spreads unless strength built; spring can be large if strength is adequate | More extensible, more steam generation inside |
| Crumb | Fine, even, closed, cottony | Open, irregular, glossy cell walls | Soft dough lets cells grow and merge |
| Crust | Thicker, duller, softer after cooling | Thinner, crisper, more blistered; needs longer bake to dry | More water to evaporate |
| Bake time | Shorter for same size | Longer; underbaked centre risk | More water must be driven off |
| Shelf life | Stales sooner | Stays moist longer | More water retained in crumb ([04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md)) |

"Higher hydration = better bread" is a myth. Hydration is a design choice:

| Product (white bread flour unless noted) | Typical hydration |
|---|---|
| Bagels | 50–58 % |
| Pretzels | 55–60 % |
| Sandwich / pan loaf | 60–66 % |
| Rolls | 60–68 % |
| Baguette | 65–72 % |
| Country loaf (pain de campagne) | 68–78 % |
| Ciabatta | 75–85 % |
| Focaccia | 75–90 % |
| 100 % whole-wheat loaf | 75–85 % |

## Flour-to-flour differences and the test dough

When a formula says 70 % and your flour is different from the author's, expect differences. Adjustments, in order of size:

| Change of flour | Typical hydration adjustment |
|---|---|
| +1 % protein (similar white flour) | +1 to +1.5 percentage points |
| White → whole wheat (100 %) | +5 to +10 points, and more rest time |
| Replacing 30 % of white with whole wheat | roughly +2 to +3 points |
| Bread flour → all-purpose | −2 to −4 points |
| Stone-ground / high-extraction white | +2 to +4 points |
| Very fresh (unaged) flour | reduce slightly, or add strength |

**The test-dough method** (use it every time you change flour):

1. Mix 100 g flour with water at the formula's hydration minus 5 points (e.g. 65 g for a 70 % formula), plus 2 g salt.
2. Knead 3 minutes, rest 10 minutes covered (to let absorption continue), then add water 1 g at a time, kneading after each, until the dough reaches the handling you want for the product.
3. The final water in grams is your hydration for that flour. Record it on the flour card from [02.1](stage-1/module-02/lesson-01.md).

Whole-grain flours need the rest to be 20–30 minutes; they keep absorbing.

## Autolyse: letting water do the work

The **autolyse** was named and popularized by Professor Raymond Calvel ([Calvel]). Mix **only flour and water** until no dry flour remains, cover, and rest **20–60 minutes** before adding salt and yeast (or levain) and mixing.

What happens during the rest:

- Flour hydrates completely without mechanical work.
- Gluten begins to form and align passively.
- Native proteases trim some chains, making the dough more **extensible**.
- Amylase begins producing sugars from damaged starch.

Bench consequences: mixing time drops (often by 20–40 %), less oxidation from the mixer (creamier crumb, more flavour), a dough that shapes and stretches more easily, and often better volume.

Practical rules:

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Leave out salt | Salt tightens gluten and slows hydration; it defeats part of the purpose |
| Leave out yeast (for a strict autolyse) | Fermentation should not start before the clock; some bakers add levain ("fermentolyse") for convenience — record it |
| 20–30 min for white bread flour; 30–60 min for strong or whole-grain flour | Longer is not always better; weak flours can turn slack |
| Hold back 3–5 % of water to dissolve the yeast/salt later, if needed | Lets you add ingredients without dry pockets |
| Account for temperature | The autolyse water sets the dough temperature; compute with DDT ([01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)) |

<div class="callout warn">

**Common confusion:** "Autolyse" in bakery language means the flour–water rest. It is not the biological self-digestion of yeast (the original meaning of the word), and a dough with yeast and salt resting before kneading is simply a rest.

</div>

## Bassinage: adding water late

Very wet doughs are hard to develop because excess free water lubricates the proteins and keeps them apart. **Bassinage** (French) solves this: mix the dough at a lower hydration until the gluten is at moderate-to-good development, then add the reserved water in small portions on low speed or by hand, waiting until each addition is fully absorbed before the next.

| Parameter | Typical value |
|---|---|
| Water held back | 5–15 % of flour weight (e.g. mix at 68 %, add 7 % to reach 75 %) |
| Additions | 3–4 portions |
| When | After gluten reaches improved development (cohesive, smooth, pulls from bowl) |
| Speed | Low; the dough will first break into slippery pieces, then come back together |
| Endpoint | Dough re-forms into one smooth mass and clears the bowl sides |

Bassinage is also a correction tool: if your test dough tells you the flour absorbs more than expected, you add water late rather than start again.

2. Autolyse comparison (small): two 100 g flour + 68 g water doughs. Mix A and add 2 g salt immediately; mix B, rest 30 min, then add 2 g salt. Knead each until the windowpane is thin and translucent; record minutes and the stretch length after a 15 min rest (as in [02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md)).

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely water/starch cause | See |
|---|---|---|
| Dough fine at mixing, sticky and slack an hour later | Flour still absorbing (whole grain) — or damaged starch/amylase releasing water; overfermentation | [Weak, slack dough](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=weak-slack-dough) |
| Dough too stiff, tears, dense loaf | Hydration too low for this flour's absorption | Test-dough method above |
| Loaf spreads flat, open but pale crumb near base | Hydration higher than the strength built; not enough folds | [02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md), [troubleshooting](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md) |
| Crumb gummy although well risen | Underbaked for the hydration; excess amylase; cut hot | [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md) |
| Wet dough impossible to knead by hand | Kneading method wrong for the hydration | Use slap-and-fold or Rubaud and folds ([02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md)) |

## Check yourself

1. A formula for a 72 % baguette dough was written for a flour with 11.5 % protein. Your flour has 13 %. What hydration would you start testing at, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 73.5–74 %: 1.5 points more protein × roughly 1–1.5 points absorption per 1 % protein ≈ +1.5 to +2.3 points. Confirm with a test dough (start 5 points lower and add water) because gluten quality and damaged starch also differ.

</details>

2. Convert a 1000 g flour dough at 65 % hydration to 72 %, using bassinage. How much water goes in at the start and how much later?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total water at 72 % = 720 g. Mix at 65 % = 650 g, then add the remaining 70 g in 3–4 portions (e.g. 4 × 17.5 g) after the dough reaches improved development.

</details>

3. Why does an autolyse shorten mixing time, and why should salt be left out?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

During the rest, flour hydrates fully and gluten forms and aligns passively without mechanical work; proteases increase extensibility. Salt tightens the gluten and slows hydration, so including it would reduce these effects.

</details>

4. Two loaves: 62 % and 76 % hydration, same flour, same schedule. Predict three differences in the finished loaves.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The 76 % loaf: more open and irregular crumb; thinner, crisper, more blistered crust; flatter shape unless strength was built; moister crumb that stales more slowly; longer bake needed. The 62 % loaf: tighter even crumb, taller for its weight if shaped well, thicker softer crust.

</details>

5. Why does a whole-wheat test dough need a longer rest before you judge its hydration?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Bran fibre and pentosans absorb water slowly and in large amounts; the dough stiffens for 20–30 minutes after mixing. Judging it immediately leads to an under-hydrated dough.

</details>
