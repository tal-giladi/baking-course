## Why this lesson exists

Every decision in bread — and many in pastry — is a decision about gluten: how much to develop, how fast, and how to keep it from forming where you do not want it. A baguette needs an extensible network that stretches into thin gas-cell walls; a tart shell needs almost none. If you understand what builds and breaks the network you can predict the effect of mixing time, fat, sugar, salt, acid, bran and even old yeast before you bake.

## You will be able to

1. Describe the roles of glutenin and gliadin and the bonds that hold the gluten network together, at a level that predicts dough behaviour.
2. Distinguish elasticity from extensibility and diagnose which one a dough has too much or too little of.
3. List what strengthens and what weakens gluten, and choose a lever to adjust a dough.
4. Perform a windowpane test correctly and know when it misleads.

## Two proteins, one network

Wheat flour contains two families of storage proteins that are insoluble in water and make up roughly 75–85 % of the total protein. Dry, they are just powder in the endosperm. With water and mechanical work they combine into **gluten**.

| | Glutenin | Gliadin |
|---|---|---|
| Molecular form | Very large polymers: subunits linked end-to-end and side-to-side by disulfide bonds; some are among the largest proteins in nature | Smaller single chains, mostly folded individually |
| Contribution | **Elasticity** and strength — the spring that pulls dough back | **Extensibility** and viscosity — the lubricant that lets chains slide |
| In your hands | "Bouncy", resists stretching, snaps back | "Stretchy", flows, sags |
| If dominant | Tough, rubbery, shrinks back, tears | Slack, sticky, spreads, weak |

Roughly equal amounts of the two are present in wheat. Their exact ratio and the size distribution of the glutenin polymers vary with wheat variety and season — this is part of "gluten quality" that a protein number does not capture.

<div class="callout science">

**Science: the bonds, intuitively.** Think of the network as long chains held by three kinds of connection:

- **Disulfide bonds** (S–S) between sulfur-containing amino acids (cysteine). Covalent and strong: they link glutenin subunits into giant polymers. These are the "welds".
- **Hydrogen bonds**, very numerous because gluten proteins are rich in glutamine. Individually weak, they break under stress and re-form in a new position — this is why dough can be reshaped and then holds its new shape.
- **Hydrophobic interactions** between water-avoiding parts of the chains, adding cohesion.

A dough that you stretch and then rest "relaxes" because the weak bonds rearrange to relieve the stress. A dough that has been mixed a long time is stronger partly because its chains have been untangled and aligned so more bonds can form. See [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md) for protein structure and [SC-12](stage-1/science/sc-12-rheology.md) for how this becomes elastic and viscous behaviour.

</div>

## How the network forms: water plus work

Gluten does not exist in flour. Two things create it.

**1. Hydration.** When water reaches the proteins they swell and become mobile. Hydrated gluten holds roughly twice its dry weight in water — which is why a washed gluten ball is about two-thirds water (you will measure this in [X1-03](experiments/X1-03-gluten-wash.md)). Without enough water, the proteins cannot move and link; with far too much, they are diluted and the network is loose.

**2. Mechanical work — or time.** Mixing brings hydrated proteins into contact, stretches and unfolds them, aligns them into sheets and strands, and incorporates air (oxygen), which promotes the formation of disulfide links. You can watch the stages in a mixer:

| Stage | What you see | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Pick-up / clean-up | Shaggy, lumpy, wet mass; tears immediately | Flour hydrating, proteins beginning to contact |
| Initial development | Cohesive but rough; tears when stretched to a few cm | Short, randomly oriented network |
| Improved / moderate development | Smoother, some sheen, stretches to a thick, cloudy membrane that tears with jagged edges | Network continuous but not fully aligned |
| Full development | Smooth, silky, elastic; stretches to a thin, translucent membrane; tears with a smooth round edge | Extensive, aligned network |
| Overmixing / breakdown | Wet, sticky, shiny, stringy; loses elasticity and tears | Network being mechanically broken and bonds exchanged faster than they form |

**Time can substitute for work.** A dough that is just mixed and then left alone develops gluten on its own: water keeps diffusing, proteins keep aligning slowly, and enzymes trim chains so they rearrange more easily. This is the basis of the autolyse ([02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md)), of stretch-and-fold bulk fermentation ([02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md)) and of the classic no-knead method. [X1-05](experiments/X1-05-mixing-time.md) will show you how far time alone gets you.

## Elasticity vs extensibility

Two different properties, often confused:

- **Elasticity** — the dough's tendency to spring back after deformation. Test: stretch a piece and let go; how much does it recoil? Pull a pre-shaped ball into a rectangle; does it shrink back?
- **Extensibility** — how far the dough can be stretched before it tears. Test: slowly pull a piece between your hands; how long does it get before rupturing?

| Dough | Elasticity | Extensibility | Bench symptom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balanced lean dough | Moderate | Good | Holds shape, stretches when coaxed |
| Bagel / high-gluten | High | Moderate | Fights shaping, needs rests |
| Freshly mixed intensive dough | High | Low | Snaps back, tears when pulled fast |
| Long-rested / autolysed | Lower | High | Stretches easily, may sag |
| Overfermented or protease-damaged | Low | Low | Slack, tears, no strength |

What you want depends on the product. Baguettes need high extensibility to be rolled long without shrinking; pan bread tolerates higher elasticity; pizza stretched by hand needs extensibility with just enough elasticity to hold its rim. Almost everything a baker does between mixing and shaping — resting, folding, temperature, time — is managing this balance.

<div class="callout key">

**Key point:** Resting a dough lowers its *apparent* elasticity (stress relaxes) and raises extensibility, without losing strength. Handling it (folding, pre-shaping) raises elasticity. So "the dough fights me" is usually solved by 10–20 minutes of rest, not by adding flour.

</div>

## What strengthens gluten

| Factor | Mechanism (intuitive) | Bench use |
|---|---|---|
| Mixing / kneading | Hydrates, aligns and links proteins; adds oxygen | Mixing time set by product ([02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md)) |
| Salt (1.8–2.2 %) | Salt ions screen electrical charges on the proteins so chains pack closer and bond more; dough tightens within a minute | Adding salt to a slack dough visibly firms it; see [X1-06](experiments/X1-06-salt.md) |
| Moderate acidity | In the normal range of fermented doughs, acid tightens the network | Sourdough and long-fermented doughs feel stronger than their protein suggests |
| Oxidation | Oxygen from air and oxidizing improvers (ascorbic acid, which is converted to an oxidant during mixing) favour disulfide links and neutralize weakening thiols | Flour with ascorbic acid tolerates more water and fermentation; intensive mixing oxidizes (and also bleaches the crumb and dulls flavour) |
| Time / rest / autolyse | Continued hydration and passive alignment | Shorter mixing for the same development |
| Folds during bulk | Stretch and realign an already hydrated network | Build strength in wet doughs without a mixer |
| Higher protein flour or vital gluten | More network-forming material | Blend 1–3 % vital gluten into weak or whole-grain flour |

## What weakens gluten

| Factor | Mechanism (intuitive) | Bench consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Fat | Coats proteins, lubricates and physically separates strands ("shortening") | Brioche is mixed *before* most butter goes in; short pastry uses fat to prevent gluten ([08.1](stage-1/module-08/lesson-01.md), [11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md)) |
| Sugar | Attracts water away from the proteins and delays hydration | High-sugar doughs need longer mixing and often osmotolerant yeast ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)) |
| Bran and germ | Particles cut the network; germ glutathione breaks bonds | Whole-wheat loaves are smaller; soak or autolyse helps |
| Overmixing | Mechanical rupture and bond exchange faster than re-formation | Sticky, shiny, stringy dough; rare with hand or home stand mixer, easy with a high-speed machine or a very small dough |
| Proteases (enzymes that cut protein) | Cut the chains | From some malted/enzyme-treated flours; from fresh pineapple, papaya, kiwi and fig; excess time in a slack dough |
| Excess acid | At high acidity the network becomes weak, short and tears | Over-soured levain doughs slump; overfermented dough tears |
| Glutathione (reducing agent) | Its thiol group attacks disulfide bonds and cuts glutenin polymers | Released by dead yeast cells (some active dry yeast, old or heat-damaged yeast) and by wheat germ; gives slack, sticky dough |
| Too much or too little water | Too much dilutes; too little prevents hydration | See the hydration series [X1-04](experiments/X1-04-hydration-series.md) |
| High dough temperature | Weak bonds break more easily; enzymes faster; fermentation outpaces strength | Aim for 24–26 °C for most lean doughs ([01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)) |

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Bakers correct gluten with the cheapest lever first: water and mixing, then rest and folds, then flour blend, and only then additives. Every lever has side effects — more mixing gives volume but a whiter, blander crumb; more folds add strength but take time; ascorbic acid adds tolerance but is not accepted in some "clean label" or traditional products.

</div>

## The windowpane test and its limits

**How to do it.** Take a 15–25 g piece of dough. Wet or lightly oil your fingers. Gently stretch it from the centre outward, rotating, using your fingertips and thumbs, until it is thin. Hold it up to the light.

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tears before it is thin, ragged edges | Under-developed (fine for some processes) |
| Thin but cloudy, tears with jagged edge | Moderate / improved development |
| Thin, translucent, you can see light and shadows through it; hole has smooth round edge | Full development |

**Limits — when the windowpane misleads:**

1. **Many good doughs should not pass it at the end of mixing.** Artisan lean doughs made with a short or improved mix are deliberately under-developed at the mixer; strength is built during bulk by fermentation and folds. Checking the windowpane at the end of bulk is often more meaningful.
2. **Whole-grain doughs** rarely give a clean pane: bran particles puncture the film even when the network is strong.
3. **Very wet doughs** (above ~78 %) are hard to stretch without tearing even when well developed; and very stiff doughs tear from lack of water, not lack of gluten.
4. **Technique matters.** Pulling fast tears any dough. Stretch slowly and let the piece relax for a few seconds midway.
5. **It tests extensibility and continuity, not elasticity or gas retention.** A dough that windowpanes but has no tension after shaping can still spread.

Use it as one data point with dough temperature, surface appearance, and the feel of elasticity — not as the definition of "done".

## On the bench

1. Run [X1-03](experiments/X1-03-gluten-wash.md) if you have not: hold a raw gluten ball and stretch it; notice how it snaps back (glutenin) and flows slowly (gliadin).
2. Windowpane practice: mix 100 g bread flour with 65 g water and 2 g salt. Knead by hand. Test a small piece at 2, 5, 8 and 12 minutes. Photograph each pane against a window. Rest 15 minutes after the last test and test again — note how much easier it stretches.
3. Rest test: stretch a 50 g piece into a strip, measure length just before tearing. Ball it, rest 15 minutes covered, repeat. Record both lengths.
4. Fat test (optional, 10 minutes): split the same dough in two; knead 10 g soft butter into one half; compare stretch and windowpane.
5. Then run [X1-05 Mixing time](experiments/X1-05-mixing-time.md), which turns these observations into loaves. In [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md) you will use the development cues from this lesson as the mixing endpoint.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely gluten cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Dough slack, sticky, spreads | Under-developed; too much water; protease/glutathione; overfermented | More mixing or folds; less water; check yeast and flour; see [Weak, slack dough](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=weak-slack-dough) |
| Dough snaps back, will not stretch, tears | Too elastic: just handled, too strong a flour, too little rest | Rest 10–20 min covered; plan a pre-shape and bench rest |
| Dough sticky and shiny after long machine mixing | Overmixing (breakdown) | Shorter second-speed time; check dough temperature; start again — a broken-down dough does not fully recover |
| Whole-wheat dough dense, crumbly | Bran interference, insufficient hydration | More water, soak/autolyse, blend with bread flour |

## Lab notebook

Record for each dough: flour and protein; hydration; mixing method and minutes at each speed; dough temperature at the end of mixing; windowpane result with photo; elasticity (springs back: little / moderate / strong) and extensibility (length at tear in cm from a standard 50 g strip) — the same simple scale throughout M02 so results are comparable.

## Check yourself

1. You add 5 % butter to a lean dough at the start of mixing and it takes much longer to develop. Why, and what would you change?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat coats the proteins and lubricates them, interfering with hydration and bonding, so the network forms more slowly. Mix the lean dough to moderate or full development first, then add the fat gradually (the brioche method in [06.4](stage-1/module-06/lesson-04.md)).

</details>

2. A dough made with old active dry yeast is unusually slack even though the flour and hydration are your standard ones. What is the likely mechanism?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dead yeast cells release glutathione, a reducing agent whose thiol group breaks disulfide bonds in glutenin, shortening the polymers and weakening the network. Check the yeast (proof test) and replace it.

</details>

3. Your baguette dough shrinks back every time you try to roll it long. Is this a problem of elasticity or extensibility, and what is the first correction?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Too much elasticity relative to extensibility at that moment, usually because the pre-shape was recent or tight. First correction: longer bench rest (15–30 min covered) and a gentler pre-shape; then consider a weaker/less strong flour or less mixing.

</details>

4. Give two reasons a well-made high-hydration country loaf might fail the windowpane test at the end of mixing.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It was intentionally given a short/improved mix and gets its strength during bulk from folds and fermentation; and very wet dough tears easily when stretched by hand. If it contains whole-grain flour, bran particles also puncture the film.

</details>

5. Why does salt make a dough feel tighter within a minute of adding it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Salt ions screen charges on the gluten proteins, reducing repulsion so the chains associate more closely and form more interactions. The network becomes stronger and less sticky almost immediately; this is independent of salt's slower effect on fermentation.

</details>
