## Why this lesson exists

A meringue is egg white and sugar, nothing else, yet three classical methods give three different materials. One bakes into a light, crisp shell but weeps within an hour if you leave it; another holds sharp piped edges for a day and survives being folded into butter; the third sits between them. The only differences are when the sugar goes in and how much heat reaches the whites. In 09.2 you learned how proteins hold air. Here you add the tools from 13.1, dissolved sugar and a hot syrup, and learn to choose the method by the job, not by habit.

## You will be able to

- Make French, Swiss and Italian meringue to a stiff, glossy peak, with measurable endpoints (temperature, specific gravity, time).
- Explain the stability ranking of the three methods from sugar dissolution and heat-induced protein unfolding.
- Choose a sugar-to-white ratio and method for a given use: baked shells, pie topping, buttercream, mousse, torching.
- Bake meringues dry, white and uncracked, and diagnose weeping, beading, stickiness, cracking and browning.
- Apply the egg-safety rules to each method.

## What sugar and heat add to a white foam

Recall the white foam from [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md): partly unfolded proteins form elastic films around air bubbles, and the foam fails by **drainage** (liquid runs down between bubbles), **coalescence** (films break and bubbles merge) and **coarsening** (small bubbles shrink into large ones). Unsweetened stiff whites are light but last minutes.

Sugar fixes this, at a price:

- **Dissolved sugar thickens the water** in the films. A 1:2 white-to-sugar meringue has more sugar than water: the "liquid" between the bubbles is a syrup of around 65–70 % sugar. Drainage slows enormously, and the films resist thinning.
- **Sugar slows whipping and reduces volume.** The viscous syrup slows the proteins reaching the bubble surface. That is why the sugar goes in *after* the whites have started foaming.
- **Undissolved sugar is a weakness.** Crystals that have not dissolved draw water out of the foam over time and form syrup droplets. This is the main reason a French meringue is less stable than a Swiss one.

Heat adds a second tool. Egg-white proteins begin to unfold at different temperatures: ovotransferrin from about 61 °C, lysozyme around 70–75 °C, ovalbumin around 80–84 °C ([09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md)). Warming whites to 60–70 °C before or during whipping partly unfolds some of them. The foam that forms is finer, denser and stiffer, with thicker films, and it tolerates stress far better. Too much heat (above about 75–80 °C in the bowl) coagulates proteins into lumps that no longer form films.

## The three methods

| | French | Swiss | Italian |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How the sugar goes in** | Raw sugar added gradually to whipping whites after soft peak | Whites and sugar heated together over a bain-marie until the sugar dissolves, then whipped | Sugar cooked into a syrup at 118–121 °C and poured into whipping whites |
| **Heat to the whites** | None | 60–70 °C (71 °C if pasteurizing) | Brief, from the hot syrup |
| **Typical white : sugar** | 1 : 1 to 1 : 2 | 1 : 1.5 to 1 : 2 | 1 : 2 (with water for the syrup ≈ 30 % of its sugar) |
| **Texture** | Lightest, largest bubbles, softest peak for its sugar | Dense, fine, very glossy, satiny | Fine, glossy, smooth, firm; pipes sharp detail |
| **Stability (unbaked)** | Lowest: starts to weep within 30–60 min | High: hours | Highest: hours to a day |
| **Egg safety** | Raw unless baked | Safe if held at 71 °C, or with pasteurized whites | Not reliably pasteurized; use pasteurized whites for uncooked uses if it matters |
| **Main uses** | Baked shells, kisses, pavlova, folding into sponges and soufflés; macarons (French method, Stage 2) | Baked meringues with a denser bite, Swiss meringue buttercream, piped decoration, pie and tart topping | Italian meringue buttercream, mousses, torched toppings (lemon tart, baked Alaska), macarons (Italian method, Stage 2) |

### French meringue

The simplest method. Whip the whites (at room temperature, 18–22 °C, for faster foaming) with cream of tartar at about 1 % of the white weight to foamy, then to soft peak. Add the sugar a spoonful at a time, about 10–15 % of it every 20–30 seconds, while whipping at medium-high speed, and continue until the meringue is **stiff, glossy and smooth when rubbed between fingers**. For a 1:2 ratio, a common technique is to whip half the sugar in as caster sugar and fold the other half in at the end as icing sugar: icing sugar dissolves faster and the meringue stays lighter.

French meringue gives the most volume per gram of white and bakes into the lightest, most fragile shell. It is also the least stable: some of the sugar is still undissolved, and the raw proteins form comparatively coarse bubbles. Pipe and bake it within 10–15 minutes, or fold it into the batter at once.

### Swiss meringue

Whisk whites and sugar together in a heatproof bowl over barely simmering water (bowl not touching the water), whisking constantly so the whites near the bowl wall do not cook. Heat to **60–70 °C**, until the sugar has fully dissolved: rub a little between your fingers and feel no grains. For guests who are pregnant, elderly, very young or immunocompromised, take it to **71 °C** (160 °F), the temperature recommended for egg dishes ([FDA-eggs]), or use pasteurized whites. Transfer to the mixer and whip at high speed until the meringue is stiff, glossy and the bowl feels cool or barely warm: about **25–30 °C**, typically 8–12 minutes in a stand mixer.

The sugar is fully dissolved and the proteins are partly unfolded before the air goes in, so the foam is denser and finer than French meringue, very glossy and very stable. It holds piped shapes for hours. Baked, it gives a denser, more candy-like crisp shell.

### Italian meringue

Italian meringue is a timing exercise. Cook a syrup of the sugar with about 30 % of its weight in water to **118–121 °C** ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)). Meanwhile whip the whites so they reach **soft-to-firm peak just as the syrup reaches temperature**: with a stand mixer, start whipping when the syrup reads about 110–112 °C. Many pastry chefs whip a small part of the sugar (about 10 % of the total) into the whites; it stabilizes them so they can wait a minute without going grainy if the syrup lags.

With the mixer at medium-high, pour the syrup in a **thin, steady stream down the side of the bowl**, between the whisk and the wall. Syrup that hits the whisk is flung onto the bowl wall, where it sets into threads and lumps. Then whip at high speed until the meringue is stiff, glossy and cooled to about **30–35 °C** for piping and torching, or to **≤25 °C** if butter goes in next ([13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md)). That takes 8–15 minutes.

The syrup lower end (118 °C) gives a softer, more supple meringue for mousses; the upper end (121 °C) gives a firmer one that holds sharp piping. Above about 125 °C the syrup starts to set into hard threads on contact with the cool whites.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** The syrup is at 118–121 °C and the mixer throws droplets if the stream touches the whisk. Pour from a small pan with a lip, keep your face back from the bowl, and never scrape syrup off the whisk while it is running. The Italian method heats the whites but is not a validated pasteurization step: for uncooked uses served to vulnerable people, use pasteurized whites.

</div>

### Sugar ratio

| White : sugar | Character | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| 1 : 1 | Soft, fast, light, less stable | Folding into soufflés and sponges; soft pie topping |
| 1 : 1.5 | Balanced | Swiss meringue buttercream, pie topping |
| 1 : 2 | Firm, glossy, stable, dries crisp | Baked meringues, pavlova, Italian meringue for decoration and buttercream |

More sugar means more stability, more gloss, more sweetness, a crisper dried product, and a longer whip. Below 1 : 1, a meringue is really sweetened whites and will not bake dry and crisp.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The three methods are one variable, applied at three strengths: how completely the sugar is dissolved and how much heat the proteins see before the foam must stand on its own. Rank them by that and you can predict their stability, density and gloss without memorizing a table.

</div>

### Measuring a meringue

Use the specific gravity (SG) method from 09.2: weigh a level 100 ml cup of meringue and divide by 100. Sugared meringues are heavier than plain whites. Typical values from home equipment at stiff peak:

| Meringue (1 : 2) | Typical SG |
|---|---|
| French | ~0.15–0.22 |
| Italian | ~0.20–0.28 |
| Swiss | ~0.22–0.32 |

Your mixer will give its own numbers; what matters is that you record them and reproduce them. Gloss (dull, satin, glossy) and peak shape are the other two routine checks.

## Baking meringues

A baked meringue is a foam that has been **dried**, not really baked: the goal is to remove nearly all the water while keeping the proteins and sugar white. The dry shell is a sugar glass supported by coagulated protein (13.1).

| Setting | Effect |
|---|---|
| **90–100 °C (195–212 °F)** | Dries slowly, stays white; the standard for plain meringues |
| **100–110 °C (212–230 °F)** | Faster; ivory tint begins |
| **120 °C+ (250 °F+)** | Beige to brown from browning reactions; faster expansion can crack the surface; interior may stay sticky while the outside colours |

Time depends on size: small kisses (3 cm, about 5 g) need about **60–90 minutes** at 100 °C; nests and 8 cm shells about **1.5–2 hours**; a large pavlova about 1.5 hours at 110–120 °C and then a cool-down. After baking, **turn the oven off and leave the meringues inside, door ajar, for at least an hour**: they finish drying and cool slowly, which prevents cracking from sudden contraction.

**Endpoint:** the meringue lifts cleanly off the parchment, sounds hollow when tapped, and a broken test piece is dry and crisp right through when cool. You can measure it: egg whites are about 88 % water, so a 1:2 meringue is about 29 % water. A fully dried meringue has lost about **25–28 %** of its piped weight. Weigh a tray before and after.

**Humidity.** Dried meringue is hygroscopic: it draws water from the air, and the sugar glass softens and turns sticky as it does (the glass problem from 13.1). On a humid day meringues take longer to dry and soften within hours at room temperature. Store them airtight, with a food-safe silica gel sachet if you have one, for up to 2 weeks. Never refrigerate them; the fridge is humid.

### Weeping and beading

Two defects appear as liquid:

- **Weeping** is syrup leaking out, usually at the base: a puddle under a pie topping, or sticky syrup under a shell. In a baked shell it usually means undissolved sugar or underbaking. On a pie or tart, it means the meringue *underneath* did not get hot enough to set; a hot filling underneath and an Italian or Swiss meringue both help.
- **Beading** is small amber droplets on the surface. It usually means overbaking (the proteins overcoagulated and squeezed out syrup) or sugar that was not dissolved. On a meringue pie, too hot or too long in the oven.

### Pavlova

A pavlova is a French meringue with a small amount of cornstarch and acid (for 100 g whites and 200 g sugar, typically about 5 g of each: cornstarch and white vinegar) spread into a thick disc and baked at 110–120 °C. The crust dries crisp, but the thick centre does not dry fully. The starch gelatinizes with the water held there, and the acid gives a whiter, more stable foam, so the centre sets soft and marshmallow-like instead of weeping. It is meant to crack a little as it cools.

## What goes wrong

- **Meringue will not reach stiff peak, or is runny.** A trace of yolk or fat in the whites, sugar added too early or too fast, or, for Italian, syrup too cool. See [meringue deflates or turns grainy](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=meringue-deflates-or-turns-grainy).
- **Gritty Swiss meringue.** The sugar never fully dissolved over the bain-marie. See [Swiss meringue grainy](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=swiss-meringue-grainy).
- **Liquid at the base or droplets on top.** See [meringue weeping or beading](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=meringue-weeping-or-beading).
- **Baked meringues sticky, cracked or beige.** See [baked meringue sticky or chewy](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=baked-meringue-sticky-or-chewy) and [baked meringue cracked or browned](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=baked-meringue-cracked-or-browned).

## Check yourself

1. Rank French, Swiss and Italian meringue by stability, and explain the ranking in one sentence each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Italian ≥ Swiss > French. Italian: the sugar arrives as a hot concentrated syrup, fully dissolved, and the heat partly unfolds and sets the proteins while the foam forms, giving fine, thick-walled bubbles. Swiss: sugar fully dissolved and proteins partly unfolded at 60–70 °C before whipping, giving a dense, fine, stable foam. French: raw proteins and partly undissolved sugar give coarser bubbles and syrup that drains, so it weeps within an hour. (Swiss and Italian are close; Italian usually holds sharper piped detail.)

</details>

2. You need a topping for a lemon tart that will be torched and served over the next 6 hours. Which meringue, which ratio, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Italian (or Swiss) at about 1 : 2. It needs to hold its shape and not weep onto the curd for hours, and it will not be baked, so the partial cooking and fully dissolved sugar matter. Italian torches evenly and holds sharp piping. French would weep and is raw.

</details>

3. A tray of 1:2 French meringues weighs 300 g piped. After 90 minutes at 100 °C and an hour in the switched-off oven, it weighs 245 g. Are they done?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Loss = 55 ÷ 300 = 18 %. A fully dried 1:2 meringue loses about 25–28 %, so they still hold roughly a third of their water: they will be chewy or sticky in the centre. Bake longer at 90–100 °C and check again.

</details>

4. Your Italian meringue has hard sugar threads on the bowl wall and small hard lumps in it. What happened, and what do you change?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The syrup hit the moving whisk and was flung onto the cool bowl wall, where it set into glass, or it was poured too fast, or was too hot (above about 125 °C). Pour a thin stream down the side of the bowl, between whisk and wall, with the mixer at medium-high, from a pan with a lip, and check the syrup is at 118–121 °C.

</details>

5. Why should you not refrigerate baked meringues, and how do you store them?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The refrigerator is humid, and the dried meringue, a sugar glass supported by protein, absorbs water and turns soft and sticky. Store them airtight at room temperature, with a silica gel sachet if possible, for up to about 2 weeks.

</details>
