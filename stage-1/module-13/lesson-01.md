## Why this lesson exists

Sugar and water are the whole ingredient list of a large part of pastry: the soaking syrup in a génoise, the 118 °C syrup behind Italian meringue and buttercream, fondant, nougat, brittle, caramel and decorative sugar. They differ only in how much water is left and whether the sugar is allowed to crystallize. A thermometer reading is your measurement of the first, and a handful of techniques control the second. In 07.2 you cooked sugar until it browned. Here you learn to stop earlier, on purpose, at a concentration you choose, and to decide whether the result sets clear, creamy or grainy.

## You will be able to

- Explain why the boiling point of a sugar syrup rises as water boils off, and use the boiling point to read the syrup's concentration.
- Name the sugar stages from thread to caramel with their temperature ranges, approximate sugar concentrations and uses, and confirm a stage with the cold-water test.
- Correct target temperatures for altitude and for your thermometer's error.
- Specify and measure uncooked syrups in °Brix, and calculate how to make or dilute a syrup to a target °Brix.
- Prevent unwanted crystallization in a cooked syrup with at least four techniques, and explain how controlled crystallization makes fondant smooth instead of grainy.

## Syrup concentration and boiling point

Put sugar and water in a pan and heat it. Once the sugar dissolves you have a solution, and the solution cannot boil at 100 °C. Dissolved sugar molecules lower water's vapour pressure, so the syrup must be hotter before its vapour pressure matches the air pressure above it and it boils. This is **boiling-point elevation** ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)). The effect is small in dilute syrups and very large in concentrated ones.

As the syrup boils, water leaves and sugar stays. Concentration rises, so the boiling point rises, so the temperature climbs. **At a given air pressure, each boiling temperature corresponds to one concentration.** That is why a thermometer in a boiling syrup is really a concentration gauge. When a recipe says "cook to 118 °C", it means "boil off water until the syrup is about 87 % sugar".

| Sucrose, % by weight | Boiling point at sea level (approx.) |
|---|---|
| 0 (water) | 100 °C |
| 50 (1:1 syrup) | ~102 °C |
| 70 | ~106 °C |
| 80 | ~111 °C |
| 85 | ~116 °C |
| 90 | ~121–122 °C |
| 95 | ~132–135 °C |
| 99 | ~150–155 °C |

Two things follow from this curve.

**Early boiling is slow, late boiling is fast.** A 1:1 syrup spends many minutes between 102 and 110 °C, because most of the water is still there to boil off. Above about 112 °C there is little water left, and each gram that leaves moves the temperature a lot. Near 118 °C the temperature commonly climbs 2–4 °C per minute on medium heat. Stay at the pan for the last part of every cook and have the next step ready.

**Water content is fixed by the endpoint, not by the start.** Starting with more water only lengthens the cook. That is why most syrups use just enough water to dissolve the sugar and wet it evenly: about 30–35 % of the sugar weight for sugar work. More water wastes time and raises the chance of crystals forming on the pan sides.

### How much water leaves

You can calculate the finished weight. At 118 °C the syrup is about 87 % sugar. If you start with 200 g sugar and 60 g water, the finished syrup weighs 200 ÷ 0.87 ≈ **230 g**: 200 g sugar and about 30 g water. You boiled off about 30 g. The same arithmetic tells you what an Italian meringue or a buttercream really contains.

## The sugar stages

Confectioners named each concentration range by what a drop of syrup does in cold water. The names survive because they describe the product: a fondant needs a soft ball, a brittle needs hard crack.

| Stage | Temperature (sea level) | Sugar, approx. % | Cold-water test | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thread | 106–112 °C (223–234 °F) | ~75–82 | Forms a soft thread between fingers; does not form a ball | Glazing syrups, candied-fruit syrups, some icings |
| Soft ball | 112–116 °C (234–241 °F) | ~83–86 | Forms a soft ball that flattens when lifted out | Fondant, fudge, lower end for Italian meringue |
| Firm ball | 118–120 °C (244–248 °F) | ~87 | Holds a ball that keeps its shape but is pliable | Italian meringue, pâte à bombe, soft caramels |
| Hard ball | 121–130 °C (250–266 °F) | ~90–93 | Hard ball that holds its shape but is still sticky | Nougat, firmer caramels, marshmallow (with other sugars) |
| Soft crack | 132–143 °C (270–289 °F) | ~95 | Separates into hard threads that bend before breaking | Butterscotch, toffee, taffy |
| Hard crack | 146–154 °C (295–309 °F) | ~99 | Brittle threads that snap cleanly | Brittle, lollipops, spun sugar, sugar glass |
| Caramel | 160 °C+ (320 °F+) | ~100 | Colour appears; the sugar is decomposing | Caramel ([07.2](stage-1/module-07/lesson-02.md), [R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)) |

Sources disagree by a degree or two at the boundaries, and the concentrations are approximate. The ranges above are standard professional practice ([Figoni], [Gisslen], [McGee]).

### The cold-water test

Keep a small bowl of iced water beside the stove. Dip a clean metal spoon in the syrup, let a few drops fall into the water, wait 5–10 seconds, then gather them with your fingers **under the water** and lift them out. Never touch syrup that is not submerged. Compare what you get with the table.

Use the test to **confirm** a thermometer reading, not to replace it. It is subjective, it takes time while the syrup keeps cooking, and each dip removes a little syrup. It is useful when you suspect your thermometer, when a batch is too small for the probe, and for learning what the numbers feel like.

## Measuring temperature you can trust

A 2 °C error at 118 °C is the difference between a soft and a firm Italian meringue. At 150 °C it can be the difference between glass and a faint yellow tint.

**Calibrate at the boiling point of water.** Boil water in the same pan, hold the probe in the middle of the water (not touching the pan), and read it. At sea level with an accurate thermometer it reads 100 °C. The reading you get includes both your thermometer's error and your altitude, which is exactly what you need: **subtract (100 − your reading) from every sugar target.** If water boils at 98.5 °C in your kitchen, cook a "118 °C" syrup to 116.5 °C.

**Altitude.** The boiling point of water falls by roughly **1 °C for every 300 m** of elevation. At 1,500 m water boils at about 95 °C, so every stage arrives about 5 °C lower. The concentration at each stage is what you are after; the temperature that marks it shifts with pressure. Weather changes the boiling point by a few tenths of a degree, which rarely matters.

**Immersion.** The probe tip must sit in the syrup, not in the foam or the air above it, with at least 2–3 cm immersed for a clip-on candy thermometer. Small batches are shallow: tilt the pan so the syrup pools over the tip, or use a smaller pan. Stir the syrup gently with the probe before reading if you have stopped stirring for crystallization reasons: the bottom is hotter than the top.

**Carry-over.** A heavy pan keeps heating the syrup after you turn the burner off. Take the pan off 1–2 °C before the target for a soft or firm ball, and for hard crack dip the base of the pan in cold water for 3–5 seconds to stop the rise.

## Uncooked syrups: °Brix

Below its boiling point a syrup has no temperature endpoint; you make it by weighing. For these, pastry uses **°Brix**: grams of sucrose per 100 g of solution. It is a percentage by weight.

| Syrup | Sugar : water by weight | °Brix |
|---|---|---|
| Light soaking syrup | 1 : 2 | 33 |
| Standard soaking syrup | 1 : 1.5 | 40 |
| Simple syrup | 1 : 1 | 50 |
| Traditional "30° Baumé" syrup | 1.26 : 1 | ~56 |
| Heavy syrup | 2 : 1 | 67 |

Soaking syrups for sponges are usually **30–50 °Brix**: lighter for a génoise you want moist and delicate, heavier when you want sweetness and some preservation. The 30° Baumé syrup appears in older French formulas; Baumé is a density scale read with a hydrometer, and 30 °Bé corresponds to a density of about 1.26 g/ml, or about 56 °Brix.

**Making a syrup to a target °Brix.** Sugar = target fraction × total weight. For 500 g at 40 °Brix: 200 g sugar + 300 g water.

**Diluting.** Sugar is conserved. To take 300 g of 50 °Brix syrup down to 30 °Brix: sugar = 150 g; total needed = 150 ÷ 0.30 = 500 g; add **200 g water**.

**Measuring.** A **refractometer** reads the refractive index of a drop of syrup and displays °Brix. Handheld units cover ranges such as 0–32, 28–62 or 58–90 °Brix, and give reliable readings on syrups up to about 85 °Brix. Above that, and for any boiling syrup, use temperature. Cool the drop to room temperature first (most units compensate for temperature between about 10 and 30 °C, labelled ATC) and wipe the prism clean between readings. A refractometer reads all dissolved solids, not just sucrose: a syrup with liqueur, glucose or fruit juice gives a reading that is useful for consistency but not an exact sucrose percentage. Alcohol in particular shifts the reading, so measure a flavoured syrup before adding the spirit, or compare only like with like.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Kitchens that make entremets in volume specify soaking syrups in °Brix and check each batch with a refractometer, because an eye cannot tell 35 from 45 °Brix and the difference shows in sweetness and in how wet the sponge becomes. A refractometer is on the *useful* list of the [equipment curriculum](equipment/useful.md); if you continue to Stage 2, buy one.

</div>

## Crystallization: why a clear syrup turns to sand

At 20 °C water can hold about 2 g of sucrose per gram of water: a saturated solution is about **67 % sugar**. Hot water holds much more (about 74 % at 60 °C and 83 % at 100 °C). A syrup cooked to 118 °C is about 87 % sugar. As soon as it cools it holds far more sugar than it "should": it is **supersaturated**, and it would like to shed the excess as crystals ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)).

| Temperature | Sucrose solubility (g per 100 g water) | Saturated syrup, % sugar |
|---|---|---|
| 20 °C | ~204 | ~67 |
| 40 °C | ~238 | ~70 |
| 60 °C | ~287 | ~74 |
| 80 °C | ~362 | ~78 |
| 100 °C | ~487 | ~83 |

Crystallization needs two steps. **Nucleation**: a few molecules must arrange themselves into a stable seed crystal, which is hard to do from scratch. **Growth**: once a seed exists, sucrose molecules add to it easily. A supersaturated syrup can stay clear for a long time if nothing starts nucleation. Once anything does, crystals grow and set off more crystals in a chain reaction; a clear syrup can turn into a solid, sandy mass in a minute.

Things that start nucleation:

- **Undissolved crystals**, including sugar that splashed onto the pan wall above the syrup, dried and later fell back in.
- **Stirring or agitation** of a supersaturated syrup, especially when it is hot.
- **Foreign particles**: dust, a spoon that has been in the sugar bag, a scratch in the pan.
- **Deliberate seeds**: a pinch of sugar, a spoonful of finished fondant.

### Preventing crystallization

| Technique | How | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolve completely before boiling | Heat gently, stirring, until the syrup is clear and no grains grate under the spoon | No undissolved crystals to act as seeds |
| Stop stirring once it boils | Swirl the pan instead, if you must | Agitation nucleates crystals |
| Wash down the sides | A pastry brush dipped in water, run around the pan wall at the syrup line | Dissolves splashes before they dry into seeds |
| Cover for 1–2 minutes at the boil | A lid on the pan once it boils | Condensing steam runs down the walls and washes them |
| Add glucose syrup | Typically 10–20 % of the sugar weight | Glucose, maltose and longer chains get in the way of sucrose molecules joining a crystal, and raise viscosity |
| Add invert sugar | About 5–10 % of the sugar weight | Glucose and fructose interfere in the same way |
| Add a little acid | Cream of tartar about 0.1–0.2 % of the sugar, or a few drops of lemon juice | Acid inverts some sucrose into glucose and fructose during boiling; the amount made depends on time and heat, so it is less predictable |
| Leave it alone while it cools | Cool undisturbed, on a rack, without stirring or scraping | Avoids nucleation while supersaturation is highest |

These glucose, invert and acid additions are called **interfering agents** or "doctors". Each has a cost. Glucose adds bulk and a slightly less sweet, more chewy texture. Invert sugar and acid-inverted sugar contain fructose, which browns earlier and attracts water, so sugar glass made with acid turns yellow sooner and goes sticky faster in humid air. For clear hard-crack sugar, glucose is the usual choice.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** A cooked syrup crystallizes when it is supersaturated **and** something starts nucleation. You control the first with temperature and interfering agents, and the second with cleanliness and by not agitating it. Every sugar technique in this module is one of those two controls.

</div>

### Crystallizing on purpose

Sometimes you want crystals, but many tiny ones. **Fondant** is sugar syrup cooked to soft ball (about 114–116 °C), cooled undisturbed to about 40–50 °C, and then worked hard with a spatula or a mixer. At that low temperature the syrup is highly supersaturated and viscous: agitation creates huge numbers of nuclei at once, and each can grow only a little before the sugar runs out. The result is a mass of crystals mostly too small for the tongue to feel (below roughly 20–30 µm) in a thin layer of syrup: white, smooth and creamy. Stir the same syrup while it is still hot and the few nuclei that form grow large: the result is grainy, like bad fudge.

**Seeding** is the other deliberate route: add crystals to a supersaturated syrup and it crystallizes around them. Confectioners seed fondant with a spoonful of old fondant to speed it up; sugared nuts (*sablage* in praline making) are made by stirring nuts in a 115–120 °C syrup until the sugar crystallizes into a sandy coat. The same principle, with cocoa butter instead of sucrose, is chocolate tempering in [16.3](stage-1/module-16/lesson-03.md).

### Glass: the other end

Cook sucrose to hard crack and cool it quickly, and the molecules have neither the water nor the time to arrange into crystals. They freeze in a disordered state: an **amorphous glass**, clear and brittle, the structure of a lollipop or sugar glass. A glass is stable only while it stays dry. It absorbs water from humid air, which makes it sticky at the surface and, in time, lets it crystallize and turn cloudy. Store sugar work airtight with a desiccant. The same logic explains why a crisp meringue softens in humid weather in [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Syrup at 118 °C burns like caramel, and hard-crack sugar at 150 °C is hotter than frying oil and sticks to skin. Clear the stove area of children and pets and of anything you might knock. Use a heavy pan filled no more than one-third, and keep the handle turned in. Keep a bowl of iced water within reach: if syrup lands on skin, plunge it in at once, then cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not peel off set sugar. Get medical help for any burn larger than a coin, on the face or hands, or that blisters. Never taste syrup from the spoon, and never put a hot sugar pan in the sink with water (it spits). Cover bare arms, and wear closed shoes.

</div>

## On the bench

1. **Calibrate your thermometer** in boiling water in the pan you will use. Record the reading and your correction.
2. **Make the four syrups in [R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md):** a 1:1 simple syrup, a 40 °Brix soaking syrup, a 118 °C syrup, and a hard-crack sugar glass. For the 118 °C syrup, take a cold-water test at 110, 114 and 118 °C, and note how each ball feels.
3. **Time the temperature climb** of the 118 °C syrup: record the temperature every minute from the boil. Plot it. The curve should flatten early and steepen late.
4. **Weigh the 118 °C syrup** in its tared pan when done and compare with the predicted weight (sugar ÷ 0.87).
5. **Run [X1-36](experiments/X1-36-sugar-crystallization.md):** four small syrups cooked to 118 °C with and without interfering agents and seeds, each split into a stirred and an undisturbed half.

## What goes wrong

- **Syrup crystallizes in the pan while boiling.** Sugar on the walls, undissolved sugar at the start, or stirring after the boil. Wash down the walls and add 10 % glucose next time. See [syrup crystallized in the pan](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=syrup-crystallized-in-the-pan).
- **The syrup reads right but behaves wrong.** A meringue that is runny from a "118 °C" syrup usually means the thermometer read high, or the probe was in the foam, not the syrup. See [syrup temperature wrong or unreliable](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=syrup-temperature-wrong-or-unreliable).
- **Hard-crack sugar turns yellow before 150 °C.** Acid or invert sugar in the syrup, or a dirty pan; use glucose only and a clean pan.
- **Sugar glass goes sticky within hours.** Humidity is being absorbed by the glass. Store airtight with desiccant and make it on a dry day.

## Lab notebook

Record for each syrup: sugar, water and any interfering agent in grams; pan diameter and material; heat setting; your thermometer correction; time from boil to target; target and actual endpoint temperature; finished weight and calculated concentration; cold-water test result; whether it crystallized and when; refractometer reading if you have one.

## Check yourself

1. A recipe written in Denver (about 1,600 m) says to cook a syrup to 113 °C. You are at sea level. What do you cook to, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 118 °C. At 1,600 m water boils at roughly 94.5–95 °C, about 5 °C lower than at sea level, and every stage shifts down by the same amount. The recipe's 113 °C is the Denver temperature for firm ball; at sea level firm ball is about 118 °C. The concentration is the target; the temperature that marks it depends on air pressure.

</details>

2. You boil 300 g sugar with 100 g water to 121 °C (about 90 % sugar). How much does the finished syrup weigh, and how much water did you boil off?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

300 ÷ 0.90 = 333 g of syrup, containing 33 g water. You boiled off 100 − 33 = about 67 g of water.

</details>

3. You have 400 g of 1:1 simple syrup and need a 35 °Brix soaking syrup. How much water do you add?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sugar = 400 × 0.50 = 200 g. Total for 35 °Brix = 200 ÷ 0.35 = 571 g. Add 571 − 400 = **171 g water**.

</details>

4. Your 118 °C syrup keeps crystallizing in the pan at about 112 °C. List, in the order you would try them, three changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Dissolve all the sugar before the syrup boils, then stop stirring. (2) Wash down the pan walls with a wet brush, or cover the pan for 1–2 minutes at the boil to steam them. (3) Replace 10–15 % of the sugar with glucose syrup as an interfering agent. Also check the sugar and pan are clean.

</details>

5. Why does fondant beaten at 45 °C come out smooth, while the same syrup stirred at 100 °C turns grainy?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 45 °C the syrup is highly supersaturated and viscous, so beating creates a very large number of nuclei at once and each crystal can grow only a little: many tiny crystals, smooth to the tongue. At 100 °C supersaturation is low and molecules move easily, so stirring makes few nuclei and they grow large: coarse, grainy crystals.

</details>
