# M13 · Sugar syrups, meringues and buttercreams

## Purpose

This module turns sugar and egg white into controlled materials. You start with sugar syrups: how the boiling point measures concentration, the stages from thread to hard crack, °Brix for uncooked syrups, and how to keep a supersaturated syrup clear or make it crystallize on purpose. You then use sugar and heat to build the three classical meringues, French, Swiss and Italian, and learn why they rank the way they do for volume and stability, and how to dry a meringue crisp and white. Finally you beat butter into meringue and pastry cream to make the buttercream family, where one variable, temperature, decides between silky, curdled and soupy. Everything here is measured with a thermometer, a scale and specific gravity, and all of it is used again in the assembled cakes and tarts of M17.

## Objectives

By the end of this module you can:

- Cook a syrup to a target stage, correcting for thermometer error and altitude, and confirm it with the cold-water test.
- Specify, make and dilute uncooked syrups by °Brix, and calculate syrup weights and water lost to evaporation.
- Prevent unwanted crystallization and explain how fondant and seeding use crystallization deliberately.
- Make French, Swiss and Italian meringue to measured endpoints, choose among them for a given use, and bake meringues dry, white and uncracked.
- Make Swiss and Italian meringue buttercream and crème mousseline at controlled temperatures, and rescue a curdled or soupy buttercream.
- Work safely with hot sugar and apply the egg-safety rules to each meringue.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Boiling-point elevation | The rise in a solution's boiling point caused by dissolved solutes; in a sugar syrup it lets the boiling temperature serve as a measure of concentration. |
| Sugar stages | Named concentration ranges of cooked syrup, from thread (106–112 °C) through soft ball, firm ball, hard ball, soft crack and hard crack (146–154 °C) to caramel (160 °C+), at sea level. |
| Cold-water test | Dropping syrup into iced water and judging the stage by the texture of what forms; a check on the thermometer. |
| °Brix | Grams of sucrose (strictly, dissolved solids) per 100 g of solution; a 1:1 simple syrup is 50 °Brix. |
| Refractometer | An instrument that reads °Brix from the refractive index of a drop of syrup; reliable up to about 85 °Brix. |
| Supersaturation | A solution holding more dissolved sugar than it can at equilibrium at that temperature; every cooked syrup is supersaturated once it cools. |
| Nucleation | The formation of the first stable seed crystals; the difficult step that agitation, undissolved grains and dust trigger. |
| Interfering agent | A substance that hinders sucrose crystallization, such as glucose syrup, invert sugar, or acid (which inverts sucrose during boiling). |
| Seeding | Adding crystals to a supersaturated syrup (or melted cocoa butter) to start crystallization deliberately. |
| Fondant | Sugar syrup cooked to soft ball, cooled undisturbed to about 40–50 °C and then worked so it forms a mass of tiny crystals: white, smooth and creamy. |
| Amorphous sugar glass | Non-crystalline solid sugar formed when a hard-crack syrup cools too quickly to crystallize; clear and brittle, sticky when it absorbs moisture. |
| French meringue | Whites whipped with raw sugar added after soft peak; lightest and least stable. |
| Swiss meringue | Whites and sugar heated over a bain-marie to 60–70 °C (71 °C for pasteurization), then whipped to cool; dense, glossy and stable. |
| Italian meringue | Whites whipped with a syrup cooked to 118–121 °C; fine, firm and the most stable. |
| Weeping | Syrup leaking from a meringue, usually from undissolved sugar, standing too long, or an unset underside. |
| Beading | Small amber droplets on a baked meringue surface, usually from overbaking or undissolved sugar. |
| Pâte à bombe | Egg yolks whipped with a syrup at 118–121 °C; the base of French buttercream and many mousses. |
| Meringue buttercream | Butter beaten into Swiss or Italian meringue to form a fat-continuous cream holding syrup and air; works at 19–22 °C. |
| Crème mousseline | Crème pâtissière enriched with butter in two stages: half into the hot cream, half beaten into the cooled cream. |
