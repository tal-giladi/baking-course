## Why this lesson exists

Danish pastry adds two new problems to lamination: a richer dough that is softer and warms faster, and **fillings**, which put water and weight on top of a structure that still has to rise and bake through. It is also the last laminated product of the module, so it is where you consolidate: by now you have laminated three doughs, and you have (almost certainly) produced some faults. The second half of this lesson turns those faults into a method. A laminated product records its whole history, from mixing to baking, in its cross-section, its tray and its colour. Learning to read that record is the skill that separates following a recipe from running a process.

## You will be able to

- Compare puff, croissant and Danish doughs by formula, layers and structure, and explain what each difference does.
- Combine laminated dough with fillings: choose bake-stable fillings, decide when to fill, and make sure the base under a filling bakes through.
- Shape pinwheels, envelopes, combs and snails, and finish with apricot nappage.
- Diagnose lamination faults by locating them in the process timeline, using the cross-section, the tray and your log as evidence.

## Three laminated doughs compared

| | Puff ([R1-33](recipes/R1-33-puff-pastry.md)) | Croissant ([R1-34](recipes/R1-34-croissant.md)) | Danish ([R1-35](recipes/R1-35-danish.md)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leavening | Steam only | Yeast + steam | Yeast + steam |
| Détrempe liquid | Water 50 % | Water + milk 56 % | Milk 40 % + egg 20 % |
| Sugar | 0 | 11 % | 12 % |
| Butter in détrempe | 12.5 % | 6 % | 8 % |
| Beurrage (% of flour) | 75 % | 55 % | 60 % |
| Turns → fat layers | 6S → 729 | 1D + 1S → 12 | 3S → 27 |
| Final sheet | 2.5–5 mm | 4 mm | 4 mm |
| Proof | None | 2–2.5× at 24–27 °C | 1.5–2× at 24–27 °C |
| Structure | Many fine, crisp, dry leaves | Open honeycomb, crisp shell | Finer, more tender flakes, softer crumb |
| Bake | 210–220 °C then 180–190 °C | 190 °C fan | 190 °C fan / 200 °C conventional |

Read across a row and you can predict the product. Egg in the Danish détrempe tenderizes (fat and emulsifiers from the yolk) and softens the dough, so it warms faster during lamination and needs a rest after every turn. More layers than croissant give thinner dough leaves and a finer, more tender, less honeycombed crumb. More beurrage adds flakiness and richness but more risk of leakage. Less proof keeps the dough strong enough to hold its shape under a filling.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** "Danish" covers a family of doughs, not one formula. Scandinavian bakeries often laminate a lean, cardamom-scented dough with a high butter ratio and few turns; American Danish is often sweeter and softer, sometimes with shortening; French *viennoiserie* shops frequently use croissant dough itself for Danish shapes. R1-35 is a middle-of-the-range educational version. What stays constant is the process logic.

</div>

## Laminated dough with fillings

A filling changes the physics of the pastry beneath it:

1. **It adds water.** Pastry cream is about 60–65 % water; fruit even more. That water must go somewhere as the pastry bakes: some evaporates from the top, some migrates into the dough below.
2. **It adds weight.** A 25 g blob of cream on an 11 cm square presses down on the layers under it; they rise less than the uncovered edges. This is desirable: it is what makes the well in a pinwheel.
3. **It shields the dough.** The dough under a filling is insulated from above, heats more slowly and is the last part of the pastry to bake through.

### Bake-stable fillings

A filling for baking must survive 15–20 minutes at 190–200 °C without running, boiling over or splitting.

| Filling | Stability in the oven | How to make it stable |
|---|---|---|
| Crème pâtissière | Moderate. The starch gel softens on reheating and loses water as steam; low-starch creams run and boil over | Raise the cornstarch from 9 % to about 11 % of the milk ([R1-35](recipes/R1-35-danish.md)); boil a full 2 minutes; use it cold and thick; pipe no more than 25 g on an 11 cm square |
| Crème d'amande / frangipane ([R1-23](recipes/R1-23-creme-damande.md)) | High. It is a batter that bakes into a cake-like set | Do not overcream; fill modestly (it rises by about a third) |
| Fresh fruit | Releases juice | Toss juicy fruit with sugar and 5 % cornstarch; drain canned fruit and pat dry |
| Jam | Boils and runs at high sugar | Use bake-stable jam (higher pectin and solids) or cook ordinary jam down; small amounts |
| Cheese filling (cream cheese, egg, sugar) | Moderate: egg sets it | Classic in Danish; a Stage 2 variant |

Professionals also use commercial **bake-stable creams** made with modified starches that do not thin on reheating; that is a Stage 2 ingredient topic.

### When to fill

- **Before the proof:** fillings that are rolled inside (snails) or enclosed (combs). The dough proofs around them.
- **After the proof, just before baking:** open fillings in wells (pinwheels, envelopes). A proofed pastry has a soft, airy centre; press the well again gently, then pipe. Filling before the proof lets the cream slide off as the dough grows, and heavy fillings suppress the rise of the centre too much.

### Baking the base

The base under a filling is the last part to bake. Two rules: **bake to a deep colour** (lift one pastry with a spatula and check that its base is golden, not pale), and **keep fruit dry**, because every gram of juice ends up in the base. If bases are consistently pale, bake on a preheated tray or on the lowest rack for the first half of the bake.

### Glazing

**Apricot nappage** is apricot jam thinned with about 20 % water, boiled and sieved, then brushed on the hot pastries. It gives shine, seals the surface of the fruit against drying, and adds a slight acidity that balances the sweet cream. Brush it **hot on hot pastries, thin**: a cool or thick glaze stays sticky. A water icing (icing sugar and 15–20 % water or lemon juice) drizzled on the cooled pastries is the other classic finish.

## Pain au chocolat: the geometry

Pain au chocolat uses croissant dough ([R1-34](recipes/R1-34-croissant.md)) cut into rectangles of about 8 × 15 cm (about 53 g at 4 mm), rolled around two bake-stable chocolate batons. Three details matter:

- **Seam down.** The seam is the end of the dough; placed underneath, the weight of the pastry holds it closed during the proof and bake.
- **Batons, not chopped chocolate.** Batons are formulated to soften without running; chopped couverture melts and leaks out of the ends and burns on the tray.
- **The same proof and bake as croissant.** The cross-section should show honeycomb around two islands of chocolate, with no dense, doughy ring around them (a doughy ring means the dough was pressed too tightly around the batons, or the piece was underproofed).

## The lamination clinic

A laminated product passes through a long chain of steps, and most faults are made hours before they are seen. The clinic method is the one from [05.4](stage-1/module-05/lesson-04.md), applied to this system: **collect evidence, locate the fault in the timeline, test the hypothesis, change one thing.**

### Step 1: collect the evidence

Before you guess, look at three things.

| Evidence | How | What it records |
|---|---|---|
| **The tray** | Photograph it straight after removing the pastries. Grease ring width (mm) or pooled fat weight (a pre-weighed foil square under a test piece; weigh after baking) | Butter that left the pastry: during the proof (warm) or the bake (thick layers, broken layers, underproof, cool oven) |
| **The cross-section** | Cut lengthwise with a serrated knife after 30 minutes; photograph with a ruler | Layer continuity (lamination), gas cells (proof), dryness of inner leaves (bake) |
| **The log** | Your temperatures and times | Where the process left its window |

### Step 2: locate the fault in the timeline

| Stage | What goes wrong there | Signature in the product |
|---|---|---|
| Détrempe mix | Overmixed; too little or too much water | Shrinkage, elasticity (over); tearing, sticky (soft) |
| Plaque and lock-in | Butter too cold; butter and dough mismatched | Shattered streaks; uneven layers; patches without butter |
| Turns | Block too warm; too many turns; flour not brushed off | Bready crumb, no layers (warm or too many); dry white streaks (flour) |
| Sheeting and cutting | Uneven thickness; unrelaxed; dragged cuts | Lopsided rise; shrinkage; sealed edges |
| Shaping | Pressing too hard | Dense spiral in the core |
| Proof | Too warm; too short; too long | Butter on tray before baking (warm); tight, burst, leaking (under); flat, collapsing (over) |
| Bake | Too cool; too hot; too short | Leakage and low rise (cool); dark outside, raw inside (hot); pale creases and collapse (short) |

### Step 3: the six common faults

**1. Butter leaking onto the tray.** The most common and the least specific symptom: it has four common causes, and the log and cross-section separate them.

| If you also see | Most likely stage |
|---|---|
| Butter on the tray *before* baking; bready crumb | Proof too warm (> 28 °C) |
| Tight, heavy pieces, split sides, dense core | Underproofed: tight layers squeeze butter out in the oven |
| Irregular thick fat pockets, shards in the cross-section | Butter too cold at lamination: broken layers let fat run |
| Good structure but oven temperature measured low | Oven too cool: fat runs before leaves set |

**2. Bready interior, no honeycomb.** The layers were lost. Lamination too warm (smeared butter), too many turns, too warm a proof, or a very long, warm lamination during which the dough fermented. Check block temperatures and the turn count first.

**3. Collapsed after the proof or after baking.** Overproofed (dough layers too stretched to hold), or underbaked (inner layers still wet; they collapse as steam condenses). The creases tell you: pale creases point to the bake; well-browned creases with a flat, spread shape point to the proof.

**4. Shattered butter streaks.** Butter was too cold (below about 10 °C) or much firmer than the dough during lock-in or a turn. Visible while rolling as plates of butter under the dough; baked as big holes next to fused, doughy areas. Common after a freezer rest.

**5. Uneven layers.** Thick and thin zones in the cross-section, lopsided rise. Uneven plaque, uneven rolling (no guides, sides not squared), thick seams from a poor lock-in, or inconsistent butter firmness across the plaque.

**6. Too dark outside, raw centre.** Oven too hot for the product size or sugar level, pieces too thick or heavy, or a dark tray. Measure the oven, check the weight of the pieces, and use the two-phase profile (lift, then dry) or lower the temperature by 10 °C and extend the time.

### Step 4: test and change one thing

A diagnosis is a hypothesis. Test it with the cheapest experiment that could disprove it: bake two pieces of the next batch under two conditions (proof at 25 °C vs 30 °C; oven at 190 vs 205 °C), keep everything else the same, and compare trays and cross-sections. [X1-40](experiments/X1-40-croissant-temperature.md) is exactly such a test. Then change **one** variable in the process and log the result. The full symptom tables are in the [laminated doughs troubleshooting database](troubleshooting/laminated.md).

## Clinic cases

Work these before opening the answers.

**Case 1.** Croissants, kitchen at 27 °C. Block temperatures were 17 °C and 19 °C after the two turns (no rests: "the butter was soft, so it rolled easily"). Proof 2.5 h at 26 °C to 2×. Result: good height, but the crumb is fine and even like brioche, and there is a moderate film of butter on the tray.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The fault is in the turns. At 17–19 °C the butter was above its plastic range and smeared into the dough as it was rolled thin, so many layers fused (bready crumb) and some fat was free to leak. The proof at 26 °C was within range and was not the cause. Correction: rest 20–30 minutes after each turn to keep the block at 10–15 °C, chill the bench and pin in a hot kitchen, and roll faster with fewer, even strokes.

</details>

**Case 2.** Danish pinwheels: the pastry cream spread over the points and burned onto the tray, and the bases were pale and soft. Cream made with R1-26 at 9 % cornstarch, piped at 40 g each before the proof; proofed to 2.5×; fresh plums used unthickened.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Four compounding causes: a standard pastry cream (9 %) thins on reheating; 40 g is too much for an 11 cm square; filling before a long proof let the cream slide as the dough grew; and overproofing (2.5× instead of 1.5–2×) left weak points that opened. The plums released juice into the base, which then could not bake crisp. Correction: 11 % cornstarch cream, 25 g piped after the proof into a re-pressed well, proof to 1.5–2×, plums tossed with sugar and 5 % cornstarch (or pre-cooked), bake until the bases are golden.

</details>

**Case 3.** Puff pastry squares rose unevenly (12 mm on two sides, 25 mm on the other two) and are slightly rectangular after baking.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Two faults visible on two axes: shrinkage in one direction (the sheet was rolled mostly in one direction and not rested after sheeting or cutting), and low rise on two sides (likely egg wash on those edges, or a dragged or dull cut; or the pieces were cut from the folded edges without trimming). Correction: roll in both directions, rest 30 minutes after cutting, trim all edges, cut straight down with a sharp blade, egg-wash only the top.

</details>

## What goes wrong

- **Filling boils over or runs.** Too little starch, too much filling, filled too early, overproofed. See [Danish filling boils over](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=danish-filling-boils-over).
- **Soggy, pale base.** Wet fruit, underbaked. See [Soggy base under the filling](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=soggy-base-under-the-filling).
- **Shapes open up or lose definition.** Overproofed, points not pressed firmly, dough too warm when shaped. See [Croissants collapse](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=croissants-collapse).
- **All the faults above for croissant and puff.** See the [index of the laminated doughs database](troubleshooting/laminated.md).

## Check yourself

1. Why is Danish dough proofed to 1.5–2× rather than the 2–2.5× used for croissant?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Danish pieces carry fillings and must keep sharp shapes (points, wells, teeth). A less expanded dough is stronger: it holds the weight of the filling and the pressed-in well, and the shapes do not open or spread. A fully proofed Danish loses definition and its fillings slide.

</details>

2. Your pastry cream has 45 g of cornstarch per 500 g of milk. How much do you use to make it bake-stable at 11 %, and why does it need more starch in the oven than in a cold éclair?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

11 % of 500 g = **55 g** (10 g more). In the oven the gel is reheated: it softens and thins as the starch network loosens, and loses water as steam. The extra starch gives a firmer gel that still holds its shape after heating and some evaporation. In an éclair the cream is piped cold into a baked shell and never reheated.

</details>

3. A tray of croissants shows a pool of butter under each piece; the cross-section shows a good honeycomb in the outer layers but split sides and a tight, dense centre. What is the most likely cause, and how do you test it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Underproofing. The layers were still tight when the oven heat arrived; they expanded violently, splitting the sides and squeezing butter out. Test: next batch, bake half at the same proof time and half after a longer proof (to 2–2.5× with jiggle), everything else equal, and compare trays and cross-sections.

</details>

4. Name the three pieces of evidence you collect before diagnosing a lamination fault, and what each one records.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The tray (butter that left the pastry, and when: before or during baking), the cross-section (layer continuity, gas cells, dryness of inner leaves), and the log (the temperatures and times that show where the process left its window).

</details>

5. Why is butter leakage, on its own, a poor basis for a diagnosis?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It has at least four common causes at different stages: too warm a proof, underproofing, broken layers from butter that was too cold at lamination, and a cool oven (plus thick layers from too few turns). Only the other evidence (cross-section, timing of the leak, log) separates them.

</details>
