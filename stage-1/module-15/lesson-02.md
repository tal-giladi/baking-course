## Why this lesson exists

Puff pastry is lamination in its purest form: no yeast, no chemical leavener, no sugar, almost no egg. Everything it does in the oven comes from layers and steam, so it is the cleanest system in which to learn the physical skills of lamination (plaque, lock-in, turns, sheeting, cutting) and to see the effect of each one. It is also one of the most useful doughs in the pastry kitchen: the same block becomes cheese straws, palmiers, vol-au-vent, tart bases, mille-feuille and galette des rois. The blitz method gives you most of that in half the time, and understanding exactly what it gives up is a lesson in its own right.

## You will be able to

- Explain the role of each ingredient in a classic puff détrempe and the ranges professionals use.
- Lock in a butter plaque with the envelope method and give six single turns (or four doubles) while keeping the block at 10–15 °C.
- Sheet, cut and bake puff pastry so that it rises straight and evenly, and explain the edge rules (sharp blade, straight down, no wash on the sides).
- Make blitz puff pastry and predict how its rise and structure differ from classic.
- Choose the method and sheet thickness for a given product.

## Three ways to make puff pastry

| Method | How the fat is incorporated | Layers | Rise | Time | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Classic** (*feuilletage classique*) | One plaque locked in the détrempe; 6 single turns (or 4 doubles) | Defined: 729 (or 256) nominal fat layers | Highest, most even | 3–4 h active, often over 2 days | Vol-au-vent, bouchées, pithivier, mille-feuille, anything that must rise straight |
| **Blitz / rough puff** (*feuilletage rapide*) | Cold butter chunks mixed into the flour with water; folds flatten them into streaks | Undefined: overlapping, discontinuous streaks | 60–80 % of classic, less even | About 1.5–2 h | Straws, palmiers, pie tops, tart bases, sausage rolls |
| **Inverted** (*feuilletage inversé*) | Butter kneaded with a little flour forms the *outside*; the détrempe is locked inside it | Defined | Very high, very tender and delicate | Longest; needs very tight temperature control | Fine pâtisserie (Stage 2) |

This course makes the classic and blitz. Inverted puff is listed here because you will meet it in any professional pâtisserie; it depends on handling a sticky butter-flour paste as the outer layer, which is a Stage 2 skill.

## The classic détrempe: what each ingredient does

The [R1-33](recipes/R1-33-puff-pastry.md) détrempe is flour 100, water 50, salt 2, butter 12.5, with a beurrage of 75 % of the flour.

| Ingredient | Range in professional practice | Role | Moving within the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100; plain/all-purpose 10–11.5 % protein, or a bread/pastry blend | Gluten sheet that can be rolled thin without tearing; starch that sets the leaves | Stronger flour: tears less, sheets thinner, but shrinks more and needs longer rests. Weaker flour: more tender, tears more easily in the late turns |
| Water | 45–55 % | Hydrates the flour; becomes steam | More water: softer, easier to roll and match to soft butter, more steam, but stickier and shrinks more. Less: stiffer, harder to roll, can crack |
| Salt | 1.5–2 % | Flavour; tightens gluten slightly | Salted beurrage: reduce détrempe salt |
| Butter in détrempe | 0–15 % (sometimes up to 20 %) | Shortens gluten: extensibility and tenderness; softens the dough towards the plaque's consistency | More: more tender, less shrinkage, slightly less lift. None: tough, springy détrempe (the old "lean" style) |
| Beurrage | 50–100 % of flour (often 70–80 %) | The layers | See [Variables in R1-33](recipes/R1-33-puff-pastry.md) |
| Vinegar or lemon juice | 0–1 % (traditional) | Said to relax gluten | Effect small at this level; rest time does far more |

The détrempe is **short-mixed**: mixed only until it holds together, rough and slightly shaggy, then rested cold (at least an hour, ideally overnight). Six turns will stretch and develop the gluten a great deal; if you start with a developed dough, it will fight you by turn four and shrink badly after cutting.

## Lock-in methods

Two lock-ins are standard; this course uses the envelope for puff and the "open" (butt-joint) lock for croissant, because both give one fat layer and keep the layer arithmetic of [15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md) simple.

| Lock-in | How | Fat layers after lock-in |
|---|---|---|
| **Envelope** (French, diamond) | Butter square set at 45° on a dough square whose side ≈ the butter's diagonal; dough corners folded to the centre | 1 |
| **Open / butt joint** | Butter on the centre of a rectangle twice its length; both ends folded to meet in the middle | 1 |
| **English** | Butter covers two-thirds of the dough rectangle; folded like a letter | 2 |

The geometry of the envelope sets the dough size. For a 20 × 20 cm plaque, the diagonal is 20 × √2 ≈ 28.3 cm, so the dough square must be about **29 cm** on a side for its corners to meet in the centre with a little overlap. The four folded corners then cover a 20.5 cm square: the butter is enclosed exactly. If the dough is smaller, the corners do not meet and butter is exposed; if much larger, thick seams of dough form in the centre and at the corners, and those spots have more dough than butter all the way through the turns.

## The turn schedule

Classic schedules for puff pastry:

| Schedule | Fat layers | Rests | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 singles, in pairs | 729 | 30 min after turns 2 and 4, ≥ 1 h after 6 | The classical French schedule |
| 4 doubles | 256 | 30 min after each | Faster; rise in practice similar |
| 2 singles + 2 doubles | 9 × 16 = 144 | 30 min between pairs | Used in some kitchens for a slightly coarser, taller leaf |
| 5 singles | 243 | | Some kitchens for vol-au-vent (taller, slightly less even) |

The block should be at **10–15 °C** whenever it is rolled. Measure it, especially the first few times: your hands and the kitchen add heat faster than you expect. A 20 × 20 × 2.5 cm block of about 960 g loses and gains heat slowly through its thickness, which is why the rest is 30 minutes and not 5.

Why pairs? The first turn of a pair warms the block and stretches the gluten a little; the second turn still usually fits inside the temperature window. Three turns in a row by hand usually does not: the butter is too soft by the third, and the gluten is too tight.

## Sheeting and cutting: the edge rules

Puff pastry rises **from its cut edges**. Each leaf is anchored at the edges; if the edges are open, the leaves can separate over their whole width. Anything that sticks leaves together at an edge pins the rise on that side:

1. **Sharp blade, straight down.** A dragged or sawing knife pulls the top layers down over the cut face and presses them together. A dull cutter crushes. Use a thin, sharp knife or a sharp cutter and press straight down; lift straight up.
2. **Trim every folded edge.** The fold at the edge of a turned block is a continuous loop of dough with no open layers. Trim 5 mm from every side of the final sheet.
3. **No egg wash on the sides.** Wash runs down the edge and glues the layers; that side bakes lower and the piece tips over. Brush only the top, and keep the brush away from the rim.
4. **Rest cut pieces cold (30 minutes).** Sheeting stretches the gluten. If it has not relaxed, the piece shrinks in the direction it was last rolled, turning squares into rectangles and circles into ovals, and it rises lopsided. Rolling in both directions and resting after sheeting (and again after cutting) is the correction.
5. **Invert rounds.** A cutter compresses the top edge slightly more than the bottom; flipping the piece puts the cleaner edge on top.

## Baking: hot, then dry

The oven profile has two jobs: **lift** (steam production while the leaves set) and **drying** (removing the remaining water from the inner leaves).

- **Lift phase: 210–220 °C (410–430 °F), first 12–15 minutes.** A preheated tray helps. Do not open the door: a pastry that has risen but not set will fall.
- **Drying phase: 180–190 °C (355–375 °F), another 8–15 minutes**, until the sides and base are deep golden and the piece feels light. An underbaked piece looks perfect for five minutes out of the oven, then sinks as its inner leaves, still soft with water, condense and collapse. Break one piece open to check: the centre leaves must be dry and crisp, not translucent.

**Docking** is the opposite choice: fork holes vent steam so that a sheet bakes flat and even (tart bases, the leaves of a mille-feuille). A rack or tray on top (about 30 minutes of the bake, then removed to colour) gives a very thin, dense, crisp sheet.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Why does egg wash colour so much faster than plain puff? The pastry itself contains almost no sugar, and its proteins are gluten; browning relies on Maillard reactions between the little reducing sugar in flour and the proteins at the surface ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)). Egg wash adds protein, fat and a thin continuous film that dries fast, so browning starts sooner and goes deeper. Unwashed puff browns more slowly and more matt, which is why bakers pull it later than they would a washed piece.

</div>

## The blitz method: layers without a plaque

In blitz puff, 2 cm cold cubes of butter are tossed in flour and bound with ice water so the cubes stay whole. The first roll flattens each cube into a thin plate; every fold stacks the plates and rolls them thinner and wider, so that after four single turns the dough contains long, thin, overlapping streaks of butter. It is the same principle as the flaky pie dough of [11.3](stage-1/module-11/lesson-03.md) taken four folds further.

What you gain: speed (no plaque, no lock-in, less resting), and robustness (small breaks in the butter do not matter because the layers were never continuous).

What you give up:

- **Continuity.** Streaks overlap but do not cover the whole sheet. Where there is no butter, two leaves fuse; where there are two streaks, the leaf is thick. The rise is lower (typically 60–80 % of classic) and less even.
- **Straight sides.** Irregular layers rise irregularly. Blitz squares tilt and bulge more; blitz is poor for vol-au-vent.
- **Toughness.** More of the flour is not coated by fat, so the détrempe develops more gluten: rest cut pieces a full 30 minutes to control shrinkage.

For straws, palmiers, sausage rolls, pie tops and tart bases, the difference is small and blitz is the rational choice in production.

## What to make with it

| Product | Why puff | Critical point |
|---|---|---|
| Cheese straws | Showcase for shattering leaves; uses scraps | Twist tightly, bake until fully golden |
| Palmiers | Sugar turns caramelize between leaves | Sugar burns fast after 10 min; watch colour |
| Bouchées and vol-au-vent | Tall, straight cases to fill with a savoury or sweet filling | Even sheet, sharp cuts, cold rested pieces, full drying bake |
| Tart bases, mille-feuille | Crisp, flat sheets | Docking and weighting ([M17](stage-1/module-17/README.md), Stage 2) |
| Pithivier, galette des rois | Almond cream enclosed in two discs | Seal, score, vent: a preview, made in Stage 2 with [R1-23](recipes/R1-23-creme-damande.md) |

## Storage and planning

The finished block keeps **2 days** refrigerated or **1 month** frozen, wrapped airtight. Thaw frozen blocks overnight in the refrigerator, never at room temperature: the outside softens while the core is still frozen, and the first roll smears the outside and shatters the core. Cut, unbaked pieces also freeze well and can be baked from frozen with 3–5 minutes more. This is why professional kitchens make puff pastry in large batches once a week ([18.1](stage-1/module-18/lesson-01.md)).

2. **Blitz.** Make the ×0.5 blitz and bake six squares under the same conditions. Compare height, evenness (difference between highest and lowest edge) and cross-section.

## What goes wrong

- **Pieces tip over or rise higher on one side.** Egg wash on an edge, dragged cut, uneven sheet, or unrested pieces. See [Uneven rise, lopsided puff](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=uneven-rise-lopsided-puff).
- **Pieces shrink into ovals.** Unrelaxed gluten from sheeting in one direction. See [Dough shrinks after rolling or cutting](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=dough-shrinks-after-rolling-or-cutting).
- **Butter pools out in the oven.** Oven too cool, butter warm at loading, or thick fat layers. See [Butter leaking during baking](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=butter-leaking-during-baking).
- **Beautiful from outside, doughy inside, sinks after 5 minutes.** Not dried. See [Raw or doughy centre](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=raw-or-doughy-centre).

## Check yourself

1. Your plaque is 18 × 18 cm. What size must the détrempe square be for an envelope lock-in?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The dough side ≈ the butter's diagonal: 18 × √2 ≈ 25.5 cm, so roll the détrempe to about **26 × 26 cm**, slightly thicker in the centre.

</details>

2. You want a classic puff with 50 % less time spent resting. Which schedule do you choose, how many fat layers does it give, and what is the trade-off?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Four double turns with a 30 minute rest after each (or two pairs of doubles if the block stays cold enough): 4⁴ = **256** fat layers instead of 729. Fewer, thicker layers; in practice the rise is similar, slightly less fine and even. Gluten is stretched more per turn, so the rests still matter.

</details>

3. A batch of vol-au-vent rose 30 mm on one side and 15 mm on the other, and they leaned. Give three causes to check.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Any three of: egg wash ran down the low side; the cutter or knife dragged or was dull on that side; the sheet was thicker on one side (uneven rolling, no guides); pieces were not rested after cutting and shrank in the last rolling direction; the pieces were cut near a folded edge that was not trimmed; uneven oven heat (one side of the tray near a hot wall).

</details>

4. Why does blitz puff rise less than classic, even with the same amount of butter?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Its butter is in discontinuous, overlapping streaks rather than continuous sheets. Where there is no butter between two dough leaves they fuse; where streaks overlap there is a thick layer. Fewer effective barriers trap less steam, and the uneven structure rises unevenly. More flour is uncoated, so more gluten forms: more shrinkage too.

</details>

5. Squares look fully golden at 16 minutes but sink slightly as they cool, and the inside is pale and gummy. What happened and what is the correction?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The lift phase was complete but the drying phase was skipped: inner leaves still contained water, and when the steam condensed on cooling they collapsed. Lower to 180–190 °C after the rise and bake another 8–15 minutes until the pieces are deep golden on the sides and base and the inside is dry and crisp. Tent with foil if the top is getting too dark.

</details>
