## Why this lesson exists

A croissant is where two systems you already know collide: the fermented, lightly enriched dough of [M06](stage-1/module-06/README.md) and the butter lamination of [15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)–[15.2](stage-1/module-15/lesson-02.md). Each system has its own temperature logic, and they conflict. Yeast wants warmth; laminated butter must stay below the point where it softens. The whole croissant process is a schedule designed to satisfy both: ferment when the butter is not in the dough, laminate cold when the yeast is nearly asleep, proof in the narrow window where both are acceptable. Once you can see the process that way, every step of the recipe has a reason and every failure has a place in the timeline.

## You will be able to

- Explain the croissant formula ingredient by ingredient and state the ranges professionals use.
- Plan a two-day croissant schedule and explain where fermentation is allowed to happen and where it is suppressed.
- Laminate with 1 double + 1 single turn and sheet by hand to 4 mm at a controlled temperature.
- Cut, shape and proof croissants and pains au chocolat, and judge the proof endpoint by volume, jiggle and layer definition.
- Read a croissant cross-section and attribute what you see to lamination, proof or bake.

## The croissant formula

| Ingredient | [R1-34](recipes/R1-34-croissant.md) | Professional range | Role and trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100 (50:50 bread + all-purpose, ≈ 11.5–12 % protein) | 11–13 % protein | Needs enough gluten to hold gas in thin layers and sheet to 3.5–4 mm without tearing, but not so much that it shrinks and fights the pin |
| Water + milk | 28 + 28 = 56 | 50–60 total | Milk adds lactose (browning; yeast cannot ferment it), milk fat and proteins (tenderness, colour). More liquid: softer dough, easier to match butter, stickier when warm |
| Sugar | 11 | 8–14 | Sweetness, browning, tenderness; slows yeast osmotically above ~10 % |
| Salt | 2 | 1.8–2.2 | Flavour; tightens gluten; slows yeast slightly |
| Yeast (instant) | 1.2 | 1–1.5 (fresh 3–4.5) | Enough to proof in 2–3 h after cold retards |
| Butter in détrempe | 6 | 3–10 | Shortens gluten (extensibility), softens dough towards butter consistency |
| Beurrage | 55 | 45–60 (sometimes up to 70) | The layers: 24 % of the laminated dough here; professional doughs often 25–30 % |

Two numbers are worth computing every time ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)):

- **True hydration:** water + 87 % of the milk + 16 % of the détrempe butter = 140 + 122 + 5 = 267 g → **53 %** of flour. A firm dough by bread standards, as it must be: a soft dough cannot hold thin layers and becomes sticky at lamination temperature.
- **Sugar in the water phase:** 55 ÷ (55 + 267) ≈ **17 %**. Above about 10–12 %, standard yeast is noticeably slowed; osmotolerant yeast is the professional choice ([X1-15](experiments/X1-15-sugar-in-dough.md)).

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Many bakeries add a **preferment** to croissant dough, typically 10–30 % of the flour as pâte fermentée (old croissant or bread dough) or poolish. It adds flavour and extensibility, and lets the baker reduce the fresh yeast. You will meet this in Stage 2; R1-34 uses the overnight retard of the détrempe to do a similar job more simply.

</div>

## Mixing: why moderate development

The détrempe is mixed to **moderate development**: cohesive and fairly smooth, but it tears before it forms a translucent windowpane. That is less than a lean bread (full development) and much less than brioche (intensive development, [06.4](stage-1/module-06/lesson-04.md)).

The reason is that mixing is not the only gluten development the dough will get. Every turn and the final sheeting stretch the gluten sheet out to many times its area, aligning and further developing the network ([SC-12](stage-1/science/sc-12-rheology.md)). A dough fully developed in the mixer becomes too elastic by the final sheet: it springs back, shrinks after cutting (triangles get shorter and fatter), and resists rolling, so you press harder, which warms and smears the butter. An underdeveloped dough, on the other hand, tears during sheeting and exposes the butter, and holds gas poorly in the proof.

## The two temperature limits

| Process | What it wants | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Yeast fermentation ([SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)) | Fastest around 30–35 °C; slow below 10 °C; nearly stopped at 3–5 °C | — |
| Butter layers ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)) | Plastic at 13–16 °C; softens above ~20 °C; oils out approaching 28–30 °C, fully melted ~35 °C | Must not smear during lamination; must not melt during proof |

So the croissant schedule separates them:

| Stage | Temperature | Yeast | Butter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mix détrempe | DDT 22–24 °C | Starts | Not yet in the dough |
| Short rest | Room, 30 min | Active | — |
| Retard détrempe | 3–5 °C, 8–16 h | Very slow: flavour, a little gas | — |
| Lock-in, turns, sheeting | Block 10–15 °C, rests at 3–5 °C | Nearly asleep (the goal) | Plastic |
| Shaping | Dough cold (≤ 12–14 °C) | Slow | Plastic |
| **Proof** | **24–27 °C, humid** | Active: the dough layers inflate | Still solid but softening: this is the upper limit |
| Bake | Oven | Final burst, then killed ~55–60 °C | Melts, steam, fries layers |

The **proof ceiling of about 27–28 °C** is set by the butter, not the yeast. Yeast would work faster at 32 °C, and a bread baker would happily proof there. In a croissant at 30–32 °C, the butter layers soften so much that they begin to merge with the dough and seep out of the edges: you see butter pooling on the tray before the croissants even enter the oven. In the bake, those layers are gone: less lift, more leakage, a bready crumb. [X1-40](experiments/X1-40-croissant-temperature.md) measures exactly this.

The **proof floor** is practical: below about 22 °C the proof takes 4–6 hours, the surface dries, and the process runs into your day. Humidity (75–85 % relative humidity) keeps the surface supple so it can expand without cracking; a dry skin gives a tight, cracked, dull crust. Too wet (water droplets on the dough) and the egg wash won't stick and the surface goes blotchy.

### Holding and retarding

Because the yeast is nearly stopped at 3–5 °C, the process can be paused in three places:

- **Détrempe**, overnight (standard in R1-34).
- **Laminated block**, up to about 12–16 h after the last turn, wrapped. Useful to split the work, but the dough will ferment slowly and become more gassy and harder to sheet evenly.
- **Shaped croissants**, covered, overnight in the refrigerator (retarded proof), then proofed the next morning (they need 1–2 h at 24–27 °C to warm up and finish). This is how many bakeries have fresh croissants at 7 a.m. Shaped croissants can also be frozen (up to about 2–4 weeks at home; thaw overnight in the refrigerator, then proof).

## Laminating and sheeting by hand

[R1-34](recipes/R1-34-croissant.md) uses an **open (butt-joint) lock-in**: the 19 × 19 cm plaque on a 21 × 40 cm détrempe, ends folded to meet in the middle, then **1 double + 1 single** turn: 4 × 3 = **12 fat layers**. Compared with puff pastry, the croissant needs far fewer layers because each dough layer must contain gas cells. From [15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md): at 4 mm, 12 fat layers leave dough layers of about 220 µm, several starch granules thick; at 81 fat layers they would be about 35 µm, barely one.

The block contains yeast, so time matters too: aim to finish the lock-in and turns in **about 1–1.5 hours**, including rests. Every minute the block spends warm is fermentation you did not plan, and a gassy block is springy and uneven to sheet.

**Final sheeting is the hardest part at home.** You are reducing a 2.5 cm block to 4 mm over an area of about 26 × 52 cm (half the batch at a time). Rules:

1. **Work with half the block.** Keep the other half cold.
2. **Roll from the centre in both directions**, rotating the sheet 90° now and then, so it is square and evenly relaxed.
3. **Lift the sheet** from time to time and let it shrink back before continuing; this is the gluten relaxing. If it keeps springing back, it needs 10–15 minutes flat on a tray in the refrigerator.
4. **Measure the thickness** (guides, or calipers at the edge). 4 mm ± 0.5 mm. Thin areas give light, flat croissants with fewer steps; thick areas give heavy croissants with a dense core.
5. **Stay cold.** If the dough feels soft and sticky or the butter becomes visible as shiny patches, chill it. In a warm kitchen, refrigerate the sheet before cutting as well: cold dough cuts cleanly.

## Cutting and shaping

A standard French croissant triangle is about **9–10 cm at the base and 25 cm tall**, weighing **55–65 g**. Pains au chocolat are rectangles of about **8 × 15 cm** with two chocolate batons.

- Cut with a **straight-down press** (knife, pizza wheel or croissant cutter). The same edge rules as puff apply: a dragged, sealed edge rises less.
- **Stretch the triangle** gently lengthwise before rolling (from 25 to about 28 cm): more length gives more steps and a more elegant shape, but over-stretching tears the layers.
- **Roll loosely and evenly without pressing down.** Pressing welds the layers of the triangle to each other, and it will bake with a dense spiral inside. Keep a little tension so the roll is snug and the steps are well defined.
- **Tip underneath**, touching the tray, so it cannot unroll during the proof and bake.
- Space them well: they grow to 2–2.5 times their volume and bake poorly if they touch.

## Reading the proof

A croissant is ready to bake when:

| Sign | Underproofed | Ready | Overproofed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume vs shaped | < 1.7× | 2–2.5× | > 3×, spreading sideways |
| Jiggle when you shake the tray | Firm, no movement | Jiggles like set jelly | Wobbles loosely, may sag |
| Layers at the sides | Tight, lines barely visible | Clearly separated, dough between them visibly airy | Blurred, stretched, dough bubbles or tears |
| Finger test (floured, gentle) | Springs back fast | Springs back slowly, leaves a slight dent | Dent stays; may deflate |
| Tray | Clean | Clean | Butter film visible if also warm |

**Underproofing** is the most common home failure and it looks like a lamination failure. The dough layers have not expanded, so in the oven they expand violently while tight: the steps split, the croissant bursts open on the sides, and butter is squeezed out of the tight layers onto the tray. The crumb is dense and bready near the centre. Because it produces leakage, many people blame the butter. Check the proof log first.

**Overproofing** stretches the dough layers so thin that they cannot hold gas in the oven; the croissant spreads, bakes flat, and may collapse and wrinkle as it cools.

## Egg wash and baking

**Egg wash twice:** once lightly after shaping (it keeps the surface moist during the proof) and again, very gently, just before baking. Brush along the steps, not into the cut edges or the ends. Whole egg with an extra yolk and a pinch of salt (salt thins the egg and helps it brush evenly) gives a deep, glossy brown.

**Oven: 190 °C fan (375 °F), after preheating to 200 °C, for 16–20 minutes** (conventional: preheat 210 °C, bake at 200 °C (390 °F)). The profile balances three things:

- Enough heat to generate steam and set the leaves before the butter runs out (lift).
- Sugar, milk and egg wash brown quickly, so too hot means dark outside and wet inside.
- The inner spiral must dry and set, or the croissant collapses as it cools.

**Bake to deep colour.** Deep golden-brown to mahogany, **including the creases between the steps** and the underside. The creases are the last to colour and are your doneness indicator: pale creases mean the inner layers are still wet. A croissant that is pulled golden and pale in the creases will collapse slightly and turn soft and chewy within the hour.

## Reading a cross-section

Cut a cooled croissant lengthwise, through the middle, with a serrated knife, and photograph it with a ruler. This is the most informative single measurement you can make on a croissant.

| What you see | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Open honeycomb: large, elongated cells in concentric arcs, thin glossy walls | Good lamination and proof. The target |
| Honeycomb at the outside, dense bready core | Core underproofed, or the spiral was pressed when shaping, or the centre was underbaked |
| Fine, even, bready crumb throughout, no honeycomb | Layers lost: butter smeared during lamination (too warm), too many turns, or proof too warm |
| Very large irregular holes, thick uneven walls, some doughy streaks | Butter shattered during lamination (too cold), or uneven sheeting |
| Tight, dense layers with a gummy, compressed centre and split sides | Underproofed |
| Collapsed, wrinkled, layers fused near the base | Overproofed, or underbaked |

The quality target used in professional competitions and bakeries is exactly the first row: an even, open honeycomb from the outside to the centre.

## Pain au chocolat

The same dough, a rectangle of about 8 × 15 cm and two chocolate batons. Place one baton 1 cm from a short end, fold the dough over it, place the second at the fold, roll up, and set seam-down. Batons are made from a chocolate formulated to hold its shape when baked (it has less cocoa butter than couverture); chopped couverture melts and runs. Proof and bake as for croissants; they take about the same time.

## What goes wrong

- **Butter on the tray.** Proof too warm, underproofed, or butter layers damaged in lamination. See [Butter leaking during baking](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=butter-leaking-during-baking).
- **Bready crumb, no honeycomb.** See [Bready, dense interior](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=bready-dense-interior).
- **Tight, heavy, split sides.** See [Underproofed croissants, tight and leaking](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=underproofed-croissants-tight-and-leaking).
- **Flat, wrinkled after cooling.** See [Croissants collapse](troubleshooting/laminated.md?id=croissants-collapse).

## Check yourself

1. A friend proofs croissants at 32 °C "to speed it up". They are ready in 70 minutes but bake flat with a lot of butter on the tray and a bready crumb. Explain.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 32 °C the yeast is fast, but the butter is close to melting: its solid fat fraction drops sharply, so the butter layers soften, merge into the dough and seep out of the edges during the proof. When the croissants enter the oven, many layers are already fused and much of the fat is outside the dough. Less steam is trapped, the lift is lower, and the crumb is that of an enriched bread. Proof at 24–27 °C, never above about 28 °C.

</details>

2. Your détrempe is mixed to a perfect windowpane. What will you notice during sheeting and shaping, and in the baked croissant?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The dough will be elastic and spring back during every turn and the final sheet; you will need more force and more rests (and the butter will warm while you fight it). Triangles shrink after cutting, becoming shorter and wider, and croissants may have fewer steps and a tighter shape. Excessive force risks smearing or tearing the butter layers. Mix only to moderate development.

</details>

3. You need fresh croissants at 8:00. You finish shaping at 20:00 the night before. Give a plan.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Retarded proof: egg-wash lightly, cover the trays loosely (film not touching) and refrigerate at 3–5 °C overnight. At about 5:30–6:00, move them to the proofing box at 24–27 °C, humid; they need about 1.5–2 h to warm and finish (judge by 2–2.5× volume and jiggle, not the clock). Preheat at 7:15, second wash, bake at about 7:30 for 16–20 minutes, cool 20 minutes. Record the proof time: it becomes your standard.

</details>

4. A croissant cross-section shows a good honeycomb in the outer half but a dense, doughy spiral in the centre. Name three possible causes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Any three of: the triangle was pressed down while rolling, sealing the inner layers; the croissant was rolled too tightly; underproofed (the centre warms last and proofs least); underbaked (pale creases, inner layers still wet); the triangle was too thick at the base (uneven sheet) or too heavy for the bake time.

</details>

5. Why does a croissant use 12–27 fat layers when puff pastry uses 256–729?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Croissant dough layers must hold yeast gas cells so they can expand in the proof and form a honeycomb. At 4 mm, 12 fat layers leave each dough layer about 220 µm thick; with hundreds of layers, the dough layers would be a few micrometres thick, thinner than a starch granule, and could not contain gas cells. The result would be a fine, bready or brioche-like crumb. Puff pastry has no yeast; its leaves only need to separate and fry, so thin, numerous layers give a finer, more even lift.

</details>
