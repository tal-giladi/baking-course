## Why this lesson exists

A custard is the simplest system in pastry that fails on a 3 °C margin. Crème anglaise is thin at 78 °C, correct at 82–84 °C and grainy at 88 °C; a crème brûlée is liquid in the centre at 72 °C and weeping at 90 °C. Almost everything you will do with eggs in dairy (anglaise as a sauce, the base of ice cream and bavarois, baked custards, the egg part of pastry cream, custard tart fillings) depends on hitting that window on purpose. This lesson gives you the mechanism, the numbers, and the two control strategies: stir and measure (anglaise), or limit the heat (baked custards in a water bath).

## You will be able to

- Explain what a custard is in colloid terms and why it thickens or sets instead of curdling.
- Predict how dilution, sugar, fat and heating rate move the setting temperature and the texture.
- Cook a crème anglaise to 82–84 °C, recognise the nappe stage, and cool it safely.
- Bake a crème brûlée and a crème caramel to a measured endpoint, and explain why one unmoulds and the other cannot.
- Apply the cooling and storage rules for egg-and-dairy products.

## What a custard is

A custard is egg proteins dispersed in a large volume of dairy and sugar, heated until those proteins unfold and bond into a loose network that traps the liquid. In the language of [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md) it is a protein **gel** (baked custard) or a thickened **sol** at the edge of gelling (stirred custard), with milk-fat droplets dispersed through it.

The chemistry is the one you met in [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md) and [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md), in three stages:

| Stage | What the proteins do | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Native | Folded, dissolved, separate | Thin liquid, like sweet milk |
| Denatured and lightly bonded | Unfolded chains touch and form scattered, weak bonds; the network is fine and holds all the water | Stirred: thickens, coats the spoon. Baked: sets to a trembling gel |
| Over-coagulated | Bonds keep forming; the network tightens into dense clumps and squeezes water out | Stirred: grains or curds in thin liquid. Baked: holes, weeping, grainy texture |

The whole craft is stopping in the middle row. You are not cooking the eggs "through"; you are building just enough network.

### Why the custard sets so much higher than an egg

Egg yolk alone thickens from about 65 °C and sets at around 70 °C ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)). Crème anglaise thickens at 80–84 °C. Three things push the temperature up:

1. **Dilution.** In [R1-24](recipes/R1-24-creme-anglaise.md), 100 g yolks go into 500 g dairy. The yolk contributes about 16 g protein to a 700 g mix: roughly 2.3 % protein. Protein chains that are far apart need more thermal motion (a higher temperature, or more time) before enough of them meet and bond. More dilution: higher setting temperature and a softer set.
2. **Sugar.** Sugar molecules crowd between the protein chains, bind water and stabilize the folded proteins, so unfolding needs more energy. A custard with 20 % sugar on dairy sets several degrees higher than the same custard unsweetened, and it sets softer. This is also why sugar is your safety margin: it widens the gap between "thick" and "curdled".
3. **Fat.** Cream fat droplets sit between the proteins and interrupt the network. A cream custard is softer, silkier and more forgiving than a milk custard with the same eggs.

Acid and salt act the other way: they lower the setting temperature and firm the gel. A pinch of salt does little; a squeeze of lemon into a hot custard can curdle it.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Custard texture is a function of *protein concentration × time × temperature*, modified by sugar and fat. More egg, less sugar or a longer hold all move the set down in temperature and up in firmness. You design a custard by choosing its protein level, then you control it with a thermometer.

</div>

### Heating rate matters

Coagulation is a rate process, not a switch. Heated slowly, a custard spends longer in the zone where the network forms and thickens slightly lower and more evenly. Heated fast (high flame, thin pan), the layer on the pan base overshoots while the thermometer in the middle still reads 80 °C. Most curdled anglaise is a hot bottom layer, not a hot average; moderate heat and stirring across the whole base solve most of it.

## Stirred custard: crème anglaise

Crème anglaise is milk and/or cream, egg yolks and sugar, flavoured (vanilla is classic), stirred over direct heat until it thickens to a pourable sauce. It is the model stirred custard and the base for ice cream, bavarois and many mousses in Stage 2.

### The formula

A standard professional ratio is **dairy 100 : yolks 20 : sugar 20** by weight. [R1-24](recipes/R1-24-creme-anglaise.md) uses 250 g milk, 250 g cream, 100 g yolks (5–6 large yolks at 17–19 g each) and 100 g sugar.

| Lever | Typical range (% of dairy) | Moving up does |
|---|---|---|
| Yolks | 16–30 % | Thicker, richer, more eggy; sets at a slightly lower temperature; less margin |
| Sugar | 15–25 % | Sweeter; raises the thickening temperature; softer, more forgiving |
| Cream share of the dairy | 0–100 % | Richer, silkier, thicker mouthfeel at the same temperature; ice-cream bases sit at the high end |

### The procedure logic

1. **Heat the dairy** (with part of the sugar and the vanilla) to 80–90 °C. Hot dairy shortens the time the eggs spend on the heat, which reduces the chance of a hot bottom layer.
2. **Whisk the yolks with the rest of the sugar** just until combined and slightly pale. Do not whisk to a thick foam: foam on the surface hides the nappe stage and traps air bubbles in the sauce.
3. **Temper.** Pour about one third of the hot dairy onto the yolks while whisking. This raises the yolks gradually to about 50–60 °C and dilutes them, so they do not scramble where hot liquid hits concentrated yolk. Then return everything to the pan.
4. **Cook, stirring**, over medium-low heat, with a heatproof spatula in a figure-eight that scrapes the whole base and the corners. The mixture starts at about 60–65 °C. Watch the thermometer: aim for a rise of no more than about 2–3 °C per 10 seconds near the end.
5. **Stop at 82–84 °C.** The foam on top disappears, the sauce looks glossier and slightly heavier, and it coats the spoon: the **nappe** stage.
6. **Strain and cool immediately** through a fine sieve into a bowl set in an ice bath. The pan holds heat; left in it, the custard climbs another 1–3 °C and can curdle after you took it off the burner.

### The nappe test

Dip a wooden spoon or spatula in the custard, lift it, hold it horizontal and draw a finger across the back. At the nappe stage the custard coats the spoon evenly and **the finger line stays clean and sharp**: the edges do not run back into it. Below about 80 °C the line blurs and closes; above 85 °C you may see a grainy texture on the spoon. Use the thermometer as the primary measure and the nappe test as confirmation; together they calibrate your eye. In [X1-33](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md) you cook the same base to 78, 82 and 86 °C and see the whole window.

| Temperature | Appearance | Result after chilling |
|---|---|---|
| 75–78 °C | Thin, surface foam still present, finger line closes | Thin, milky sauce; slightly raw egg note |
| 80–82 °C | Foam gone, glossier, coats the spoon; line just holds | Light, pourable sauce |
| 82–84 °C | Nappe: coats the spoon, line clean and sharp | Classic anglaise: velvety, pourable, coats a plate thinly |
| 85–88 °C | Thicker at first, then fine grains, slightly sulfurous smell | Grainy; may look fine after blending but tastes of cooked egg |
| > 88 °C | Visible curds in watery liquid | Curdled; not fully recoverable |

The exact curdling point depends on the formula and the heating rate; with this ratio, standard professional practice treats 85 °C as the ceiling.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** At the first sign of graininess, pour the custard into a cold bowl and blend with an immersion blender for 20–30 seconds: the clumps break into particles too small to feel. Visible curds in watery liquid cannot be rescued; the proteins have already expelled their water.

</div>

## Baked custards: crème brûlée and crème caramel

A baked custard is not stirred, so it sets into a continuous gel. There is no nappe to watch, and you cannot see the centre. The control strategy is to **limit the heat** so that the edges cannot run far ahead of the centre, and to judge the endpoint by internal temperature and wobble.

### Crème brûlée vs crème caramel

| | Crème brûlée | Crème caramel |
|---|---|---|
| Dairy | Cream (35 %) | Milk (whole) |
| Eggs | Yolks only (~20 % of dairy) | Whole eggs plus some yolks (~38 % of dairy) |
| Sugar | 18 % in the custard, caramelized crust on top | 20 % in the custard, caramel layer in the mould |
| Protein in mix | ~2.3 % | ~3.2 % |
| Set | Soft, spoonable, barely holds its shape | Firm enough to unmould and stand |
| Served | In the ramekin | Turned out, caramel as sauce |

The table shows why only one of them unmoulds. The crème caramel in [R1-25](recipes/R1-25-creme-brulee.md) carries about 25 g egg protein in 795 g of mix; the crème brûlée about 16 g in 695 g. Whole eggs bring egg-white proteins (mainly ovalbumin), which form a firmer, more elastic gel than yolk proteins, and milk carries less fat than cream to interrupt the network. The result is a custard that holds its shape when turned out. A crème brûlée is a rich cream only just set by yolk: turned out, it slumps.

The caramel layer of a crème caramel is made first, as in [R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md): sugar cooked to a medium amber (about 170–180 °C (340–355 °F)), poured into the moulds, left to harden. During baking and an overnight chill, moisture from the custard partly dissolves the hard caramel into a thin sauce. The brûlée crust is the opposite: a thin layer of sugar caramelized with a torch just before serving, so it is still glassy when it reaches the table.

### Why a water bath works

A bain-marie (water bath) is a heat limiter. Water cannot rise above 100 °C at atmospheric pressure, and in a 150 °C (300 °F) oven a pan of water typically sits around 80–90 °C because evaporation keeps cooling it. The sides and base of each ramekin are therefore held close to the custard's target temperature instead of at 150 °C. The edge and the centre set at nearly the same time, and the edge cannot overshoot into the curdling zone while the centre catches up.

| Setup | Edge temperature environment | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| 170–180 °C oven, no water bath | Air at 170 °C, dish walls hot | Puffed, browned top; bubbles and holes at the edges; weeping; grainy |
| 150 °C (300 °F) oven, water bath | Water ~80–90 °C | Smooth, even set, 30–45 min |
| 100–110 °C (212–230 °F) fan oven, no water bath | Air barely above target | Smooth, even set, but slow: 45–70 min |

The low-oven method uses the air itself as the limiter, but depends on an oven that holds a low temperature accurately ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)). In a water bath, put a cloth under the ramekins (it insulates the base from the hot tin) and fill with 60–70 °C water to half or two-thirds of their height.

### Judging the endpoint

| Test | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Centre temperature | **78–82 °C** (crème brûlée ~80 °C; crème caramel 80–82 °C) | Probe into the centre of one ramekin, reading the middle depth |
| Wobble | Tap the side: the whole surface moves as one sheet, like set jelly; the centre 2–3 cm may tremble more but does not ripple like liquid | Liquid waves in the centre = not set |
| Knife (crème caramel) | A thin knife near the centre comes out clean and wet, not milky | Milky liquid on the blade = not set |

Pull at the target; the custard keeps cooking for a few minutes from its own heat and the hot dish, then continues to firm as it chills (the gel strengthens at 4 °C). A custard that looks perfectly firm in the oven is usually overbaked when cold. Stir rather than beat the mix, strain it and skim the foam: whisked-in air sets as a foam layer and small holes ([Bubbles, foam or tunnels in baked custard](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=bubbles-foam-or-tunnels-in-baked-custard)).

## Food safety of custards

Custards are ideal growth media for bacteria: neutral pH, high water activity, protein and sugar. Two separate issues apply ([00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md), [SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)).

**The eggs.** Raw eggs may carry *Salmonella*. The U.S. FDA advises cooking egg dishes to 71 °C (160 °F) ([FDA-eggs]). A crème anglaise at 82–84 °C and a baked custard at 80 °C are well above this. A custard pulled at 75 °C is borderline and should be treated as made with lightly cooked eggs: use pasteurized eggs if serving vulnerable people.

**After cooking.** Cooking does not sterilize. Standard food-safety practice is to cool cooked foods from 60 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours and to 5 °C within 6 hours in total; for custards, do much better.

| Rule | Target |
|---|---|
| Stirred custard | Strain into a bowl in an ice bath; stir until below 10 °C (about 15–20 min for 700 g) |
| Baked custard | Cool 20–30 min on a rack, then refrigerate uncovered until cold, then cover |
| Storage | ≤ 5 °C, covered, surface film on stirred custards |
| Shelf life | 2–3 days refrigerated (standard professional practice); discard if sour-smelling or separating |
| Never | Leave custard at room temperature to "cool down" for hours |

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Raw eggs are an allergen and a *Salmonella* risk; wash hands and utensils after cracking eggs, and keep raw mix cold until you cook it. Caramel for crème caramel is at 170–180 °C and sticks to skin: pour it with a dry ladle, keep children and pets away, and have a bowl of cold water ready for a burned finger. A kitchen torch is an open flame: keep the ramekins on a heatproof surface away from paper and towels.

</div>

## On the bench

1. Make [R1-24 Crème anglaise](recipes/R1-24-creme-anglaise.md). Record the temperature every 30 s from the moment the yolks go back into the pan. Do the nappe test at 78, 80, 82 and 84 °C on the same spoon, and photograph each.
2. Run [X1-33 Custard endpoint temperature](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md) (you can use one batch of base for both the recipe and the experiment if you plan it).
3. Bake [R1-25 Crème brûlée and crème caramel](recipes/R1-25-creme-brulee.md). If your oven allows, bake two brûlées without a water bath at the same setting as a control, and compare edges and texture after chilling.
4. Chill everything, then taste side by side: anglaise at 82 °C vs 86 °C, brûlée vs caramel. Write down the difference in words (silky, grainy, eggy, firm, trembling).

## What goes wrong

- **Grainy or curdled anglaise.** Overshoot past 85 °C, a hot bottom layer, or leaving it in the hot pan. See [Curdled or grainy anglaise](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=curdled-or-grainy-anglaise).
- **Thin anglaise.** Pulled below 80 °C or too little yolk. See [Thin anglaise](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=thin-anglaise).
- **Weeping, grainy or holey baked custard.** Oven too hot, no water bath, or baked past 85 °C. See [Baked custard weeping, grainy or full of holes](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=baked-custard-weeping-grainy-or-full-of-holes).
- **Baked custard that will not set.** Pulled too early, ramekins too deep, or oven cooler than the dial. See [Baked custard not setting](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=baked-custard-not-setting).

## Check yourself

1. You make an anglaise with 30 % yolks and 10 % sugar on the dairy instead of 20/20. Will it thicken at a higher or lower temperature, and what happens to your margin before curdling?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lower. More protein (less dilution) and less sugar both make the proteins bond earlier: it thickens perhaps at 78–80 °C and curdles sooner. The window between nappe and curdling narrows, so you need slower heating and an earlier pull, judged by the nappe test as well as the thermometer.

</details>

2. Your thermometer read 83 °C in the centre of the pan, but the anglaise has fine grains. What probably happened, and how do you prevent it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A layer at the bottom and corners of the pan overshot (probably well above 85 °C) while the centre read 83 °C, because heat was too high or stirring did not scrape the whole base. Use moderate heat, a heavy pan, a flat spatula in a figure-eight that covers the base and corners, and strain into a cold bowl immediately.

</details>

3. Why does a crème caramel unmould and a crème brûlée not?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Crème caramel uses whole eggs (plus yolks) in milk: more total protein (~3.2 % vs ~2.3 %), egg-white proteins that form a firmer, more elastic gel, and less fat interrupting the network. Crème brûlée is yolks in cream, a soft gel that only holds in its dish.

</details>

4. A 150 °C oven is much hotter than a custard needs. Why does a crème brûlée bake smoothly at that setting in a water bath?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The water around the ramekins cannot exceed 100 °C and in practice stays around 80–90 °C, because evaporation removes heat. The ramekin walls see water-bath temperature, not oven air, so edges and centre set at nearly the same rate and the edges cannot overshoot.

</details>
