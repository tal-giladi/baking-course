# M12 · Custards and creams

## Purpose

M09 showed you how egg proteins coagulate and how foams form; M04 and M06 showed you how starch gelatinizes. This module puts those mechanisms to work in the creams that fill and finish most French pastry. You will learn three systems: **egg-thickened custards** (stirred crème anglaise and baked crème brûlée and crème caramel), controlled by an endpoint temperature a few degrees wide; **starch-and-egg creams** (crème pâtissière and crème diplomate), controlled by a full boil that finishes the starch and destroys the yolk's amylase; and **whipped dairy cream** (Chantilly), a foam held up by partly crystalline fat and controlled above all by temperature. With these you can make, store and diagnose every cream you will need for éclairs, tarts, Danish and layered cakes later in the course, and you have the bases that Stage 2 turns into ice creams, bavarois, crémeux and mousses.

## Objectives

By the end of this module you can:

- Explain how dilution, sugar, fat and heating rate set the thickening and curdling temperatures of an egg custard, and cook crème anglaise to 82–84 °C by thermometer and nappe test.
- Bake crème brûlée and crème caramel to a measured endpoint using a water bath or low oven as a heat limiter, and explain why only one unmoulds.
- Make crème pâtissière that stays firm for 2–3 days, explaining the roles of starch gelatinization and yolk α-amylase, and choose between cornstarch, flour and root starches.
- Whip cream to the right stage for folding or piping, measure overrun, and stabilize it for a 24 h hold.
- Cool and store egg-and-dairy products safely, and diagnose curdling, thinning, weeping and graininess from symptoms.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Custard | Egg proteins dispersed in dairy and sugar, thickened or set by heat-induced coagulation. |
| Stirred custard | A custard cooked while stirring so it thickens to a pourable sauce without setting into a gel; crème anglaise. |
| Baked custard | A custard set undisturbed in the oven into a continuous protein gel; crème brûlée, crème caramel. |
| Crème anglaise | Stirred custard of milk and/or cream, yolks and sugar (standard ratio 100 : 20 : 20), cooked to 82–84 °C. |
| Nappe | The stage at which a custard coats the back of a spoon and a finger line drawn through it stays clean; about 82–84 °C for anglaise. |
| Tempering | Adding hot liquid gradually to eggs while whisking, to warm and dilute them before they go on the heat. |
| Curdling | Over-coagulation of egg proteins into clumps that expel water: grain, then visible curds in thin liquid; above about 85 °C in anglaise. |
| Bain-marie (water bath) | Water surrounding a baking mould; it stays around 80–90 °C in a 150 °C oven and limits the edge temperature of a custard. |
| Syneresis (weeping) | Liquid squeezed out of a gel or drained from a foam as its network contracts or ages. |
| Crème pâtissière | Pastry cream: milk, yolks, sugar and starch (about 8–10 % cornstarch on milk), boiled 1–2 min, enriched with butter. |
| Starch gelatinization | Swelling of starch granules heated in water, with loss of crystalline order and leaching of amylose; thickens the liquid and sets it on cooling. |
| Yolk α-amylase | An enzyme in egg yolk that digests starch; it survives heating to 80–85 °C and thins an under-boiled pastry cream overnight. |
| Crème diplomate | Pastry cream lightened with whipped cream, often set with a little gelatin. |
| Crème mousseline | Pastry cream enriched with a large amount of beaten butter (treated in M13). |
| Line-spread test | Viscosity test: a fixed amount of sample is released at the centre of concentric circles and its spread measured after a set time. |
| Partial coalescence | Sticking together of partly crystalline fat globules without full merging; forms the fat network that holds whipped cream. |
| Overrun | Percentage increase in volume from incorporated air; (weight of a cup of liquid − weight of the same cup of foam) ÷ weight of foam × 100. |
| Soft peak / medium–firm peak | Whipping stages: a peak that flops over (for folding) / a peak that holds with only its tip bending (for piping). |
| Crème Chantilly | Cream (35–40 % fat) whipped with 8–10 % icing sugar and vanilla. |
| UHT cream | Cream heated to about 135–140 °C for seconds for long shelf life; usually homogenized; whips more slowly and less stably than pasteurized cream. |
