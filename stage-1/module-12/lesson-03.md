## Why this lesson exists

Whipped cream looks like the easiest thing in pastry, and it is the component most often ruined at the last minute: cream that will not whip on a hot day, cream that goes grainy while you pipe it, a cake that weeps onto the board by the evening. Unlike an egg-white foam, whipped cream is held up by **fat**, not protein, and fat is ruled by temperature. Once you see whipped cream as a network of partly crystalline fat globules, every one of these failures becomes predictable, and the choice of cream, temperature, stage and stabilizer becomes a design decision.

## You will be able to

- Read a cream label and choose a cream that will whip reliably for a given job.
- Explain partial coalescence and why cream must be cold to whip.
- Stop whipping at the correct stage for folding, filling or piping, and recover slightly overwhipped cream.
- Make crème Chantilly and measure its overrun.
- Choose a stabilizer (gelatin, mascarpone, milk powder) and predict its effect.

## Cream: what is in the carton

Cream is milk concentrated in fat: an oil-in-water emulsion of milk-fat globules (typically 1–10 µm) each wrapped in a thin membrane of phospholipids and proteins (the milk-fat globule membrane), dispersed in skim milk ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)).

| Label (market) | Fat content | Whips? |
|---|---|---|
| Single cream (UK), half-and-half (US), cooking cream | 10–20 % | No |
| Light cream (US), "crème liquide légère" (FR) | 18–30 % | No, or only to a weak foam that collapses |
| Whipping cream (US "light whipping") | 30–36 % | Yes; soft, light foam |
| French "crème liquide entière" / "crème fleurette" | ≥ 30 %, usually 30–35 % | Yes |
| Whipping cream (UK) | ≥ 35 % | Yes, reliably |
| Heavy cream (US), heavy whipping cream | ≥ 36 %, often 36–40 % | Yes; firmer, more stable |
| Double cream (UK) | ≥ 48 % | Yes, very fast; easy to overwhip; often thinned with milk for whipping |

Legal definitions differ by country; read the fat percentage on the label rather than the name. For whipping, **35–40 %** is the target. Below about 30 % there is not enough fat to build a continuous network around the bubbles, and the cream will not whip reliably whatever you do.

**Processing matters as well.** Most supermarket cream is heat-treated in one of two ways:

| Treatment | What it does | Whipping |
|---|---|---|
| Pasteurized (about 72–80 °C for seconds) | Minimal change to globules and proteins | Best volume and stability |
| UHT (about 135–140 °C for seconds), usually homogenized | Globules made smaller and coated with casein; some whey proteins denatured | Whips more slowly, to a softer, less stable foam that weeps sooner; many UHT creams add carrageenan to compensate |

Plant-based "whipping creams" are a different system: usually hydrogenated or lauric fats (palm kernel, coconut) with emulsifiers and stabilizers, designed to whip to high volume and hold for days. They are more stable than dairy cream and taste different. Chilled full-fat coconut cream (about 20–25 % fat) can be whipped using its solid coconut fat, but softly. These are Stage 2 topics; the principles below still apply.

## Why cream whips: partial coalescence

An egg-white foam is held by protein films at each bubble. Cream starts that way and then does something different.

1. **Air in, protein first.** The whisk folds in air. Milk proteins adsorb at the new bubble surfaces and give a thin, unstable foam. This is the frothy first minute.
2. **Globules meet bubbles.** As whipping continues, fat globules collide with the bubble surfaces. The air–water interface strips part of their membrane.
3. **Crystals make them stick.** At refrigerator temperature, milk fat is roughly half solid: each globule contains fat crystals, and some of them poke through the damaged membrane like burrs. When two such globules touch, the crystals pierce into each other and the liquid fat between them bridges the contact. They stick without fully merging: **partial coalescence** ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)).
4. **A network forms.** Chains and clusters of partly coalesced globules build up around each bubble and between bubbles, forming a continuous fat skeleton. The foam stiffens and holds its shape: that is whipped cream.
5. **Too far: butter.** Keep whipping and the clusters grow into grains of fat; they no longer surround the bubbles but squeeze together, releasing air and water. The foam collapses into yellow butter grains in thin buttermilk. The emulsion has **inverted** from fat-in-water to water-in-fat ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)).

Whipped cream is therefore a foam held up by a partially churned emulsion. Butter-making is simply whipping carried to the end.

### Why cold is not optional

Partial coalescence needs the globules to be *partly* crystalline: enough solid fat to make them stick without merging, enough liquid fat to glue them.

| Cream temperature | Fat state | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 2–5 °C | Mostly crystalline | Slow start, then the best, finest, most stable foam |
| 5–7 °C | Mostly crystalline | Good; the practical working upper limit |
| 10–12 °C | Much more liquid fat | Globules merge fully instead of partially; slow, low volume, greasy, collapses sooner |
| > 15 °C | Largely liquid | Will not whip to a stable foam |

The cream warms as you whip it: the whisk adds friction energy and the bowl is often warmer than the cream. Keep the cream **at or below 5–7 °C** from the refrigerator until the end: chill the bowl and whisk for 10–15 minutes in the refrigerator or freezer, work in a cool room, and, for large amounts or a warm kitchen, whip over a bowl of iced water. Cream that has once been warmed and re-chilled whips worse, because the fat recrystallizes into coarser crystals; store it cold throughout.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Whipped cream is held up by fat crystals, so temperature is the first variable. If cream will not whip or turns greasy, measure its temperature before blaming the cream.

</div>

## The stages of whipping

Volume and texture change continuously; name the stages by what the cream does on the whisk.

| Stage | What you see | Overrun (approx.) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frothy / foamy | Large bubbles, still liquid | < 30 % | — |
| Soft trails ("ribbon") | Whisk leaves trails that fade slowly | 40–60 % | Adding sugar and flavour |
| **Soft peak** | Lifted whisk makes a peak that flops over; cream is glossy and still flows slightly | 70–90 % | **Folding** into mousses, diplomate, bavarois; topping desserts |
| **Medium–firm peak** | Peak holds and only its tip bends; surface still smooth and satiny; holds a spoon trench | 90–110 % | **Piping and filling** (cakes, choux, tarts) |
| Stiff / overwhipped | Peak rigid, surface matte and slightly rough, texture "rippled" | falling | Nothing: stop before this |
| Grainy / curdled | Visibly grainy, beginning to weep yellow-white liquid | collapsing | Rescue possible only at the first sign (see below) |
| Butter | Yellow grains, thin buttermilk | — | Butter |

**Overrun** is the percentage of volume added by air. You can measure it by weight: fill a cup level with liquid cream and weigh it; fill the same cup level with whipped cream and weigh it.

**Overrun % = (weight of cup of liquid − weight of cup of whipped) ÷ weight of cup of whipped × 100**

Dairy whipped cream reaches at most about 100–120 % overrun (roughly double volume); egg whites reach 500–700 %. That difference is the difference between a fat-supported and a protein-supported foam.

**Why stop early for folding:** folding adds work. Cream whipped to firm peaks and then folded into a base goes past its optimum during folding and turns grainy. Whip to soft peaks and let the folding finish the job.

**Speed and time.** A stand mixer on medium-high whips 500 g cold cream to soft peaks in about 2–4 minutes; the step from firm to grainy can take 10–20 seconds. Finish the last stage by hand with a whisk, or at low speed, so you can stop exactly.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Slightly overwhipped cream (matte, just starting to look rough) can be brought back by folding in 5–10 % liquid cold cream by hand: the new, unwhipped globules loosen the over-connected network and restore gloss. Once it has visible grains or weeps liquid, the fat has churned; use it for something baked, or keep going and make butter.

</div>

## Crème Chantilly

Crème Chantilly is sweetened, flavoured whipped cream: cream with about **8–10 % icing sugar** (on the cream weight) and vanilla ([R1-27](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md)). Icing sugar dissolves quickly in the cold cream without grittiness; many icing sugars contain a little starch, which adds a trace of stability. Add the sugar and vanilla when the cream reaches soft trails: sugar added at the very start slightly slows foaming and reduces volume; added at the end it may not dissolve. Sugar also dissolves into the liquid between the bubbles, raising its viscosity and slowing drainage.

Some pastry chefs reserve "Chantilly" for cream whipped with sugar and vanilla only, and call cream folded into other components simply "whipped cream" or "crème fouettée". The mechanism is the same.

## Stabilizing whipped cream

Unstabilized whipped cream is at its best for a few hours. Over 4–24 hours in the refrigerator, liquid drains from the foam (**weeping** or syneresis) and the bubbles coarsen. Every stabilizer works on one of two levers: **thicken or gel the liquid between the bubbles**, or **add more fat and solids to the network**.

| Stabilizer | Typical dose (on cream) | How it works | Effect and caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gelatin | 0.5–1 % (2.5–5 g per 500 g cream) | Bloomed, melted, tempered with a little cream and whisked in at soft trails; sets the liquid phase into a gel as it cools | Holds shape for 1–3 days; can make the cream slightly firm or "jellied" at the top of the range; must be added warm (~35–40 °C) to cold cream quickly or it forms strings |
| Mascarpone | Replace 10–25 % of the cream | Adds fat (~40 % and more) and solids; the network is denser | Richer, slightly tangy, very pipeable; whips fast; overwhips easily |
| Skimmed milk powder | 3–5 % | Adds milk protein and lactose; thickens the serum and strengthens the interface | Subtle milky flavour; modest effect |
| Higher-fat cream (38–40 %) | — | More fat for the network | Firmer, more stable; less airy |
| Starch-containing icing sugar, or commercial stabilizer (modified starch, carrageenan) | per pack | Thicken the serum | Modest; convenient |

Stabilizers slow weeping; they do not rescue warm or low-fat cream. Temperature and fat content still come first.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Cream is a high-risk dairy product. Keep it at ≤ 5 °C, return it to the refrigerator within about 30 minutes of use, and keep cakes and pastries filled with whipped cream refrigerated. Unstabilized Chantilly is best used within a few hours, stabilized within 1–2 days (standard professional practice). Milk is an allergen.

</div>

## On the bench

1. Make [R1-27 Crème Chantilly](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md). Measure the cream temperature before and after whipping and the overrun at soft peak and at medium–firm peak.
2. Make the gelatin- or mascarpone-stabilized variant in the recipe alongside a plain batch. Pipe a rosette of each on a plate, and one spoonful of each in a sieve over a cup; check both after 2 h and 24 h (weigh the liquid in the cup).
3. Whip 150 g of cream past the end, into butter, as in [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md). Weigh the butter and the buttermilk; compare the butter weight with the fat content on the label.
4. Fold soft-peak cream into pastry cream to make crème diplomate ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)).

## What goes wrong

- **Will not whip.** Cream too warm, below 30 % fat, UHT, or warmed and re-chilled. See [Whipped cream will not whip](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=whipped-cream-will-not-whip).
- **Grainy or buttery.** Overwhipped, or whipped hard and then folded or piped. See [Overwhipped or grainy whipped cream](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=overwhipped-or-grainy-whipped-cream).
- **Weeping.** Underwhipped, too warm, too long before use, or unstabilized for a long hold. See [Whipped cream weeping](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=whipped-cream-weeping).

## Check yourself

1. It is 29 °C in the kitchen. Your first batch of cream whipped slowly and looks greasy. List three changes before the next batch.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Chill the bowl and whisk in the freezer for 10–15 min; keep the cream in the refrigerator until the moment of use (≤ 5 °C) and whip it over a bowl of iced water; whip in smaller batches, and check the label for ≥ 35 % fat and non-UHT cream.

</details>

2. A cup holds 245 g of liquid cream and 118 g of whipped cream. What is the overrun, and which stage is it probably at?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(245 − 118) ÷ 118 × 100 = 107.6 %. Medium–firm peak, close to the maximum overrun for dairy cream.

</details>

3. Why should cream for a mousse or diplomate be whipped only to soft peak?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Folding adds more work and more partial coalescence. Cream that is already at firm peak goes past its optimum during folding and becomes grainy; soft-peak cream reaches the right texture as the fold finishes, and it blends more smoothly into the base.

</details>

4. Whipped cream and egg-white foam: which reaches higher overrun, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Egg-white foam (500–700 % vs about 100 % for cream). Egg whites build thin, elastic protein films that stretch around many small bubbles. Cream is held by a network of partly coalesced fat globules, which are large compared with protein films and need to fill the space between bubbles; once enough fat has connected to hold the foam, extra whipping churns it rather than adding air.

</details>

5. You need piped rosettes that will sit on a cake for 24 h in the refrigerator. Give two stabilization options with doses.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Gelatin at 0.5–1 % of the cream weight, bloomed, melted and whisked in warm at soft trails; or replace 15–25 % of the cream with mascarpone. Either way use 35–40 % fat pasteurized cream, whip cold to medium–firm peak, and keep the cake at ≤ 5 °C.

</details>
