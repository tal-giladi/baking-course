## Why this lesson exists

Crème pâtissière (pastry cream) fills éclairs, tarts, Danish and layer cakes; it is probably the most used component in French pastry. It looks like a thick anglaise, but it follows the opposite rule: anglaise must never boil, pastry cream must boil. Cooks who carry the anglaise instinct into pastry cream pull it off the heat as soon as it thickens, and it turns to soup overnight. This lesson explains why, with two mechanisms (starch gelatinization and yolk amylase), and gives you the numbers to make a pastry cream that cuts cleanly on day one and is still firm on day three.

## You will be able to

- Explain why starch lets pastry cream boil without curdling, and why it must.
- Describe the yolk α-amylase trap and design a process that avoids it.
- Choose between cornstarch, flour and root starches, and adjust the dose when you swap them.
- Predict the effect of sugar, acid, fat and over-whisking on thickening.
- Make, cool, store and re-texture pastry cream, and build crème diplomate from it.

## Two thickeners in one pot

Pastry cream is milk, yolks, sugar and starch, cooked together, then enriched with butter and flavoured. The eggs thicken it a little and give richness, colour and flavour. The starch does most of the structural work.

| Thickener | Mechanism | Temperature | Behaviour on cooling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg yolk proteins | Coagulate into a fine network ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md)) | Thickening ~75–85 °C in this mix | Firm slightly |
| Starch | Granules absorb water, swell, leak amylose; the paste thickens ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)) | Cornstarch in sweetened milk: thickening from ~75–80 °C, full gelatinization and peak thickness near 90–95 °C and above | Amylose forms a gel network: the cream **sets** as it cools |

### Why the eggs do not curdle

In a crème anglaise, 85 °C is the ceiling. Pastry cream goes to 100 °C and boils for a minute or two without curdling. The starch protects the proteins. Swelling starch granules take up much of the water and fill the space between protein chains; leaked amylose raises the viscosity of the liquid. Egg proteins still coagulate, but they are held apart, so they cannot gather into the large, water-expelling clumps you see in a curdled anglaise. The coagulated protein ends up as small particles dispersed in the starch gel, too small to feel. This is also why you must keep whisking: the whisk keeps the thickening mass uniform while the bottom layer heats fastest.

### Why the starch must reach a boil

Cornstarch granules gelatinize over a range. In plain water, cornstarch starts to swell around 62–72 °C. In pastry cream, the sugar (about 20 % of the milk) competes for water and stabilizes the granules, which raises the gelatinization temperature and lowers the peak viscosity ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)). A cream heated to only 80–85 °C has thickened visibly, but many granules are not fully swollen and too little amylose has leaked out to build a firm gel on cooling. Taken to a boil, the granules swell completely, the cream passes its peak viscosity, and on cooling you get the firm, clean-cutting set you want.

You can see the peak happen. As the cream approaches the boil it goes suddenly thick, even lumpy; keep whisking. As it boils, it becomes **glossier and slightly looser** than at its thickest point. That small drop after the peak is "breakdown": the fully swollen granules are soft and some fragment under the whisk. It is your visual signal that gelatinization is complete. It is also why you do not boil for 5 minutes or blend it hard afterwards: continued breakdown thins the cream.

### The amylase trap

Egg yolk contains an α-amylase, an enzyme that cuts starch chains ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)). It is surprisingly heat-tolerant. In a cream pulled at first thickening (~80–85 °C) some of it survives, and over the next hours, in the refrigerator, it keeps cutting the gelatinized starch. The pastry cream that was thick at 18:00 is runny at 08:00. There is no way to rescue it once it has happened: the starch chains are cut.

Bringing the cream to a full boil and holding it at the boil, whisking, for about **1–2 minutes** inactivates the amylase. That is the professional rule, and it is the reason for it; the starch gelatinization alone would be almost finished at a shorter boil. In [X1-34](experiments/X1-34-starch-thickeners.md) you make one batch pulled at 85 °C and one boiled for a minute, and compare them at 2 h and at 24 h.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Anglaise and pastry cream are the same eggs in the same milk with opposite endpoints. Without starch, heat past 85 °C curdles the yolks. With starch, heat below the boil leaves both the starch and the yolk enzyme unfinished. Pastry cream: **full boil, 1–2 minutes, whisking**.

</div>

## The formula

[R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md) uses, on 500 g whole milk:

| Ingredient | % of milk | Grams | Range in professional practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole milk | 100 | 500 | Part cream for richer creams |
| Egg yolks | 20 | 100 | 16–24 % (some formulas use part whole egg) |
| Sugar | 22 | 110 | 20–25 % |
| Cornstarch | 9 | 45 | 8–10 % (or a cornstarch/flour mix) |
| Butter | 7 | 35 | 5–10 % |
| Vanilla extract | 1 | 5 | or 1 pod |
| Salt | 0.1 | 0.5 | pinch |

What each lever does:

| Lever | More | Less |
|---|---|---|
| Starch | Firmer, more cuttable, more "pudding"; above ~10 % pasty and gluey | Softer, creamier, runs when piped; below ~7 % may not hold in a tart |
| Yolks | Richer, yellower, more flavour, slightly softer set per unit of starch | Leaner, whiter, more starch-forward flavour |
| Sugar | Sweeter; softer set (sugar lowers gel strength); needs a good boil | Less sweet, slightly firmer |
| Butter (added hot) | Richer, silkier, firmer when cold (butterfat solidifies) | Lighter, less rich |
| Cream in place of part of the milk | Richer, softer | — |

### Mixing the starch in without lumps

Starch granules clump the instant they meet hot liquid: the outside of a clump gelatinizes into a skin and the inside stays dry. There are two defences, and pastry cream uses both. First, **disperse the starch in sugar and yolks** before any heat: each granule is separated by sugar crystals and coated in liquid. Second, **temper**: add the hot milk to the yolk mixture gradually while whisking, so no part of it sees boiling milk undiluted. Sift the starch if it has lumps in the packet.

## Choosing the starch

| Starch | Dose for similar thickness (cornstarch = 1) | Thickens at | Cooled texture | Appearance | Flavour | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cornstarch | 1× (~8–10 % of milk) | Needs ~90–95 °C+, i.e. a boil | Firm, short, clean-cutting gel | Opaque but glossy | Neutral once boiled | Good; weeps slowly after 2–3 days (retrogradation) |
| Wheat flour | ~2× | Similar, needs a boil and a longer cook to lose raw taste | Softer, pastier, less clean cut | Dull, cloudier | Cereal/"floury" if undercooked | Good; less weeping |
| Cornstarch + flour mix (e.g. 50:50 by thickening power) | — | Boil | Creamy with body; traditional French style | Opaque | Neutral | Good |
| Potato starch | ~0.8–1× | Lower (~60–70 °C); thickens before the boil | Softer, elastic, stringy if over-stirred | Clearer, glossy | Neutral | Loses viscosity with prolonged boiling and shear; softer after storage |
| Tapioca starch | ~1–1.25× | Lower (~60–70 °C) | Soft, elastic, stringy/"gummy" | Clearest | Neutral | Similar to potato; less gel on cooling |

Why the difference: cornstarch has small, fairly tough granules and about 25–28 % amylose, which leaks out and forms a firm gel on cooling. Root and tuber starches (potato, tapioca) have large or fragile granules that swell early and enormously, and they gel less on cooling (tapioca has less amylose). They make excellent glossy fruit fillings ([11.3](stage-1/module-11/lesson-03.md)) but a softer, slippery pastry cream that does not hold a clean edge in a tart. Wheat flour is only about 70 % starch; the rest (protein, fibre) contributes little thickening and dulls both colour and flavour, so you need about twice the weight.

The rule of thumb for substitution is standard professional practice and approximate: **1 part cornstarch ≈ 2 parts flour ≈ about 1 part potato starch**. Always confirm with a test batch, because brands differ.

### Sugar, acid and fat

| Factor | Effect | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | Raises gelatinization temperature, lowers peak viscosity and gel strength | High-sugar creams need a full boil and slightly more starch |
| Acid (lemon juice, fruit purée) | At boiling temperature acid cuts (hydrolyses) starch chains: thinner paste | Add acid ingredients after cooking, off the heat, and only once the cream has cooled a little; for lemon creams use more starch |
| Fat (butter, cream) | Coats granules, slows swelling slightly; butter firms the cold cream | Add butter at the end, off the heat, while the cream is still hot (~80 °C) so it melts in and emulsifies |
| Shear after cooking | Breaks swollen granules | Whisk cold pastry cream briefly to smooth it; do not blend it for minutes or beat it in a mixer on high |

## Cooling, storage and re-texturing

Pastry cream leaves the pan at nearly 100 °C, in a dense mass that cools slowly. It is a rich growth medium: cool it fast.

1. **Spread thin.** Pour into a shallow tray or dish lined with food film, spread to **about 2 cm** thick.
2. **Film in contact.** Press film directly onto the surface. This prevents a skin (a dried, gelled layer that makes lumps when whisked back in) and reduces condensation.
3. **Chill fast.** Refrigerate immediately, uncovered by anything else, on a rack if possible. A 2 cm layer reaches about 10 °C in roughly 1–1.5 h in a domestic refrigerator; a blast chiller does it in 20–30 min. Target: below 21 °C within 2 h and below 5 °C within 6 h at the latest (standard food-safety practice); aim for much faster.
4. **Store** at ≤ 5 °C, covered, **2–3 days** (standard professional practice). Freezing makes cornstarch creams weep and grainy on thawing; do not freeze.
5. **Before use**, the cold cream is a firm gel. Turn it into a bowl and whisk or beat with a paddle for 20–40 seconds until smooth and creamy. Stop there: prolonged beating breaks the gel and it becomes runny.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Pastry cream spits when it boils: the thick mass traps steam and releases it in hot bursts. Use a pan with high sides, whisk continuously, and wear long sleeves. Never taste from the whisk at the boil. Contains egg and milk (allergens).

</div>

## Derivatives

Pastry cream is a base. Three classic derivatives change its texture for different jobs.

| Derivative | Built from | Texture and use | Where in the course |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Crème diplomate** | Pastry cream + whipped cream (typically 2:1 to 1:1), often with a little gelatin | Lighter, creamy, holds for filling and layering; fruit tarts, mille-feuille, cakes | [R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md), here |
| **Crème mousseline** | Pastry cream + softened butter beaten in (often 25–50 % of the pastry cream weight) | Rich, stable, pipeable; fraisier, Paris-Brest | Full treatment in [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md) and [R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md) |
| **Crème chiboust** | Pastry cream + gelatin + Italian meringue | Very light, set; torched top | Stage 2 |

### Crème diplomate

The whipped cream lightens the pastry cream and makes it softer and more "creamy" on the palate. Gelatin (about 0.6–1 % of the finished weight) holds the air so the diplomate can be piped and layered without slumping.

The critical step is temperature matching. Bloomed gelatin must be melted (40–50 °C) and mixed into pastry cream that is not fridge-cold; otherwise the gelatin sets into rubbery threads the moment it touches the cold cream. Standard method: whisk the cold pastry cream smooth and let it come to about 18–20 °C, melt the gelatin, whisk a spoonful of pastry cream into it to temper, then whisk that into the rest. Fold in cream whipped to soft peaks ([12.3](stage-1/module-12/lesson-03.md)) in two additions, and use it at once: with gelatin, it begins to set within 30–60 minutes.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** In production, pastry cream is made in large batches, cooled in a blast chiller in shallow trays, and portioned for the day's use. The line cook's rule is "boil it, chill it, beat it": full boil, fast chill in a thin layer, brief beating before piping. Bakeries often write the boil time on the formula as a critical control point (e.g. "boil 2 min, whisking") alongside the cooling target.

</div>

## What goes wrong

- **Lumpy.** Starch not dispersed in sugar and yolks, milk added too fast, or not whisked through the thickening stage. See [Lumpy pastry cream](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=lumpy-pastry-cream).
- **Runny next day.** Not boiled long enough (amylase), acid added hot, or over-beaten. See [Pastry cream runny the next day](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=pastry-cream-runny-the-next-day).
- **Rubbery or pasty.** Too much starch, too much flour, or reduced by over-long cooking. See [Rubbery or pasty pastry cream](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=rubbery-or-pasty-pastry-cream).
- **Starchy taste.** Not cooked long enough after the boil, especially with flour. See [Starchy or floury taste](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=starchy-or-floury-taste).
- **Skin.** No film in contact. See [Skin on custard or pastry cream](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=skin-on-custard-or-pastry-cream).

## Check yourself

1. A colleague takes pastry cream off the heat "as soon as it is thick, to avoid curdling the eggs". It is perfect at 17:00 and runny at 09:00. Explain and fix.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Yolk α-amylase survived the gentle heating and digested the gelatinized starch overnight; the starch may also have been only partly gelatinized. Starch protects the yolks from curdling, so the cream can and must be brought to a full boil and held there, whisking, for 1–2 minutes. The runny batch cannot be rescued.

</details>

2. You want to replace the 45 g cornstarch in R1-26 with flour only. How much flour, and what differences will you see and taste?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 90 g (2×). The cream will be duller and more opaque, softer and slightly pastier, less clean-cutting, and it needs a full boil for 2 minutes or more to lose the raw-flour taste.

</details>

3. Why does pastry cream look slightly looser and glossier after a minute of boiling than at its thickest point? Is that a problem?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The starch has passed its peak viscosity: fully swollen granules soften and some fragment under the whisk (breakdown). It is the sign that gelatinization is complete and is normal. It becomes a problem only if boiling or vigorous shearing continues for many minutes, which keeps thinning it.

</details>

4. You are making a lemon pastry cream. When and how do you add 60 g lemon juice?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

After cooking, off the heat, once the boil is complete (and ideally after the cream has cooled somewhat), because acid at boiling temperature hydrolyses the starch and thins the cream. Compensate for the added liquid and the acid with slightly more starch (e.g. 10–11 % of the milk).

</details>

5. Your crème diplomate is full of rubbery strings. What happened?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The melted gelatin met pastry cream that was too cold (straight from the refrigerator) and set on contact before it was dispersed. Let the pastry cream come to about 18–20 °C, temper the melted gelatin with a spoonful of cream, then whisk it in quickly before folding in the whipped cream.

</details>
