# M09 · Eggs: coagulation, foams and emulsions

## Purpose

Eggs build structure, trap air and hold fat in suspension, and each of those jobs is done by proteins and lipids whose behaviour you can predict. This module gives you the egg in numbers (weights, water, protein, fat), a coagulation map from 60 to 85 °C that you measure yourself, a working model of foams (how proteins form films round bubbles, what sugar, acid and fat do to them, how to fold without losing air) and a model of emulsions (droplets, emulsifiers, phase volume, breaking and rescue). The practicals are deliberately simple model systems, a mayonnaise and a sabayon, so that each mechanism is visible on its own. Everything here is used again in cakes (M10), custards and creams (M12), meringues and buttercreams (M13), choux (M14) and ganache (M16).

## Objectives

By the end of this module you can:

- Convert egg counts to grams and calculate the water, protein and fat they bring to a formula.
- State the setting temperatures of white, yolk, whole egg and a milk custard, and predict how sugar, dilution, acid and salt move them.
- Whip egg white to a specified stage, verified by specific gravity, and predict the effects of sugar timing, cream of tartar and a trace of fat.
- Fold a foam into a heavier base and measure the air lost.
- Make a mayonnaise by hand, keep its water phase in proportion to its oil, and rescue it when it breaks.
- Cook a sabayon to a measured endpoint without scrambling it, and explain why it is both a foam and a cooked egg.
- Handle raw and lightly cooked eggs safely.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Albumen | Egg white: about 88 % water and 10–11 % protein, roughly two thirds of the edible egg. |
| Ovalbumin | The main egg-white protein (about half the white's protein); sets fully around 80 °C, giving a firm white. |
| Ovotransferrin (conalbumin) | Egg-white protein that begins to set around 62 °C, giving the first milky, tender gel; binds copper, which stabilizes foams whipped in copper bowls. |
| Lecithin | Common name for phosphatidylcholine and related phospholipids in egg yolk; molecules with a water-loving head and fat-loving tails that emulsify oil in water. |
| Denaturation | Unfolding of a protein's folded shape by heat, acid, salt, air–water interfaces or mechanical stress. |
| Coagulation | Bonding of unfolded proteins into a network that traps water, turning liquid egg into a gel; irreversible. |
| Syneresis | Contraction of an over-coagulated gel that squeezes out its water; the weeping of overcooked eggs, custards and overwhipped whites. |
| Tempering | Adding hot liquid to eggs gradually while whisking so they warm evenly instead of setting in threads. |
| Foam | Gas dispersed as bubbles in a liquid or solid, separated by thin liquid films (lamellae). |
| Soft / firm / stiff peak | Stages of whipped white judged by the peak on a lifted whisk: sinks back / tip curls over / stands straight. |
| Specific gravity (SG) | Weight of a volume of foam or batter divided by the weight of the same volume of water; lower SG means more air. |
| Overrun | Increase in volume from incorporated air, as a percentage of the unwhipped volume. |
| Cream of tartar | Potassium bitartrate, a dry acid used at about 1 % of white weight to give a finer, more stable egg-white foam. |
| Drainage | Liquid flowing down between bubbles of a foam under gravity; seen as liquid collecting under a meringue or mousse. |
| Ribbon stage | Endpoint of a whole-egg or yolk foam: the mixture falls from the whisk in a thick ribbon that sits on the surface for a few seconds. |
| Folding | Combining a foam with a heavier mixture using cutting and lifting strokes, after lightening the base with a sacrificed third of the foam. |
| Emulsion | Droplets of one liquid dispersed in another it does not mix with; oil-in-water (O/W: mayonnaise, cream) or water-in-oil (W/O: butter). |
| Continuous phase | The liquid that surrounds the droplets in an emulsion and determines how it behaves (water in O/W, fat in W/O). |
| Emulsifier | A molecule or particle that sits at the oil–water interface, lowers interfacial tension and stops droplets merging (yolk lecithin, lipoproteins, proteins). |
| Phase volume | The fraction of an emulsion occupied by the dispersed phase; mayonnaise can reach ~75–80 % oil because its droplets pack and deform. |
