## Why this lesson exists

Butter, cream, ganache, buttercream, creamed cake batter, hollandaise and mayonnaise are all emulsions: one liquid dispersed as droplets in another it does not mix with. Each can "split", and each split has the same few causes. Mayonnaise is the cleanest model: three working ingredients, no heat, and it fails and recovers in front of you in minutes. Learn its rules here and you will diagnose a broken ganache in M16 or a curdled buttercream in M13 by reasoning, not by memory.

## You will be able to

- Distinguish oil-in-water (O/W) and water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions and classify common pastry emulsions.
- Explain the roles of the continuous phase, droplet size, emulsifier, mechanical energy, viscosity and phase volume in emulsion stability.
- Make a mayonnaise by hand, controlling the rate of oil addition and the water-phase ratio, and explain why it can hold ~80 % oil.
- Diagnose a broken emulsion (too fast, wrong temperature, too little water phase) and rescue it.
- Handle raw-egg emulsions safely.

## What an emulsion is

An **emulsion** is a dispersion of droplets of one liquid (the **dispersed phase**) in another liquid it does not mix with (the **continuous phase**). In food, the two liquids are almost always oil (or melted fat) and water (with whatever is dissolved in it).

| Type | Continuous phase | Droplets | Feels / behaves like | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **O/W** (oil-in-water) | Water | Oil or fat | The water phase: mixes with water, not greasy on the tongue, conducts electricity | Milk, cream, mayonnaise, hollandaise, ganache, cake batter (mostly) |
| **W/O** (water-in-oil) | Fat | Water | The fat: greasy, mixes with oil, melts with the fat | Butter, margarine, some buttercreams (butter-continuous) |

A quick test: a drop of an O/W emulsion disperses in a glass of water; a drop of a W/O emulsion does not. The same ingredients can form either type depending on proportions, emulsifier and process, and an emulsion can **invert** from one to the other: whipping cream until it turns to butter converts an O/W emulsion (cream) into a W/O one (butter), with buttermilk left over ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)).

### Why oil and water separate

Water molecules bond strongly to each other (hydrogen bonds) and not to oil. Every square millimetre of oil–water boundary costs energy (interfacial tension), and the system lowers that cost by reducing the boundary: droplets merge (coalescence) until you have one layer of oil floating on one layer of water. An emulsion is therefore never truly stable; it is *kinetically* stable, held apart for hours or months by the factors below.

## What holds an emulsion together

| Factor | What it does | How you control it | Mayonnaise example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emulsifier** | Molecules with a water-loving and a fat-loving part sit at the droplet surface, lower interfacial tension, and form a film that stops droplets merging | Choose the ingredient and the amount | Yolk lecithin (phospholipids), yolk lipoproteins and proteins; mustard adds some surface-active material and fine particles |
| **Mechanical energy** | Breaks the dispersed phase into small droplets | Whisk speed, blender, rate of addition | Whisking hard as each addition goes in |
| **Droplet size** | Small droplets rise or settle more slowly and have more film surface per gram | Energy, emulsifier availability | Mayonnaise droplets are a few micrometres |
| **Viscosity of the continuous phase** | Slows droplet movement and collision (Stokes' law, [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)) | Sugar, starch, egg, cooling | Packed droplets make mayonnaise stiff |
| **Phase volume** | Ratio of dispersed to continuous phase. More droplets → thicker, until the continuous phase runs out | Proportions and rate of addition | ~75–80 % oil is possible; beyond that the water films between droplets become too thin |
| **Temperature** | Changes viscosity, fat crystallization and emulsifier behaviour | Ingredient temperatures | Oil and yolk at ~18–22 °C |

### How an emulsifier works

Lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) has a charged, water-loving head and two fatty-acid tails that dissolve in oil. At an oil–water boundary it lines up with its head in the water and its tails in the oil, like a surfactant in detergent. Yolk also contains lipoprotein particles (low-density lipoproteins) and proteins that do the same job and form a thicker, more robust film. The film lowers the energy of the boundary, so less work is needed to make small droplets, and it acts as a barrier: when two coated droplets collide, the films repel and the droplets bounce apart instead of merging.

One yolk (~20 g) contains far more emulsifier than it needs for a cup of oil. Egg yolk is so effective that the practical limit on how much oil a yolk can emulsify is set by **water**, not by lecithin: the oil droplets need a continuous water phase to be suspended in.

### Phase volume: why mayonnaise can be 80 % oil

If you pack identical spheres randomly into a box, they fill only about 64 % of the space; even perfect stacking fills about 74 %. Yet mayonnaise commonly holds 75–80 % oil. This is possible because the oil droplets are soft and of different sizes: smaller droplets fill the gaps between larger ones, and once the droplets are packed tight, further oil squeezes them into polyhedral shapes separated by very thin films of water phase. That packed, deformed structure is what makes mayonnaise **stiff and spoonable** even though it contains no thickener: the droplets cannot move past each other without deforming.

It is also why mayonnaise breaks when you add too much oil for its water: the films between droplets become so thin that they rupture, droplets coalesce, and the whole emulsion suddenly thins and turns oily. The warning sign is a mayonnaise that becomes very stiff, shiny and gel-like, and starts to feel "tight" under the whisk. Add a few grams of water or acid then, and you can keep adding oil.

## Mayonnaise as a model

The formula in [R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md) is:

| Phase | Ingredient | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Water phase + emulsifier | Egg yolk (pasteurized) | 20 |
| | Dijon mustard | 6 |
| | Lemon juice or white wine vinegar | 10 |
| | Water | 6 |
| | Salt | 2 |
| Dispersed phase | Neutral oil | 160 |
| **Total** | | **204** |

Oil is 160/204 = **78 %** of the batch, before counting the yolk's own fat. The water phase is the yolk's ~10 g water plus acid, mustard and added water: about 30 g.

### The process, stage by stage

| Stage | Oil added (cumulative) | Rate | What you see | What is happening |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Base | 0 g | — | Yolk, mustard, salt and half the acid whisked smooth, slightly frothy | Emulsifier dissolved in a small water phase |
| 2. Start | 0–30 g | **Drop by drop**, about a drop every 1–2 s, whisking constantly | Mixture thickens and turns paler and opaque; no oil visible | First droplets formed and coated. There is almost no emulsion yet to absorb new oil, so each drop must be broken up alone |
| 3. Build | 30–100 g | Thin thread (~5–10 g per 10 s), whisking | Thick, glossy, holds whisk marks | Existing emulsion is viscous enough to shear incoming oil into small droplets quickly |
| 4. Adjust | ~100 g | Add remaining acid and the water | Loosens slightly | Water phase replenished so films stay thick enough |
| 5. Finish | 100–160 g | Thread, whisking | Thick, glossy, stands in soft peaks; wobbles like a stiff gel | Droplets packed and deformed |

The slow start is the step people rush. Until an emulsion exists, each drop of oil must be broken up in a thin, watery base; add oil faster than the whisk can break it into droplets and the oil forms a separate layer that the base can no longer take up. Once a thick emulsion has formed, its viscosity helps shear incoming oil, and you can go faster.

**Blender method.** In a tall narrow jar just wider than an immersion blender head, put all the water-phase ingredients at the bottom and the oil on top. Set the blender flat on the bottom, run it at full speed for 10–15 seconds without moving it, until the emulsion forms up the jar, then lift slowly to draw the remaining oil down. It works because the blade emulsifies a small amount of oil at a time at the bottom, the equivalent of adding oil drop by drop. It is fast and reliable, and it gives smaller droplets and a stiffer, paler mayonnaise.

### Temperature

Use yolk and oil at about **18–22 °C**. Cold oil (from a cold pantry or the refrigerator, below ~10 °C) is viscous and resists being broken into droplets, and some oils start to cloud as fats crystallize; the emulsion is slow to form and breaks easily. A cold yolk thickens less readily. Very warm conditions (above ~30 °C) thin the continuous phase and make the finished emulsion more prone to separating. Mayonnaise that is later refrigerated is usually fine because the structure is already formed, but olive oils that partly crystallize in the refrigerator can damage it: crystals pierce the films. That is one reason neutral oils (sunflower, rapeseed/canola, grapeseed) are preferred for the base, with olive oil blended in for flavour at up to about a quarter of the oil.

### Why it breaks, and how to rescue it

| Cause | What you see | Why | Rescue / prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oil added too fast**, especially at the start | Never thickens; oily, thin, oil pooling | Oil not broken into droplets fast enough; no emulsion forms | Rescue below; next time drop by drop for the first 30 g |
| **Too much oil for the water phase** | Becomes very stiff and gel-like, then suddenly thins, curdled and greasy | Water films between packed droplets rupture; droplets coalesce | Add 5–10 g water or acid when it feels tight; rescue below if broken |
| **Too cold** (oil or yolk) | Slow to thicken; separates | High oil viscosity; poor droplet formation | Warm ingredients to ~20 °C; rescue with a room-temperature base |
| **Too warm** | Thin, oily sheen, weeps oil on standing | Low viscosity, faster coalescence | Keep below ~25 °C |
| **Too little emulsifier** (e.g. very large batch from one yolk with no mustard) | Unstable, separates on standing | Not enough film to cover all the droplet surface | Use one yolk per ~150–200 g oil; include mustard |

**The rescue.** A broken emulsion is oil droplets that have merged, sitting in or on a water phase. Start again with a new base and treat the broken mayonnaise as if it were oil:

1. In a clean bowl, put a fresh yolk (pasteurized) or, if you have no yolk, 10 g water or 5 g mustard with 5 g water.
2. Add the broken mixture **drop by drop**, whisking hard, exactly as at stage 2.
3. When the new emulsion is thick, add the rest of the broken mixture in a thin stream.

The new base supplies a fresh continuous phase and emulsifier; the broken mixture is re-dispersed into it. The same logic rescues a broken hollandaise (fresh yolk or a spoon of warm water), a split ganache (a little warm liquid, then emulsify, [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md)) and a curdled buttercream (correct the temperature, [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md)).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Mayonnaise uses raw yolk. Acid and salt do not reliably kill *Salmonella*. Make it with **pasteurized eggs** (in-shell or liquid yolk), keep it at 4 °C or below, and use it within 3 days. Do not leave it out more than 2 hours ([FDA-eggs]). Do not serve homemade raw-egg mayonnaise to pregnant people, young children, older adults or immunocompromised people unless it is made from pasteurized eggs. Mustard, egg and some oils (nut, sesame) are declarable allergens in many countries ([00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md)).

</div>

## Emulsions across the pastry kitchen

| Product | Type | Emulsifier / stabilizer | What breaks it | Where in the course |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Milk and cream** | O/W, 3.5 % (milk) to 35–40 % (whipping cream) fat | Milk-fat globule membrane, casein | Overwhipping cream → butter (phase inversion) | [12.3](stage-1/module-12/lesson-03.md) |
| **Butter** | W/O, ~16 % water | Fat crystal network | Melting (separates into fat and milk solids/water) | [08.1](stage-1/module-08/lesson-01.md) |
| **Creamed cake batter** | Mostly O/W with air; butter, egg and sugar | Egg yolk, milk proteins, butter structure | Cold eggs or eggs added too fast → "curdled" batter | [10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md) |
| **Ganache** | O/W: cocoa butter and milk fat in a sugar-water phase, with cocoa particles | Milk proteins, lecithin in chocolate | Too much fat relative to water phase, too hot | [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md) |
| **Meringue buttercream** | Butter-continuous with dispersed meringue and water | Butter fat crystals, egg proteins | Too cold (curdles) or too warm (soupy) | [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md) |
| **Hollandaise, béarnaise** | O/W warm: melted butter in a cooked yolk base | Yolk, partly coagulated | Too hot (yolks scramble), too cold (butterfat solidifies), butter too fast | Stage 2 |
| **Mayonnaise** | O/W, ~75–80 % oil | Yolk lecithin and lipoproteins; mustard | Too fast, too cold, too little water phase | [R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md) |

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Diagnose any split emulsion with three questions. (1) *Was the dispersed phase added faster than it could be broken up?* (2) *Is there enough continuous phase for the amount of dispersed phase?* (3) *Is the temperature right for the fats and the emulsifier?* The rescue is almost always: fresh continuous phase with emulsifier, re-add the broken mixture slowly, at the right temperature.

</div>

## On the bench

1. **Make [R1-15 Mayonnaise](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md) by hand**, logging grams of oil at each stage and the time. Note when the emulsion first forms.
2. **Break it on purpose.** With a second small batch (half the recipe), pour the first 40 g of oil in at once. Watch it fail. Then rescue it with a fresh base and record how much oil the rescued emulsion holds.
3. **Push the phase volume.** Continue adding oil to a finished mayonnaise *without* adding water and note the weight of oil at which it goes tight and gel-like; add 5 g water and feel it loosen; continue.
4. **O/W or W/O?** Drop a small amount each of mayonnaise, softened butter and cream into separate glasses of water. Note which disperse.
5. **Optional comparison:** blender mayonnaise with the same formula. Compare stiffness and colour.

## What goes wrong

- **Mayonnaise never thickens, or breaks.** See [Broken mayonnaise](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=broken-mayonnaise).
- **Greasy, oily mouthfeel or oil beading on the surface.** See [Greasy or oily emulsion](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=greasy-or-oily-emulsion).
- **Mayonnaise separates in the refrigerator.** Oil crystallizing (olive oil), or an emulsion near its phase-volume limit. Same entry.
- **Too stiff, gel-like and tight.** Phase-volume limit: add water or acid before continuing.

## Check yourself

1. Classify as O/W or W/O: butter, whipping cream, mayonnaise, ganache. Which of them can invert, and how?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Butter W/O; cream, mayonnaise and ganache O/W. Cream inverts to butter (W/O) when overwhipped; ganache can split towards a fat-continuous, oily mass when fat overwhelms the water phase.

</details>

2. Why must the first 30 g of oil in a hand-made mayonnaise go in drop by drop, while the last 60 g can go in as a thin stream?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At the start there is no emulsion: each drop of oil must be broken up in a thin, watery base, which the whisk can do only slowly. Once a thick emulsion exists, its high viscosity shears incoming oil into small droplets quickly, and those droplets are immediately coated and packed in, so the rate can increase.

</details>

3. Your mayonnaise has 170 g oil in it and has become very stiff and shiny. You want to add 40 g more oil. What do you do first, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Whisk in about 5–10 g of water or acid. The water films between the packed droplets are nearly too thin; adding water phase thickens them so more oil can be accommodated without rupture and coalescence.

</details>

4. You make mayonnaise in a 14 °C kitchen with oil stored by the window. It fails twice. What is the most likely cause, and what is the rescue?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cold, viscous oil (and probably a cold yolk) that is hard to break into droplets. Warm the oil and a fresh yolk (or water) to about 20 °C, then add the broken mixture drop by drop into the fresh base, then faster once it has thickened.

</details>

5. One yolk contains enough lecithin to emulsify much more oil than 200 g. Why can't you make 1 kg of mayonnaise from one yolk and 950 g oil without adding anything else?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The limit is the continuous water phase, not emulsifier. One yolk supplies only ~10 g of water; there is not enough water phase to separate 950 g of oil droplets. You would need to add water and acid (roughly 15–20 % of the oil weight in total water phase) to keep the films intact.

</details>
