## Why this lesson exists

The egg is the most versatile ingredient in pastry. In one product it can build structure, trap air, hold fat in suspension, add water, colour the crust and carry flavour, and it does most of these with proteins that change irreversibly with heat. Custards, cakes, choux, meringues, mousses, glazes and enriched breads all depend on knowing *at what temperature egg proteins set, and what moves that temperature*. This lesson gives you the composition in numbers, the list of jobs an egg does, and a coagulation map you will measure yourself in [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md) and use for the rest of the course.

## You will be able to

- Give the approximate weight and composition of a whole egg, white and yolk, and convert "3 eggs" in a recipe into grams of water, protein and fat.
- Name at least seven functions of egg in baked goods and identify which part of the egg performs each.
- State the temperature ranges at which white, yolk, whole egg and a diluted custard mixture set, and predict how sugar, milk, acid and salt shift them.
- Explain curdling and weeping (syneresis) as over-coagulation and avoid them by controlling temperature and time.
- Store, separate, weigh and temper eggs safely, and choose pasteurized eggs where the product is not cooked to a safe temperature.

## The egg in numbers

### Size and weight

Egg sizes are set by law and differ between countries, so a recipe that says "2 large eggs" is ambiguous by 10–20 %. In the United States, "large" means a minimum of about 57 g per egg *in the shell* (sold by the dozen); in the EU, size **L** is 63–73 g in the shell and size **M** is 53–63 g. Other countries use their own grades. The only reliable unit is the gram.

For this course, one "standard egg" is:

| Part | Share of egg in shell | Grams (for a ~56 g egg in shell) | Share of edible egg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shell and membranes | ~10 % | ~6 | — |
| White (albumen) | ~60 % | ~32–33 | ~⅔ |
| Yolk | ~30 % | ~17–18 | ~⅓ |
| **Edible egg (white + yolk)** | ~90 % | **~50** | 100 % |

In practice professional kitchens use **50 g whole egg, 30 g white, 20 g yolk** as working numbers (yolks run 17–20 g). Every formula in this course gives egg in grams; when you convert an old recipe, write down the weights, not the count.

### Composition

| Component | Water | Protein | Fat | Other | Solids |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole egg (no shell) | ~75 % | ~12–13 % | ~10–11 % | ~1 % minerals, <1 % carbohydrate | ~25 % |
| White | ~88 % | ~10–11 % | trace | ~1 % minerals and glucose | ~12 % |
| Yolk | ~50 % | ~16 % | ~30–33 % | ~1–2 % minerals, carotenoid pigments | ~50 % |

Figures are standard food-composition values ([McGee], [Figoni]); they vary a few percent with hen breed, feed and age.

What matters in those numbers:

- **The white is dilute protein in water.** Its proteins are a mixture: ovalbumin (about half the protein), ovotransferrin (also called conalbumin, about an eighth), ovomucoid, lysozyme, ovomucin (which makes the thick white thick) and others ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)). Each unfolds and bonds at its own temperature, which is why egg white sets over a range rather than at one point.
- **The yolk is an emulsion already.** About a third of its fat is phospholipid, mainly phosphatidylcholine, which cooks call **lecithin**, bound with proteins into lipoprotein particles. Those particles and the free lecithin are what make yolk an excellent emulsifier (09.3). The fat and lecithin also tenderize: they interrupt gluten and protein networks, much as fat does in [08.1](stage-1/module-08/lesson-01.md).
- **Whole egg is three quarters water.** In a formula, 100 g of whole egg brings 75 g of water. You used this in [06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md) to calculate true hydration; the same arithmetic applies to cakes in [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md).

**Worked example.** A cake recipe calls for "4 large eggs". Take 4 × 50 = 200 g whole egg. It supplies about 150 g water, 25 g protein and 21 g fat. If you replace 2 eggs with 4 yolks (80 g), you add 80 × 0.32 ≈ 26 g fat and only 40 g water instead of 75 g: a richer, more tender, more yellow, drier batter. That is a formulation move, and you can now predict it.

### Freshness and pH

A freshly laid egg white has a pH of about 7.6–7.9. As the egg ages, carbon dioxide escapes through the shell and the white becomes more alkaline, reaching pH 9 or more within a couple of weeks. At the same time the thick white thins and the yolk membrane weakens. Consequences on the bench:

| Property | Fresh egg | Older egg (2–3 weeks) |
|---|---|---|
| Thick white | Stands tall, holds together | Spreads, watery |
| Yolk | Domed, membrane strong; separates cleanly | Flatter, breaks easily when separating |
| Whipping | Slightly slower to foam, stable | Foams faster (thinner white), slightly less stable |
| Peeling a hard-cooked egg | Difficult | Easy (higher pH loosens the membrane) |

For pastry the differences are small compared with temperature and cleanliness, but they explain why the same method gives slightly different foams from week to week.

## What eggs do in baked goods

| Function | Part mainly responsible | Mechanism | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | White and yolk proteins | Heat coagulation forms a protein network that sets the product | Cakes, choux, custards, soufflés; eggs replace part of flour's structural role |
| **Leavening (aeration)** | White (also whole egg, yolk) | Proteins stabilize air bubbles in a foam, which expand in the oven | Meringue, génoise, chiffon, soufflé, mousse |
| **Leavening (steam)** | Water in whole egg | Water turns to steam and puffs a set shell | Choux ([14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md)), popovers |
| **Emulsifying** | Yolk (lecithin, lipoproteins) | Coats fat droplets and keeps them dispersed | Mayonnaise, hollandaise, creamed cake batters, buttercream |
| **Moisture** | Whole egg, white | ~75–88 % water | Batters, doughs (count it in hydration) |
| **Tenderizing** | Yolk fat and lecithin | Interrupts gluten and protein networks | Brioche, yolk-rich cakes, sablé |
| **Colour and browning** | Yolk pigments; proteins for Maillard | Carotenoids colour the crumb; amino groups react with sugars | Yellow crumb, golden crust, egg wash |
| **Flavour** | Mainly yolk | Fat-soluble flavour and richness | Custards, brioche |
| **Glaze and shine** | Whole egg, yolk, or white | Thin protein film sets and browns | Egg wash on bread, pastry, choux |
| **Binding** | Whole egg, white | Coagulating protein glues particles | Nut fillings, frangipane, crumb coatings |

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The white is mostly a *structure and foam* ingredient; the yolk is mostly an *emulsifier, tenderizer, colour and flavour* ingredient. Whole egg does a bit of everything. When you swap one for another you are shifting the product along those axes, and you can predict the direction.

</div>

## Coagulation: how liquid egg becomes solid

### The mechanism

Egg proteins in the raw egg are folded, dissolved and mostly repel each other. Heat makes them vibrate until the weak bonds that hold their folded shape break; they **denature** (unfold). The unfolded chains expose parts that were hidden inside, stick to each other and form new bonds, including disulfide bridges between sulfur-containing amino acids. They **coagulate** into a three-dimensional network that traps the water around it ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)). The liquid becomes a gel: opaque, then soft, then firm.

Push the heat further and the network keeps tightening. Proteins bond more and more closely, the network shrinks and squeezes out the water it was holding. That is **syneresis**: the rubbery white and the puddle on the plate of overcooked scrambled eggs, the weeping baked custard, the curdled crème anglaise. It is the same reaction continuing too far, not a different fault.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Native protein<br/>clear, liquid] -->|heat, acid, salt, whipping| B[Denatured<br/>unfolded]
  B -->|bonds form| C[Coagulated gel<br/>opaque, tender, holds water]
  C -->|too hot or too long| D[Over-coagulated<br/>shrinks, rubbery, weeps]
```

### The coagulation map

| Temperature | Egg white | Yolk | Whole egg (beaten) | Custard mix (egg + milk + sugar) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ~60 °C | Starting to turn milky; still flows | Liquid | Liquid | Liquid |
| ~62–65 °C | Ovotransferrin sets: milky, soft, fragile gel | Begins to thicken (~65 °C) | Thickening | Liquid |
| ~65–70 °C | Tender, opaque, just-firm gel | Thick paste, then set by ~70 °C | Soft, then set around **70–73 °C** | Slightly thicker |
| ~70–80 °C | Firming | Firm, pasty to crumbly | Firm | Thickening; stirred custards coat a spoon ~80 °C |
| ~80 °C + | Ovalbumin sets fully: firm, then rubbery | Dry, crumbly | Rubbery, weeping | Sets ~**80–85 °C**; curdles above ~85 °C |

These are ranges from standard food-science references ([McGee], [Figoni]); the exact point depends on heating rate, time and the mixture. You will see your own numbers in [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md).

Three facts from this table run through the rest of the course:

1. **Eggs never need 100 °C.** All of egg's useful cooking happens between about 62 and 85 °C. A custard, a sabayon or a curd fails by overshooting, not by undershooting boiling.
2. **White and yolk set at different temperatures.** That is why a soft-cooked egg can have a set white and a runny yolk (white ~65 °C in the outer layers, yolk below 65 °C), and why a sous-vide egg held at 63–64 °C has a barely set, milky white and a thick, jammy yolk.
3. **Time matters as well as temperature.** Coagulation is a rate process. Held at 65 °C for 60 minutes, egg sets more than at 65 °C for 10 minutes. A thermometer tells you where you are; the time you stay there decides how far the reaction goes.

### What shifts the setting temperature

| Change | Effect on setting | Why | Bench consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dilution with milk, cream or water** | Higher (custards ~80–85 °C) and softer gel | Protein molecules are further apart and meet less often | Custards need more heat than egg alone and set more gently |
| **Sugar** | Higher; more tender gel | Sugar molecules get between proteins and hold water, delaying bonding | Sweet custards tolerate a few more degrees; sabayon survives ~75–80 °C |
| **Acid** (lemon, wine, fruit juice) | Lower / faster, until very acidic | Neutralizes charges that keep proteins apart | Acidic curds and sabayon set sooner; vinegar in poaching water sets the white faster |
| **Salt** | Lower / firmer | Shields charges; proteins bond more readily | Salted egg mixtures set more firmly |
| **Starch** (flour, cornstarch) | Protects against curdling | Swelling starch thickens and gets between proteins | Pastry cream can be boiled; anglaise cannot ([12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md)) |
| **Faster heating** | Sets at a slightly higher measured temperature, but with more risk of overshoot | Less time at each temperature; the outside overheats before the inside sets | Low, even heat (bain-marie, water bath) gives smoother results |

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Proteins in solution carry electric charges; like charges repel, which keeps them apart and dissolved. Acid moves egg proteins towards the pH where their net charge is zero (their isoelectric point), and salt ions shield the charges. Either way the proteins can approach and bond at a lower temperature. Sugar and dilution work the other way: they keep proteins apart physically. This is the whole logic of custards in [12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md). See [SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md) for pH.

</div>

### Tempering

When hot liquid meets cold egg, the egg that touches it first can overheat locally and set into threads. **Tempering** means adding the hot liquid to the egg gradually while whisking, so the egg warms as a whole before the mixture goes back on the heat. For a stirred custard, pour about a third of the hot milk into the yolk-sugar mixture in a thin stream while whisking, then return everything to the pan. The sugar already whisked into the yolks raises their setting temperature and gives a margin.

### A curiosity: the grey-green ring

Hard-cooked eggs cooked too long or cooled slowly develop a grey-green layer around the yolk. Heating releases hydrogen sulfide from sulfur-containing proteins in the white; it migrates inward and reacts with iron in the yolk to form ferrous sulfide. More heat, more time and a more alkaline (older) white all make more. It is harmless, but it is a visible record of overcooking. Cook for the minimum time and plunge the eggs into ice water.

## Handling eggs: storage, safety and technique

### Safety

Eggs can carry *Salmonella*, on the shell and occasionally inside an intact egg. The U.S. FDA advises refrigerating eggs at 4 °C (40 °F) or below, discarding cracked or dirty eggs, not leaving eggs or egg dishes out of the refrigerator for more than 2 hours, cooking egg dishes until they reach 71 °C (160 °F), and using pasteurized eggs or egg products for any dish in which eggs stay raw or lightly cooked ([FDA-eggs]). Rules on washing and refrigerating eggs differ by country (US eggs are washed and must be refrigerated; in much of the EU they are not washed and are sold unrefrigerated). Follow your local guidance, and once eggs have been refrigerated, keep them cold.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** In this module, mayonnaise ([R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md)) uses raw yolk, and several samples in [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md) never reach 71 °C. Use **pasteurized eggs** for anything eaten raw or undercooked, and never serve raw-egg products to pregnant people, young children, older adults or anyone immunocompromised. Wash your hands and tools after handling shells.

</div>

**Pasteurized eggs** come in two forms: in-shell eggs heated in a controlled water bath below the white's setting temperature, and liquid whites, yolks or whole egg in cartons. Both are safe for raw use. Pasteurized whites often take longer to whip, and some carton whites whip poorly (the heat treatment partly denatures the proteins, and some contain additives); check the label for "suitable for whipping", add a little cream of tartar, and allow more time.

### Separating

- **Separate cold, whip warm.** A cold yolk is firmer and less likely to break. Separate eggs straight from the refrigerator, then let the whites warm up (below).
- **Use three bowls.** Break each egg over a small bowl, let the white fall, drop the yolk into a second bowl, and only then tip the white into the main bowl. One broken yolk then spoils one white, not the whole batch.
- **Break the shell on a flat surface**, not the bowl rim; the rim drives shell fragments into the egg.
- **Remove any trace of yolk** with a piece of shell or a clean spoon (not your finger, which is greasy). Even a small amount of yolk fat reduces foam volume (09.2).

### Weighing

Whisk whole eggs lightly with a fork to break the thick white before weighing, so the portion you pour is representative and does not slide out in a lump. Weigh whites and yolks to the gram. Surplus whites keep, covered, refrigerated for 2–3 days, or frozen for months (label with the weight); surplus yolks skin over and are best used within a day, or frozen with 5–10 % of their weight in sugar or a pinch of salt to prevent them turning gel-like on thawing.

### Temperature before use

| Use | Egg temperature | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Whipping whites | ~18–22 °C (room temperature) | Lower viscosity; proteins unfold and spread at the bubble surface faster; bigger volume sooner |
| Whole-egg or yolk foams | Warmed to ~40–45 °C over a bain-marie, or cooked (sabayon) | Warm yolk foams more; sugar dissolves |
| Creamed cake batters | ~18–22 °C | Cold eggs chill the butter, which hardens and curdles the batter |
| Mayonnaise | ~18–22 °C, like the oil | Cold oil is viscous and hard to break into droplets |
| Enriched doughs | Cold is acceptable | Helps hold dough temperature down in a long mix ([01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)) |

To warm cold whole eggs or whites quickly, stand the closed container (or the eggs in shell) in warm tap water (~40 °C) for 5–10 minutes.

## On the bench

1. **Weigh ten eggs.** Take a carton of eggs of one size. Weigh each egg in its shell, then separate and weigh white, yolk and shell for at least five of them. Calculate the mean and range for each part and the white:yolk ratio. Record the size grade and country. This gives you your own conversion numbers.
2. **Run [X1-25 Egg coagulation temperature series](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md).** White, yolk, whole egg and a custard mixture held at seven temperatures. This is the most important experiment in the module for custards later: you will see the texture at each temperature with your own eyes.
3. **Watch coagulation in a pan.** Cook one beaten egg with 10 g milk over low heat, stirring, with a probe in the mixture. Note the temperature at which it first thickens, when it forms soft curds, and when it starts to release liquid. Repeat with high heat and compare the texture and the temperature spread.
4. Keep the separated whites for [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md) (09.2); they keep refrigerated for 2–3 days.

## What goes wrong

- **Curdled or grainy egg mixture.** The mixture overshot its setting range or was heated unevenly. See [Curdled or scrambled egg mixture](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=curdled-or-scrambled-egg-mixture).
- **Rubbery, weeping egg.** Over-coagulation and syneresis: too hot or too long. Same entry.
- **Grey-green ring on hard-cooked eggs.** Overcooking and slow cooling. See [Grey-green ring around the yolk](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=grey-green-ring-around-the-yolk).
- **Inconsistent results from "the same" recipe.** Eggs were counted, not weighed. Weigh them.

## Check yourself

1. An American recipe calls for "3 large eggs and 2 extra yolks". Convert it to grams and estimate the water and fat the eggs contribute.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

3 × 50 = 150 g whole egg, plus 2 × 20 = 40 g yolk. Water: 150 × 0.75 + 40 × 0.50 = 112.5 + 20 = about 132 g. Fat: 150 × 0.105 + 40 × 0.32 ≈ 15.8 + 12.8 = about 29 g. (Egg sizes vary; weigh your own.)

</details>

2. Why does a soft-cooked egg have a set white and a runny yolk, when yolk alone sets at a lower temperature than the firm white?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Heat enters from the outside, so the white is hotter than the yolk throughout cooking. The outer white passes 65–80 °C while the yolk at the centre is still below ~65 °C. It is a heat-penetration effect, not a difference in the proteins' order of setting.

</details>

3. Crème anglaise is cooked to 82–84 °C, but whole egg alone sets at ~70–73 °C. Explain the difference and what happens at 90 °C.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The yolks are diluted by milk and sugar. Dilution spaces the proteins apart and sugar gets between them, so bonding needs more heat and the gel is softer. At 90 °C the proteins over-coagulate: they bond tightly into curds and squeeze out liquid, so the sauce curdles.

</details>

4. You hold two ramekins of beaten egg in a 65 °C water bath, one for 10 minutes and one for 60 minutes. Predict the difference.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The 60-minute sample is more set and firmer. Coagulation is a rate process; at 65 °C it proceeds slowly, so time at temperature determines how far it goes. The 10-minute sample may be thickened but still loose, partly because the sample also needed several minutes just to reach 65 °C.

</details>

5. A lemon curd (eggs, sugar, lemon juice, butter) thickens at a lower temperature than a plain custard with the same egg-to-liquid ratio. Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Acid lowers the coagulation temperature: it reduces the net charge on the egg proteins so they bond more readily. (The sugar pushes the other way, but the acid effect wins.) There is also no milk to dilute the proteins further.

</details>
