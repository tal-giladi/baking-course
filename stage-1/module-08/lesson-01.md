## Why this lesson exists

In M06 you added butter to bread dough and saw it soften the crumb. From here on, fat becomes a structural material: it traps the air in a creamed cake, forms the flakes in a scone and the layers in a croissant, and keeps a muffin tender. The same 100 g of butter does each of these jobs only if it is in the right physical state, and that state is set almost entirely by temperature. This lesson gives you the model — composition, crystals, solid fat content and plasticity — that lets you predict what a fat will do before you touch it, and the numbers you need to control it.

## You will be able to

1. State the composition of butter, shortening, lard, margarine and oil, and predict the consequence of substituting one for another (fat, water and solids delivered).
2. Explain plasticity in terms of solid fat content and give the butter temperature windows for rubbing-in, lamination and creaming.
3. Name the six jobs fat does in baked goods, the physical state each job requires, and a product in this course that depends on each.
4. Measure and log fat temperature at the moment of use, and explain why the dial temperature of the fridge is not good enough.
5. Convert a formula from butter to oil (or back) while keeping fat and water constant.

## What a baking fat is

All the fats in this module are mostly **triglycerides**: three fatty-acid chains on a glycerol backbone. Long, saturated chains stack tightly and form crystals at room temperature; unsaturated chains are kinked, stack badly and stay liquid ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)). Every natural fat is a mixture of hundreds of different triglycerides, so it does not have one melting point. It has a **melting range**, and at any temperature inside that range it is part crystal, part liquid oil.

The number that describes this on the bench is the **solid fat content (SFC)**: the percentage of the fat that is crystalline at a given temperature. You will not measure SFC in Stage 1, but you will feel it every time you press a block of butter.

### Butter

| Component | Typical amount | What it does in baking |
|---|---|---|
| Milk fat | ~80–82 % (EU minimum 82 %, US minimum 80 %) | Everything in this lesson |
| Water | ~15–17 % | Dissolves sugar in a creamed mix; becomes steam in scones, pie and puff pastry; counts toward true hydration |
| Milk solids (protein, lactose, minerals) | ~1–2 % | Browning; flavour; slightly emulsifying |
| Salt (salted butter only) | ~1.5–2 % | Flavour; adjust formula salt if you use it |

Milk fat's composition varies with season and the cows' feed, so two brands, or the same brand in January and July, can feel different at the same temperature. That is why professionals measure the butter, not the fridge.

Approximate behaviour of butter by temperature (probe in the centre of the block):

| Butter temperature | State | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| 4–7 °C (fridge) | Hard, brittle, snaps when bent; ~45–50 % solid | Rubbing-in, pie dough: pieces survive mixing |
| 10–12 °C | Firm, still cracks when sheeted | Pie dough on a warm day; too cold for lamination |
| 13–16 °C | Cold-plastic: bends like firm clay | Lamination ([15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)) |
| 18–20 °C | Soft-plastic: a finger pushes in with light pressure and leaves a clean dent; the surface is not shiny | Creaming ([08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md)) |
| 22–25 °C | Soft, greasy surface, finger slides in easily | Too warm to aerate; spreads in cookies |
| 28–33 °C | Most crystals gone; oily, slumping | Melted-butter methods (muffins) |
| ~35–36 °C and above | Fully melted, clear | Melted-butter methods, brown butter |

The whole range from "snaps" to "oily" spans only about 15 °C. Butter's plastic window — roughly 13 to 20 °C — is narrow, and a home kitchen at 24 °C is above it.

### Shortening, lard, margarine and oil

| Fat | Fat % | Water % | Plastic range | Baking character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Butter | 80–82 | 15–17 | Narrow, ~13–20 °C | Best flavour; browns; steam from its water; unforgiving of temperature |
| All-purpose shortening | 100 | 0 | Wide, roughly 15–30 °C+ | Often has emulsifiers (mono- and diglycerides) and 10–12 % gas whipped in; creams easily; very tender; no flavour; can feel waxy because it finishes melting above mouth temperature |
| Lard | ~100 | ~0 | Moderately wide | Coarse (β) crystals; very flaky pie crust; savoury flavour; not neutral |
| Baker's margarine (≥80 % fat) | ~80 | ~16–20 | Engineered: wide for creaming margarines, very wide and firm for lamination margarines | Behaves like a designed butter; flavour depends on product. Low-fat spreads (60 % fat or less) are not baking fats: too much water |
| Liquid oil | 100 | 0 | None: no crystals at bench temperature | Coats flour thoroughly; tender, moist, even crumb; cannot cream, cannot layer |
| Coconut oil | 100 | 0 | Brittle below ~20 °C, liquid above ~24 °C | Behaves like a solid fat in a cold kitchen and like oil in a warm one |

Two practical conclusions:

- **Only a plastic fat can be creamed or laminated.** Oil has no crystal network, so there is nothing to hold air cells or to form a sheet.
- **Only butter and margarine bring water.** Shortening, lard and oil do not. Substitutions change the water balance as well as the fat.

## Plasticity: why a fat can hold air or form layers

A plastic fat is a solid crystal network with liquid oil held in its gaps, like a sponge soaked in oil. It holds its shape under light pressure but deforms without cracking under more force. As a rule of thumb, fats behave plastically when roughly 15–40 % of the fat is solid ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)). Below that, the network is too weak: the fat is greasy and flows. Above it, the network is too rigid: the fat is brittle and cracks.

Crystal size matters too. Butter and good shortenings crystallize mainly in the small **β′ (beta-prime)** form, which gives a smooth fat that lines air bubbles well. Fats that melt and re-set slowly drift toward large **β** crystals: grainy, poor at creaming. That is why butter that has half-melted on the counter and been re-chilled creams badly even at the right temperature, and why you must never soften butter in the microwave to the point of liquid spots.

<div class="callout key">

**Key point:** Temperature is the main control variable for any solid fat. Measure the fat itself with a probe pushed into the centre of the block or piece, at the moment you use it. Fridge temperature, "room temperature" and "softened" are not specifications.

</div>

## The six jobs fat does

| Job | Mechanism | State the fat must be in | Where you use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tenderizing** ("shortening") | Fat coats flour proteins and starch granules, so less water reaches the proteins and gluten strands cannot link into long networks ([02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md)); in the baked crumb it interrupts the protein–starch matrix | Any; liquid oil coats most thoroughly, so it tenderizes most per gram | Muffins (R1-12), short dough ([11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md)), brioche |
| **Aeration** | During creaming, sugar crystals cut into plastic fat and drag air in; the fat-crystal network holds the air cells; chemical leavening gas later expands those cells ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)) | Plastic: butter 18–20 °C, or shortening | Banana bread (R1-13), pound cake ([10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md)), creamed cookies |
| **Flakiness and layering** | Discrete solid pieces or sheets separate dough layers; in the oven the fat melts, the water in butter turns to steam, and the gap puffs and sets | Solid pieces (4–7 °C) for flakes; cold-plastic sheets (13–16 °C) for lamination | Scones (R1-14), pie dough ([11.3](stage-1/module-11/lesson-03.md)), puff and croissant ([15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)) |
| **Moistness perception** | Fat lubricates the crumb in the mouth; oil stays liquid when cold, so oil-based cakes feel moist even from the fridge | Liquid in the finished product | Muffins, chiffon cake, carrot cake |
| **Flavour** | Butter's own flavour (short-chain fatty acids, lactones, diacetyl) plus fat-soluble aroma from spices, vanilla, citrus zest | Butter for flavour; neutral oil when another flavour should lead | All; brown butter is a Stage 2 flavour tool |
| **Heat transfer** | Hot fat conducts heat into food fast (frying), and a greased pan transfers heat evenly to a batter | Liquid, hot | Frying (not covered in Stage 1); greasing pans |

Notice that the jobs conflict. Maximum tenderness comes from oil, which cannot aerate or layer. Maximum flakiness comes from cold, hard pieces, which do little tenderizing because they barely coat the flour. Every mixing method in this module is a way of choosing which jobs the fat does:

| Mixing method | Fat state at mixing | Main jobs | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Muffin (melted fat or oil) | Liquid | Tenderizing, moistness | [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md) |
| Creaming | Plastic, 18–20 °C | Aeration, tenderizing | [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md) |
| Rubbing-in (biscuit) | Cold solid pieces, 4–7 °C | Flakiness, some tenderizing | [08.4](stage-1/module-08/lesson-04.md) |
| Lamination | Cold-plastic sheets, 13–16 °C | Layering | [15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md) |

## Temperature control in practice

Butter warms fast. A 250 g block taken from a 4 °C fridge into a 22 °C kitchen takes roughly 1.5–3 hours to reach 18–20 °C at the centre, depending on shape and air movement, and the surface softens well before the centre. Small cubes warm in 15–30 minutes. Your hands are at ~32–34 °C, which is inside butter's melting range; every second of contact melts some fat.

| To get butter to... | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| 18–20 °C for creaming | Cut the block into 2 cm slices, spread on a plate at room temperature for 30–60 min; or leave the block out 1.5–3 h | Probe 18–20 °C; finger dent with light pressure, no shine |
| 18–20 °C quickly | Microwave 5 s bursts, turning, probing between bursts; stop before any liquid appears | Same; any melted patch means β-crystal trouble later |
| 4–7 °C in pieces | Cut 1 cm cubes and refrigerate 20–30 min, or freeze 10 min | Probe a cube |
| Frozen for grating | Freeze the block ≥1 h; grate on the coarse side of a box grater, holding it in paper | Shreds stay separate on the plate |

Record in your notebook: kitchen temperature, butter temperature at use, and how long it was out of the fridge. After three bakes you will have your own warming time for your kitchen.

## Substituting one fat for another

Because fats differ in fat content, water and physical state, a straight weight swap changes the formula.

**Butter → oil, keeping fat and water constant.** 100 g butter ≈ 81 g fat + 17 g water + 2 g solids. Replace with **81 g oil + 17 g extra water or milk**. A straight 100 g swap adds about 19 g fat and removes about 17 g water: greasier, denser, more tender.

**Butter → shortening.** Same logic: 81 g shortening + 17 g water. The product is more tender, rises slightly higher in creamed cakes (emulsifiers and wide plastic range), browns less, and tastes of nothing.

**Oil → melted butter** (e.g. in R1-12): 100 g oil → 123 g butter (100 ÷ 0.81) and remove ~21 g water from the liquid. In practice most bakers swap weight for weight and accept a slightly less tender, more flavourful product; know which you are doing.

**Physical state is not interchangeable.** You cannot replace creamed butter with oil and keep the same method: there is nothing to cream. You cannot replace cold butter pieces in a scone with oil and get flakes. Change the method when you change the state.

## On the bench

Do these three demonstrations before you bake anything in this module (45 min total). They are the ones in [SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md), with measurements added.

1. **Bend and dent test.** Cut three 1 cm × 2 cm × 8 cm strips from one block of butter. Hold one at fridge temperature, one at ~15 °C, one at ~20 °C (probe each). Bend each slowly over your finger and record: snaps / bends / smears. Press each with a fingertip for 2 s and record the dent: none / clean / greasy and shiny.
2. **Creaming test.** In two small bowls, weigh 50 g butter and 50 g sugar. Bring one bowl's butter to 19 °C, the other to 26 °C. Beat each with a hand whisk or hand mixer for 3 min. Spoon each into a small tared cup of known volume (a 60 mL espresso cup), level it and weigh; compute specific gravity (mass ÷ mass of the same cup filled with water). The 19 °C mix should be paler, fluffier and lighter. You will use this measurement throughout [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md).
3. **Coating test.** Rub 20 g oil into 50 g flour; rub 20 g butter at 19 °C into another 50 g flour. Add 25 g water to each and knead 1 min. Stretch each: the oil dough is shorter and tears sooner. Fat coating limits gluten.

Then read the three recipes of this module and, for each, write which job the fat does and what temperature it must be at.

## What goes wrong

- **Butter too warm for the method.** Creamed mixtures turn greasy and dense; scones spread and lose their flakes. See [dense, heavy banana bread](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=dense-heavy-banana-bread) and [scones spread or did not rise](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=scones-spread-or-did-not-rise).
- **Butter too cold for creaming.** It breaks into lumps that sugar cannot aerate; the mixture stays gritty and heavy. Warm it, do not beat longer.
- **Melted-and-reset butter.** Looks right at 19 °C but creams poorly because the crystals regrew coarse.
- **Wrong fat swapped at equal weight.** Low-fat spread in place of butter adds water and loses fat; oil in place of butter adds fat and removes water. Recalculate.

## Check yourself

1. A recipe says "softened butter". Replace that with a specification you could measure, for creaming.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Butter at 18–20 °C measured with a probe in the centre; a fingertip leaves a clean dent with light pressure and the surface is not shiny or greasy. No melted patches.

</details>

2. Why can shortening be creamed successfully in a 27 °C kitchen when butter cannot?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Shortening has a much wider plastic range: at 27 °C it still has enough crystalline fat to form a network that holds air, while butter at 27 °C is mostly liquid (low solid fat content) and greasy. Many shortenings also contain emulsifiers and whipped-in gas that help aeration.

</details>

3. You replace 120 g melted butter with 120 g oil in a muffin formula. How much more fat and how much less water does the muffin contain? What will you notice?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Butter at ~81 % fat and ~17 % water: 120 g brings ~97 g fat and ~20 g water. Oil brings 120 g fat, 0 g water. So about 23 g more fat and 20 g less water. Expect a more tender, slightly denser, moister-feeling muffin with less butter flavour and slightly less browning.

</details>

4. Rank the fat states for maximum tenderness in a muffin and for maximum flakiness in a scone. Explain the reversal.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Tenderness: oil > melted butter > soft butter > cold pieces. Flakiness: cold (or frozen) pieces > cool butter > soft butter > melted/oil. Tenderness needs the fat spread thinly over as much flour surface as possible; flakiness needs the fat kept as discrete solid pieces that do not coat the flour, so they can leave steam-filled gaps.

</details>

5. Butter was left on a sunny counter, went partly liquid, and was re-chilled. It now reads 19 °C. Will it cream like fresh butter? Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Melting destroyed the small β′ crystal network; on slow re-cooling the fat recrystallized into larger, more stable β crystals. The fat is grainier and less plastic, so it traps less air. Use it for melted-butter methods instead.

</details>
