## Why this lesson exists

Baking feels safe because the oven kills almost everything. That is mostly true for the baked product — and it hides the real risks: the raw dough you taste, the pastry cream sitting on the bench, the almond flour in a cake served to a guest with a nut allergy, the whole-wheat flour going rancid in a warm cupboard. A professional is responsible for everything they serve. This lesson gives you the working rules for a home-based learner who intends to cook like a professional, and the reasons behind them so you can apply them to products the course has not covered.

**Warning:** This lesson is a working guide for learning at home. If you sell food, local regulations (registration, training, labelling, inspections) apply and override anything here. Check them before your first sale.

</div>

## You will be able to

1. Explain why raw flour and raw eggs are hazards, and apply the rules that follow.
2. State the temperature danger zone and cooling rules, and apply them to custards, creams and fillings.
3. Identify the major allergens used in pastry, and prevent cross-contact in a shared kitchen.
4. Wash hands and sanitise surfaces to a professional standard.
5. Store flour, yeast, butter, nuts and eggs correctly, using FIFO and labels with dates.

## The mechanism in one paragraph

Food-borne illness comes from pathogenic bacteria (and some viruses and toxins) reaching a dose large enough to cause illness. Bacteria multiply by division; in the right conditions — moisture, nutrients, neutral pH and warmth — a population can double in 20–30 minutes. Heat kills vegetative bacteria; cold slows them but does not kill them; some bacteria form heat-resistant spores or toxins that survive cooking. Every rule below does one of three things: **keep pathogens out** (clean hands and surfaces, separated raw and ready-to-eat zones), **keep them from multiplying** (time and temperature control), or **kill them** (cooking to a sufficient temperature). [SC-08 Microbiology](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md) covers the biology that also drives fermentation.

## Raw flour: do not eat raw dough or batter

Flour is a raw agricultural product. Wheat is not treated with a kill step before milling, so flour can carry pathogens from the field, including pathogenic *E. coli* (STEC) and *Salmonella*. Outbreaks have been traced to raw flour and to raw dough and batter, and U.S. food-safety guidance advises against eating raw dough or batter for that reason [FDA-flour]. The baked product is safe because baking takes the interior well above the temperatures that kill these bacteria.

Working rules:

- **Do not taste raw dough, batter or cookie dough.** Taste for salt and sweetness in the baked product.
- Treat flour like raw meat for cross-contamination purposes: wash hands after handling it; do not let flour dust drift onto finished products.
- Products eaten raw and containing flour (edible cookie dough, some no-bake fillings) need heat-treated flour. Home heat-treatment of flour is hard to validate; buy heat-treated flour for these uses.
- Keep children away from raw dough play unless the dough is made with heat-treated flour.

## Raw eggs

Eggs can carry *Salmonella*, on the shell and occasionally inside the egg [FDA-eggs]. The risk per egg is low; across hundreds of eggs in a course it is not negligible, and the consequences are serious for the elderly, pregnant people, young children and anyone immunocompromised.

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Storage | Refrigerate at ≤ 5 °C (41 °F) where eggs are sold refrigerated; do not use cracked or dirty eggs. |
| Cracking | Crack into a separate small bowl, not over the batch; wash hands after handling shells. |
| Raw or lightly cooked preparations (mayonnaise R1-15, French meringue on an unbaked dessert, mousse, tiramisu-type creams) | Use pasteurised eggs, or choose a cooked method (Italian or Swiss meringue heated to at least 70 °C). |
| Cooked custards and creams | Cooking to the endpoint (crème anglaise 82–84 °C; crème pâtissière brought to a boil) is also the safety step. |
| Tasting | Do not taste raw batter or raw egg mixtures. |

## The temperature danger zone and cooling

Most pathogens multiply fastest between about **5 °C and 60 °C (41–140 °F)**: the temperature danger zone. Food-safety practice limits the total time a perishable food spends in it.

| Rule | Standard practice |
|---|---|
| Cold holding | ≤ 5 °C (41 °F). Check your refrigerator with a thermometer; many run warmer than the dial suggests. |
| Hot holding | ≥ 60 °C (140 °F). |
| Room-temperature limit for perishable foods | 2 hours total (1 hour above ~32 °C); after that, refrigerate or discard. |
| Cooling cooked perishables | Pass through the danger zone fast: from 60 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, then to 5 °C within a further 4 hours (the two-stage cooling rule used in food service). |

How to cool fast — heat leaves through surface area:

- **Spread it thin.** Crème pâtissière goes into a shallow tray, ≤ 3–4 cm deep, with plastic film in direct contact with the surface (also prevents a skin).
- **Ice bath.** Crème anglaise goes into a bowl set over ice water and is stirred.
- **Divide it.** Two small containers cool faster than one large one.
- **Do not put a large hot mass straight into a domestic refrigerator** and close the door; it warms everything else. Cool it first (ice bath, shallow tray), then refrigerate.

### High-risk products: creams, custards, fillings

Egg- and dairy-based fillings are nearly ideal bacterial media: moist, neutral pH, rich in protein. They are also often handled after cooking (piping, filling, assembling) — the moment recontamination happens.

- Cook fully to the specified endpoint; cool fast as above.
- Keep at ≤ 5 °C; standard practice is to use crème pâtissière and similar cooked creams within 2–3 days.
- Pipe with clean bags and tools; never return leftover cream from a piping bag to the stock.
- Filled pastries (éclairs, fruit tarts with pâtissière, cream cakes) are refrigerated products. Serve cold; do not leave on a buffet for hours.
- Whipped cream and buttercreams made with cooked meringue are more robust but still refrigerated products.

## Allergens and cross-contact

An allergic reaction can be triggered by a very small amount of protein — traces transferred on a spoon, a board or flour dust. Pastry uses nearly all the major allergens.

| Allergen | Where it appears in pastry | Hidden sources |
|---|---|---|
| Gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats unless certified) | Almost everything | Malt, some starches, cross-contaminated oats, flour dust |
| Egg | Custards, cakes, enriched doughs, egg wash, meringue | Glazes, some pasta-like doughs, "brushed with egg" |
| Milk | Butter, cream, milk powder, cheese, chocolate | Milk chocolate, some dark chocolate, whey in baking mixes |
| Tree nuts | Almond flour, frangipane, praline, nut garnishes | Marzipan, nut oils, "may contain" chocolate |
| Peanuts | Peanut butter cookies, garnishes | Some oils, praline substitutes |
| Sesame | Seeded breads, tahini, garnishes | Seed mixes, some oils |
| Soy | Lecithin in chocolate, soy flour in bread improvers | Emulsifiers, margarine |

Lists of legally declared allergens differ between countries (for example the U.S. and EU lists are not identical); follow your jurisdiction if you ever label or sell.

**Cross-contact** is when an allergen gets into a food that should not contain it. Cooking does not remove allergens. Rules:

- Ask about allergies before you bake for anyone.
- Make allergen-free products **first**, on a cleaned station, with washed tools, before the allergen-containing products.
- Store nuts, sesame and peanuts in sealed, labelled containers on a low shelf so they cannot spill into other foods.
- Clean with detergent and water; wiping with a dry cloth or only sanitiser does not remove allergen protein.
- Flour dust travels. A truly gluten-free product cannot be guaranteed in a kitchen that is dusted with wheat flour; say so honestly.
- Keep an ingredient list for every product you give away, including brand-specific "may contain" statements.

## Hand washing and sanitation

| Practice | Standard |
|---|---|
| Hand washing | Warm water and soap, rub all surfaces for at least 20 seconds, rinse, dry with a clean towel or paper. Before starting, after handling raw eggs or raw flour, after touching face/phone/bin, after a break. |
| Jewellery, hair, cuts | Remove rings and watches; tie hair back; cover cuts with a waterproof dressing (a coloured one is easy to see if it falls off). |
| Surfaces | Clean first (detergent removes soil and allergen protein), then sanitise (dilute bleach or a food-safe sanitiser at the label concentration), then air-dry. Sanitiser does not work on a dirty surface. |
| Cloths | Separate cloths for raw-zone and finished-product zone; wash hot frequently. Damp cloths left on the bench are bacterial incubators. |
| Tools | Thermometer probe: wipe with sanitiser or wash between raw and cooked products. Piping bags and nozzles: wash hot, dry fully. |
| Illness | Do not bake for others when you have vomiting or diarrhoea, and for 48 hours after symptoms stop. |

## Ingredient storage

Storage protects both safety and quality. Most quality failures in storage are rancidity (fats oxidising), staling of leaveners, moisture pick-up and pests.

| Ingredient | Main risk | Storage | Typical usable life (standard practice) |
|---|---|---|---|
| White flour | Moisture, odours, pantry moths, weevils | Airtight container, cool and dry | 6–12 months; check smell |
| Whole-wheat and rye flour, wheat germ | Rancidity — the germ and bran contain unsaturated fats and active enzymes | Airtight, cool; refrigerate or freeze if not used within 1–2 months | 1–3 months at room temperature; ~6 months frozen |
| Instant/active dry yeast | Loss of activity after opening (oxygen, moisture, heat) | Sealed, refrigerated or frozen after opening | Unopened: to the best-before date; opened: ~4–6 months cold. Test activity if unsure (lesson [03.1](stage-1/module-03/lesson-01.md)). |
| Fresh (compressed) yeast | Rapid decline, mould | Wrapped, 1–4 °C | ~2 weeks; firm, crumbly, beige is good; sticky, dark or sour-smelling is not |
| Butter | Absorbs odours; oxidation; mould on surface | Well wrapped, refrigerated; freeze for longer storage | Weeks refrigerated; months frozen |
| Nuts and nut flours | Rancidity (high in unsaturated fats), pests | Airtight, cool; freeze for long storage | A few months at room temperature; ~1 year frozen. Rancid nuts smell of paint or cardboard. |
| Eggs | *Salmonella*, quality loss | Refrigerated in the carton, away from strong odours | To the carton date |
| Dairy (milk, cream) | Spoilage bacteria | ≤ 5 °C | To the date; once opened, a few days |
| Chocolate | Bloom from temperature swings; odour pick-up | 15–18 °C, dry, wrapped | Long if stable; see M16 |
| Chemical leaveners | Moisture reacts them slowly | Tightly closed, dry | Months; test baking powder in hot water (vigorous bubbling) |
| Sugar | Moisture caking; ants | Airtight | Indefinite |

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Rancidity is mainly oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (and in whole-grain flour, enzyme-driven breakdown of fats). The rate roughly doubles for every ~10 °C increase in temperature, which is why cold storage extends the life of whole-grain flour and nuts so strongly. See [SC-04 Lipids](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md) when you reach M06.

</div>

### FIFO, labelling and dating

**FIFO — first in, first out.** New stock goes behind old stock; you always use the oldest first. With labelled containers this becomes automatic.

Label every container you decant into, and every prepared item you store:

| Label field | Example |
|---|---|
| Contents | Bread flour, Brand X, 12.5 % protein |
| Date opened / made | Opened 2026-10-02 |
| Use-by | Use by 2027-03 (or "cream: use by 2026-10-05") |
| Allergens (prepared items) | Contains: milk, egg, gluten |
| Lot / batch (for experiments) | Lot on bag: L2461; or X1-04 · 70 % |

Keep the original packaging label (or photograph it) when you decant, so you still have the lot number and best-before date; your bake log refers to it.

## On the bench

1. Check your refrigerator with a probe thermometer (a glass of water on the middle shelf, measured after a few hours): target ≤ 5 °C. Record it.
2. Decant flour, sugar, nuts and yeast into airtight labelled containers. Move whole-grain flour and nuts to the refrigerator or freezer if you will not use them within a month.
3. Set up a FIFO shelf.
4. Write an allergen list of the ingredients in your kitchen and where they are stored.
5. Put a laminated card by the sink: 20-second hand wash; raw zone / finished zone.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Bread or cookies taste bitter, "paint-like" or stale | Rancid whole-grain flour or nuts | Smell before use; store cold; buy smaller quantities. |
| Dough rises slowly with an unchanged formula | Old or badly stored yeast | Date the yeast when opened; store cold; test it. |
| Pastry cream smells sour after two days | Slow cooling or recontamination | Shallow tray and ice bath; clean piping tools; use within 2–3 days. |
| Small moths or webbing in the flour | Pantry moth infestation | Discard infested stock; clean the cupboard; airtight containers; freeze new flour for a few days if infestations recur. |
| A guest reacts to a "nut-free" cake | Cross-contact from shared tools or flour | Make allergen-free products first, washed tools, separate storage; never guarantee what you cannot control. |

## Lab notebook

Record: refrigerator and freezer temperatures; storage locations; the opening date of each yeast package and flour bag; allergen list; your cooling method for creams (how deep the tray, how long to 5 °C when you first make pâtissière in M12).

## Check yourself

1. A friend wants to eat the raw cookie dough. The eggs are pasteurised. Is it safe now?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Raw flour is a separate hazard (pathogenic *E. coli*, *Salmonella*). The dough would need heat-treated flour as well as pasteurised eggs.

</details>

2. You made 1 kg of crème pâtissière at 10:00 and left it covered in the pot on the stove. At 13:30 it is at 28 °C. What went wrong and what should you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It spent about 3.5 hours in the danger zone and cooled far too slowly (the deep pot retains heat). Discard it. Next time spread it ≤ 3–4 cm deep in a shallow tray with film on the surface, cool fast (ice bath under the tray if needed), and refrigerate once below ~21 °C, aiming for 5 °C within about 6 hours total.

</details>

3. Why is whole-wheat flour stored differently from white flour?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It contains the germ and bran, which bring unsaturated fats and active enzymes. They oxidise and break down, making the flour rancid within a few months at room temperature. Cold storage slows the reactions.

</details>

4. You will bake a nut-free cake and a frangipane tart on the same day. Put the work in order and name two controls.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Nut-free cake first, on a cleaned station, with washed tools; wrap and remove it from the area before opening the almond flour. Controls: wash tools and surfaces with detergent between products; store and label nuts sealed and separately; check "may contain" labels on the other ingredients (chocolate, sprinkles).

</details>
