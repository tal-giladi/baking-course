## Why this lesson exists

Two bakers use the same formula; one gets a perfect loaf in four hours, the other a flat, sour one in six, or an over-proofed one in three. The difference is usually not the recipe but the dough temperature. Fermentation is a set of biological reactions, and their rate depends steeply on temperature. Professionals therefore treat temperature as an ingredient: they specify a **desired dough temperature (DDT)** and hit it by calculating the temperature of the water. This lesson gives you the method, shows you how to measure your own mixing friction, and sets up the thermometer skills the rest of the course depends on.

## You will be able to

1. Explain why dough temperature controls fermentation rate, and estimate the effect of a 2 °C miss.
2. Calculate the water temperature needed to reach a DDT, for straight doughs and doughs with a preferment.
3. Determine the friction factor of your mixer or hand-kneading.
4. Choose the right thermometer for a measurement and measure dough, liquid and product temperatures correctly.
5. State the internal doneness temperatures for the main product families.

## Why temperature controls fermentation

Yeast fermentation is a chain of enzyme-catalysed reactions inside the yeast cell. Like most biochemical reactions, its rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, then falls as enzymes and cells are damaged. For baker's yeast in dough:

| Dough temperature | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1–5 °C (refrigerator) | Yeast nearly dormant; enzymes and bacteria still work slowly — the basis of retarding (lesson [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md)) |
| 10–20 °C | Slow fermentation; flavour development from long times |
| 24–27 °C | The usual target for bread doughs: predictable, controllable rate |
| 30–35 °C | Fast gas production; used for final proof of some enriched doughs; flavour thinner, timing harder |
| > 40 °C | Yeast stressed; activity falls |
| ~ 55–60 °C | Yeast cells die (this happens in the oven) |

In the normal working range a small temperature change has a large effect. As a working rule, fermentation speeds up by very roughly 15–25 % for every 2 °C above target, and slows by a similar amount below. A dough mixed at 22 °C instead of 25 °C may need 25–40 % more bulk time — the difference between a finished loaf by lunch and one by mid-afternoon. Worse, temperature also shifts the balance between yeast and bacteria (flavour, acidity) and the rate of enzyme activity on starch and gluten. Hitting the dough temperature is how you make the fermentation times in a recipe *true*.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The fermentation times in a recipe are valid only at the recipe's dough temperature. Hit the DDT and the times work; miss it and every time in the recipe is wrong by an amount you can estimate.

</div>

## The DDT method

Four things set the temperature of a freshly mixed dough: the temperature of the flour, the air in the room, the water, and the heat added by mixing (friction). Everything except the water is hard to change quickly, so you use the water to steer.

The bakers' rule treats the final dough temperature as the average of the contributing temperatures, with the friction treated as one of them:

> **Water temperature = (DDT × N) − (sum of the other temperatures)**

- **Straight dough, N = 3**: flour, room, friction factor.
  Water = DDT × 3 − (flour + room + friction)
- **Dough with a preferment (poolish, levain, biga), N = 4**: flour, room, preferment, friction factor.
  Water = DDT × 4 − (flour + room + preferment + friction)

Measure flour temperature with a probe pushed into the flour bag or bin; room temperature with the same probe in air (or a room thermometer); preferment temperature in the centre of the container.

### Worked example 1: straight dough, stand mixer

DDT 25 °C; flour 21 °C; room 23 °C; friction factor 9 °C (your mixer, measured as below).

Water = 25 × 3 − (21 + 23 + 9) = 75 − 53 = **22 °C**

### Worked example 2: hand kneading

Same conditions, friction factor 3 °C by hand.

Water = 75 − (21 + 23 + 3) = **28 °C**

### Worked example 3: with a poolish

DDT 25 °C; flour 21 °C; room 23 °C; poolish 19 °C; friction factor 9 °C.

Water = 25 × 4 − (21 + 23 + 19 + 9) = 100 − 72 = **28 °C**

### Worked example 4: a hot summer day

DDT 25 °C; flour 28 °C; room 30 °C; friction 9 °C.

Water = 75 − 67 = **8 °C**

Use refrigerated water (typically 4–7 °C). When the calculation gives a temperature below what your tap and refrigerator can deliver, you have three levers: replace part of the water with the same *weight* of crushed ice (1 g of melting ice absorbs about as much heat as cooling 80 g of water by 1 °C, so a little ice goes a long way — the ice counts as part of the formula water); chill the flour in the refrigerator; or shorten or slow the mix to reduce friction.

When the calculation gives water above about 35–38 °C (a cold kitchen), use it, but do not pour very hot water directly on the yeast; mix it into the flour first. Better: warm the room or the flour.

### What the formula is really doing — and why the friction factor is measured, not looked up

The "× 3" rule is an empirical simplification. A physically exact heat balance would weight each ingredient by its mass and specific heat: water has a specific heat of about 4.2 J/(g·K), flour about 1.8 J/(g·K), so at ~65–70 % hydration the water and flour contribute roughly equal heat capacity. The room is not really an ingredient at all; it stands in for the bowl and the air exchanged during mixing. The friction factor then absorbs all the model error along with the actual mixing work.

Two consequences follow:

- **Measure the friction factor for your own setup** — mixer, speed, time, batch size and hydration. Published values are only a starting point.
- **Use °C consistently.** Many bread books give friction factors in °F. A friction factor is a temperature *difference*, so divide a °F value by 1.8 to use it in the °C formula (a friction factor of 24 °F is about 13 °C). Do not subtract 32.

### Determining your friction factor

Mix a dough exactly as you intend to mix it normally, and measure everything.

1. Measure flour, room and water temperatures (and preferment, if any) just before mixing.
2. Mix with your usual method, speed and time.
3. Immediately measure the dough temperature at its centre.
4. Friction factor = (dough temperature × N) − (sum of the measured temperatures).

**Example.** Stand mixer, 3 min on speed 1 then 6 min on speed 2, 1 kg dough at 70 %: flour 21 °C, room 22 °C, water 24 °C, final dough 26.5 °C.
Friction factor = 26.5 × 3 − (21 + 22 + 24) = 79.5 − 67 = **12.5 °C**.

Repeat on two or three bakes and average. Record the value against the mixing method in your notebook — it changes with speed, time, batch size and hydration.

| Mixing method | Typical friction factor (°C, starting estimate) |
|---|---|
| Hand mixing, short (folds, "no-knead" style) | 0–2 |
| Hand kneading 8–12 min | 2–6 |
| Stand mixer, low speed, short mix | 4–8 |
| Stand mixer, medium speed, 6–10 min | 8–15 |
| Food processor | Very high: 10+ °C in under a minute; use cold water or ice |

These are ranges from standard practice; your measured number replaces them.

## Typical DDT targets

| Dough | DDT | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lean yeasted bread (M03) | 24–26 °C | Controllable rate for a 1.5–3 h bulk |
| Preferment doughs (M05) | 24–26 °C | As above |
| Sourdough (M05) | 24–27 °C | Supports both yeast and lactic bacteria |
| Enriched doughs, challah (M06) | 24–26 °C | |
| Brioche (M06) | 22–24 °C | Keeps butter plastic and the dough manageable |
| Laminated doughs, croissant (M15) | 20–24 °C | Dough must stay cool so the butter layers do not melt |

## Measuring temperature

### Choosing a thermometer

| Type | How it works | Speed | Use it for | Do not use it for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Digital probe (thermocouple) | Junction at the tip | 1–4 s | Everything inside a product: dough, custard, syrup, bread core | — |
| Digital probe (thermistor) | Sensor near the tip | 3–8 s | Same, a little slower | Very fast-changing syrups (read late) |
| Dial (bimetal) | Coil along the lower stem | 15–30 s | Oven thermometer (the dial type made for ovens) | Dough or custard: it averages over several cm of stem and reads slowly |
| Infrared (IR) | Reads emitted infrared radiation from a surface | Instant | Surface of a stone, steel, bench, pan, chocolate on a marble | **Anything internal.** It sees only the surface. Shiny metal and water films read wrongly; the measured spot grows with distance. |
| Oven thermometer | Dial or probe left in the oven | Slow | Real oven air temperature | — |
| Candy/sugar thermometer (probe with clip, to 200 °C+) | Probe | — | Syrups, caramel (M07, M13) | — |

### Where and how to measure

| Measurement | How |
|---|---|
| Flour | Push the probe 5+ cm into the flour container. |
| Water | Measure in the weighed water just before adding. |
| Room | Probe in open air at bench height, away from the oven, windows and sunlight; wait for a stable reading. |
| Dough after mixing | Centre of the dough mass, immediately after mixing. Record in the log. |
| Dough during bulk | Centre, occasionally; the dough drifts toward room temperature. |
| Bread core (doneness) | Probe from the bottom or side into the geometric centre. |
| Custard, syrup | Tip in the liquid, not touching the pan bottom; stir while reading. |
| Stone or steel | IR thermometer on the surface. |

Check thermometers against the **ice point** (0 °C in a slurry of crushed ice and a little water) and the **boiling point** (100 °C at sea level; about 1 °C lower for every 300 m of altitude) — procedure in [X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md). A probe that reads 1 °C off is usable if you know the offset and correct for it.

## Internal doneness temperatures: an overview

Internal temperature is one of the most reliable doneness checks for many products. It does not replace colour and feel (crust colour is its own variable), but it removes guessing about the interior.

| Product | Internal temperature at the end of baking | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lean bread, hearth loaves | 96–99 °C | Below ~94 °C the crumb is often gummy; lesson [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md) |
| Sourdough, rye | 96–99 °C | Rye-heavy breads need the top of the range |
| Enriched breads (milk bread, challah, brioche) | 88–92 °C | Sugar and fat make the crumb set lower; the crust browns fast |
| Butter and sponge cakes | about 93–99 °C | Confirm with spring-back and a clean skewer (lesson [10.4](stage-1/module-10/lesson-04.md)) |
| Baked custards (crème caramel, crème brûlée) | about 78–82 °C at the centre | Wobbly centre; set further as it cools (lesson [12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md)) |
| Cheesecake | about 65–70 °C at the centre | Set on cooling |
| Stirred custard (crème anglaise), not baked | 82–84 °C | Coats a spoon; above ~85 °C it curdles ([X1-33](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md)) |

## On the bench

1. Check your probe thermometer at the ice point and boiling point ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)).
2. **Friction-factor test.** Mix 500 g flour, 350 g water, 10 g salt and 5 g instant yeast with your usual method (hand or mixer), recording flour, room and water temperatures and the final dough temperature. Compute your friction factor. If you have both a mixer and your hands, do it both ways. You can let the dough ferment and bake it as a free-form loaf, or discard it; the data is the point. (It is the same dough you will make properly in [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md).)
3. Mix a second dough with the water temperature calculated for DDT 25 °C using your friction factor, and check how close you land. Within ±1 °C is the professional target.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bulk fermentation takes much longer than the recipe | Dough temperature below DDT | Check the log: DDT actual; room temperature | Calculate water temperature; ferment in a warmer spot; extend time by observation, not the clock. |
| Dough over-ferments; slack, sour, collapses | Dough temperature above DDT (warm day, friction) | Log the actual DDT | Cold water or ice; shorter or slower mix; reduce yeast (lesson [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md)). |
| Calculated water temperature never gives the DDT | Friction factor wrong (method, batch size or °F value used) | Re-measure the friction factor | Measure for each mixing method and batch size. |
| Bread gummy even though it looked done | Removed below ~94 °C internal | Probe the core | Bake to 96–99 °C for lean bread. |
| Thermometer disagrees with another | One is off | Ice-point check | Correct for the offset or replace. |

See also [lean bread troubleshooting](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md).

## Check yourself

1. DDT 26 °C, flour 20 °C, room 21 °C, friction 10 °C. What water temperature?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

26 × 3 − (20 + 21 + 10) = 78 − 51 = 27 °C.

</details>

2. Levain dough: DDT 26 °C, flour 22 °C, room 24 °C, levain 25 °C, friction 4 °C (hand). Water?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

26 × 4 − (22 + 24 + 25 + 4) = 104 − 75 = 29 °C.

</details>

3. A book gives a friction factor of 26 °F for a spiral mixer. What value do you use in the °C formula?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

26 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 14.4 °C. It is a temperature difference, so you only divide by 1.8; you do not subtract 32.

</details>

4. You mixed at 22.5 °C instead of the 25 °C target and the recipe says 2 h bulk. What do you expect, and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Using the working rule (15–25 % per 2 °C), roughly 20–35 % longer bulk: about 2 h 25 min to 2 h 40 min. Judge the end of bulk by volume increase and dough feel, not the clock, and ferment in a warmer spot if possible. Record it; next time recalculate the water temperature.

</details>

5. Why is an IR thermometer the wrong tool for checking that a loaf is baked?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It reads only the surface, which is the crust — far hotter than the crumb. Doneness is about the core; use a probe in the centre.

</details>
