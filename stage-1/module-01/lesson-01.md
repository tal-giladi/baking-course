## Why this lesson exists

A baking formula is a ratio, and a ratio is only as good as the measurements that produce it. Measuring flour by the cup introduces errors of 20 % or more between two people — enough to turn a 70 % hydration dough into a 58 % or an 82 % one. Every professional bakery weighs everything, including liquids and eggs. This lesson makes weighing automatic, shows you where scales lie, and gives you the numbers to reason about how much error a measurement can tolerate.

## You will be able to

1. Choose the right scale for an ingredient from its quantity and the error you can tolerate.
2. Tare correctly and avoid the common weighing errors (creep, drift, off-centre loads).
3. Quantify why volume measurement fails, and convert a volume recipe to grams with stated assumptions.
4. Weigh liquids and eggs, and scale a recipe that says "2 eggs".
5. Check your scale against a known mass.

## Mass, not volume

What matters chemically and physically in a dough is the *amount* of each substance: how many grams of water per gram of flour, how much salt per gram of flour. Mass measures that directly. Volume measures space occupied, and for powders the space depends on how tightly packed the powder is — which depends on how you fill the cup.

| Ingredient | Why volume fails |
|---|---|
| Flour | Compressible powder. Sifted, spooned, scooped or tapped gives very different masses in the same cup. |
| Sugar | Granulated is fairly consistent; brown sugar depends on packing; icing sugar on sifting. |
| Salt | Crystal size differs by brand: a teaspoon of fine table salt weighs roughly twice a teaspoon of flaky kosher salt. |
| Butter | Air pockets in a cup; softness changes packing. |
| Honey, syrup | Clings to the cup; you lose a variable amount. |
| Chopped nuts, chocolate | Piece size changes packing density. |

### How big is the error for flour?

A US cup (≈ 237 mL) of all-purpose flour weighs anywhere from about **120 g** (sifted or spooned lightly and levelled) to **160 g** (scooped and packed). Published "standard" values for one cup of all-purpose flour vary between sources, typically 120–130 g, because they assume different filling methods.

Take a bread recipe that says 4 cups flour and 1⅓ cups water (≈ 316 g water):

| Filling method | g per cup | Flour (g) | Hydration = water ÷ flour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sifted, spooned | 120 | 480 | 66 % |
| Spoon and level | 130 | 520 | 61 % |
| Dip and sweep | 145 | 580 | 54 % |
| Scooped and packed | 160 | 640 | 49 % |

Same recipe, same flour: a comfortable, slightly soft dough at one end and a stiff bagel-like dough at the other. You will measure your own spread in [X1-01](experiments/X1-01-volume-vs-weight.md).

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Weighing does not just make you more precise; it makes your recipe *transferable*. A formula in grams gives the same dough in any kitchen with any operator. A formula in cups gives the same dough only for the person who wrote it, on a good day.

</div>

## Scales: resolution, accuracy and the right tool

Two numbers describe a scale:

- **Resolution (readability, "d")**: the smallest step the display shows — 1 g, 0.1 g or 0.01 g.
- **Accuracy**: how close the displayed value is to the true mass. It is never better than the resolution and often worse: a typical 1 g kitchen scale may be accurate to ±1–2 g, and slightly more at high loads.

What matters is the **relative error** on the ingredient you are weighing. A ±1 g error is 0.2 % of 500 g of flour — irrelevant — but 20 % of 5 g of yeast and 100 % of 1 g of yeast.

| Ingredient quantity | Relative error from ±1 g | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| 500 g flour | 0.2 % | Main scale (1 g) |
| 50 g sugar | 2 % | Main scale (1 g) |
| 10 g salt | 10 % | Precision scale (0.1 g or 0.01 g) |
| 5 g baking powder | 20 % | Precision scale |
| 2 g instant yeast | 50 % | Precision scale |

The rule: **use the precision scale for anything under about 20 g**, and always for yeast, salt, chemical leaveners, gelatine, spices and colourings. A 0.01 g jewellery-type scale with 100–200 g capacity is cheap and adequate; a 0.1 g scale with 500 g capacity is more convenient for salt and sugar. Check your specifications against [Essential equipment](equipment/essential.md).

### Using a scale correctly

| Practice | Why |
|---|---|
| Place on a hard, level surface, away from drafts, the oven and the running mixer | Slope, vibration, heat and air currents cause drift. Precision scales are affected by a draft from an open window. |
| Switch on with nothing on it; wait for a stable zero | Many scales zero themselves at power-on. |
| **Tare**: place the empty container, press tare, confirm the display reads 0 | You weigh only the ingredient. |
| Load near the centre | Off-centre loads read differently on some scales. |
| Add slowly near the target, and watch for **creep** | Some kitchen scales ignore very small, slow additions (a sprinkle of salt) because their auto-zero tracking treats slow change as drift. If the display does not move when you add a pinch, lift and replace the container, or use the precision scale. |
| Watch for auto-off | Many scales switch off after 1–3 minutes; when you switch back on, the tare is lost and the bowl reads zero. Write down cumulative weights when you add several ingredients to one bowl. |
| Do not exceed capacity | Readings near or above capacity are unreliable ("O-Ld" or "Err"). A heavy mixer bowl plus dough can exceed 5 kg. |
| Replace weak batteries | Low batteries cause drift and random readings before the low-battery warning appears. |

**Weighing several ingredients into one bowl (cumulative weighing).** Tare the bowl, add flour to 500 g, tare, add water to 350 g, and so on. It is fast, but a single overshoot cannot be removed once mixed in. For experiments and anything with small quantities, weigh each ingredient into its own container (mise en place, [00.2](stage-1/module-00/lesson-02.md)).

**Removing an overshoot.** If you overshoot a dry ingredient in its own container, spoon out the excess. If you overshoot flour or water in the mixing bowl, either accept it and record the actual weight, or scale up the other ingredients to match (lesson [01.2](stage-1/module-01/lesson-02.md) shows how). Never guess.

### Checking the scale

A scale can be wrong and consistent. Check it:

- **Known masses.** Calibration weights (inexpensive class M or F weights) are best. Coins are a convenient substitute because mints publish their masses — for example the U.S. 5-cent coin is specified at 5.000 g and the €1 coin at 7.50 g. Look up your own currency's specifications and weigh 10 identical coins together to reduce the effect of resolution.
- **Water.** At room temperature 1 mL of water has a mass of very nearly 1 g (0.998 g at 20 °C). A 500 mL volumetric flask is accurate; a kitchen measuring jug is not accurate enough to check a scale with.

The full procedure is in [X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md). A scale that reads more than ~1 % off at 500–1000 g should be recalibrated (if it supports it) or replaced.

## Weighing liquids

Weigh water, milk and other liquids on the scale, in grams. It is faster (no reading a meniscus), more accurate, and it means every ingredient in a formula is in the same unit, so baker's percentages work directly.

| Liquid | Approximate density (g/mL) | 250 mL weighs |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 1.00 | 250 g |
| Whole milk | 1.03 | ≈ 258 g |
| Single/whipping cream | ≈ 1.00 | ≈ 250 g |
| Vegetable oil | 0.92 | ≈ 230 g |
| Honey | ≈ 1.4 | ≈ 350 g |
| Golden/glucose syrup | ≈ 1.4 | ≈ 350 g |

For honey and syrups, weigh directly into the bowl: pouring from a measuring cup loses whatever clings to it.

## Weighing eggs

Eggs are sold by grade, and grades cover a range. A US "large" egg is on average about 57 g in the shell and about **50 g out of the shell** (roughly 30–33 g white and 17–19 g yolk). In the EU and UK a "large" (L) egg is 63–73 g in the shell. A recipe that says "3 eggs" therefore specifies anywhere from about 130 to 180 g of egg, depending on the eggs and the country — a ±15 % variation in a major liquid and structure ingredient of a cake.

Professional practice:

- **Formulas give egg in grams**, separately for whole egg, yolk and white when relevant.
- Crack eggs into a bowl, whisk briefly to homogenise white and yolk, then weigh the amount you need. Store the remainder covered in the refrigerator and use within 1–2 days (lesson [00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md)).
- When converting a recipe that says "N eggs", use 50 g per whole large egg (without shell), 18 g per yolk and 32 g per white, unless the recipe states otherwise, and write down the assumption.

## Converting a volume recipe to grams

Use the conversion table below, **state your assumptions** in the formula sheet, and after the first bake adjust from observation (the dough or batter consistency tells you whether the author's cup was heavier or lighter).

| Ingredient | 1 US cup (≈ 237 mL) | 1 tablespoon (≈ 15 mL) | 1 teaspoon (≈ 5 mL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour, spoon and level | 125 g (range 120–140) | 8 g | — |
| Bread flour, spoon and level | 130 g | 8 g | — |
| Granulated sugar | 200 g | 12.5 g | 4 g |
| Brown sugar, packed | 210–220 g | 13 g | — |
| Icing sugar, spooned | 115–125 g | 7.5 g | — |
| Butter | 227 g | 14 g | — |
| Water | 237 g | 15 g | 5 g |
| Milk | 244 g | 15 g | 5 g |
| Table salt (fine) | — | 18 g | 6 g |
| Kosher salt, flaky brands | — | 8–15 g | 3–5 g (brand-dependent) |
| Instant yeast | — | 9 g | 3 g |
| Baking powder | — | 12–14 g | 4–4.5 g |
| Baking soda | — | 17–18 g | 5.5–6 g |

These are working values from standard practice; individual products differ. The salt row is the reason never to substitute one salt for another by volume.

### Worked example

A family recipe: 2¼ cups all-purpose flour, 1 cup butter, ¾ cup granulated sugar, ¾ cup packed brown sugar, 2 eggs, 1 tsp baking soda, 1 tsp table salt.

| Ingredient | Volume | Assumption | Grams |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour | 2¼ cups | 125 g/cup | 281 g |
| Butter | 1 cup | 227 g/cup | 227 g |
| Granulated sugar | ¾ cup | 200 g/cup | 150 g |
| Brown sugar, packed | ¾ cup | 213 g/cup | 160 g |
| Eggs, large | 2 | 50 g each, out of shell | 100 g |
| Baking soda | 1 tsp | 5.5 g/tsp | 5.5 g |
| Table salt | 1 tsp | 6 g/tsp | 6.0 g |

Note the assumptions in the formula sheet. If the dough spreads much more than expected, the author probably used a heavier cup of flour — the next bake tests 140 g/cup.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dough much stiffer or slacker than a recipe describes | Volume conversion wrong for your filling method | Weigh your own "cup" (X1-01) | Use grams; adjust water by feel on the first bake and record it. |
| Bread rises very fast or very slowly with no other change | Yeast weighed on a 1 g scale | Re-weigh the same yeast on the precision scale | Weigh yeast, salt and leaveners on the precision scale. |
| Cake texture varies between bakes of the same recipe | Eggs counted, not weighed | Weigh eggs from several cartons | Specify and weigh egg in grams. |
| Salty or bland bread after changing salt brand | Salt measured by volume | Weigh one teaspoon of each salt | Always weigh salt. |
| Scale display "stuck" when adding a pinch | Auto-zero creep | Add a known 1 g mass slowly and watch | Lift and replace the container; use the precision scale. |

## Check yourself

1. You need 1.4 g instant yeast. Your only scale has 1 g resolution. What is the worst-case error, and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A 1 g scale shows 1 or 2 g for 1.4 g, and the true value behind a "1" display may be anywhere from about 0.5 to 1.5 g: an error of roughly −64 % to +7 %, worse with accuracy error. Use a precision scale. Without one, weigh a larger amount (say 10 g), divide it visually into equal parts on a clean surface, or make the dough in a larger batch — but buy the precision scale.

</details>

2. Why is weighing water better than using a measuring jug, if 1 mL of water weighs 1 g anyway?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Jug graduations are coarse and reading the meniscus is error-prone (±5–10 mL is common); the scale reads to 1 g. And weighing keeps every ingredient in the same unit, so you can weigh into the bowl on the same scale and compute baker's percentages directly.

</details>

3. A recipe says "3 large eggs". You are in Europe and your L eggs average 58 g out of the shell. How much egg should you use?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

If the recipe is American, "large" means about 50 g out of shell, so 3 × 50 = 150 g. Three of your eggs give 174 g, 16 % more. Weigh 150 g of whisked egg. Record the assumption.

</details>

4. Your scale reads 502 g for a 500 g calibration mass and 1006 g for 1000 g. Is it good enough for flour? For salt?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The error is 0.4–0.6 %, fine for flour and water. For salt the relative error of a 1 g-resolution scale on ~10 g (±5–10 %) matters far more than the calibration error — use the precision scale for salt regardless.

</details>
