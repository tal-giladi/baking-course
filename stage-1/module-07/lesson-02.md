## Why this lesson exists

Colour is the first thing anyone judges in a baked product, and it is also a flavour: the roasted, nutty, toffee and bitter notes of crusts, cookies and caramel all come from browning reactions. In [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md) you met browning as something that happens to a bread crust. Here you take control of it: you learn to tell the two browning families apart by what they need, predict which levers move each one, and cook sugar on purpose, to a chosen colour, without crystallizing or burning it and without burning yourself. Caramel is also your first encounter with **hot sugar**, which you will use again in custards, syrups, meringues and nougatine.

## You will be able to

1. Distinguish Maillard browning from caramelization by reactants, temperature range, pH sensitivity and flavour, and predict which dominates in a given product.
2. Use four levers (sugars and proteins at the surface, surface dryness, temperature, pH) to make a product browner or paler on purpose.
3. Make a dry caramel and a wet caramel to a target colour, and explain what glucose syrup, stirring and washing down the sides do to crystallization.
4. Stop a caramel at the right colour and deglaze it with cream to make a smooth sauce of controlled thickness.
5. Work safely with sugar at 170–190 °C and know the first-aid response to a sugar burn.

## Two ways to make brown

| | Maillard reaction | Caramelization |
|---|---|---|
| Reactants | Reducing sugar + amino group (protein, peptides, free amino acids) | Sugar alone |
| Where it is fast | Dry surfaces above ~140 °C; visible slowly from ~100–120 °C over long times | Sucrose ~160–180 °C; fructose from ~110 °C; glucose ~160 °C |
| Water | Needs a little; fastest at intermediate aw (~0.6–0.8), stalled by a wet surface | Proceeds once water is driven off; in a syrup, only after the water has boiled away |
| pH | Much faster at higher (alkaline) pH | Occurs across pH; acid first inverts sucrose |
| Flavours | Roasted, malty, bready, nutty, meaty; hundreds of compounds | Caramel, butterscotch, then bitter and acrid |
| Where you meet it | Bread crust, cookie and cake surfaces, egg wash, milk-based caramels, dulce de leche | Caramel, praline, crème caramel, sugar crusts, the edges of very sweet cookies |

In most baked goods **both happen at once**. A cookie edge contains reducing sugars (from brown sugar, invert, flour), proteins (flour, egg, milk solids in butter) and sucrose. The Maillard reaction starts first, as soon as the surface dries and passes ~140 °C; caramelization joins in where the sucrose-rich surface gets hotter still. A caramel made from pure sucrose and water is the rare case of caramelization alone, until you add cream and butter, whose milk proteins and lactose then add Maillard notes as the sauce simmers.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Neither reaction has a sharp "on" temperature. Both are chemical reactions whose rate climbs steeply with temperature, roughly doubling for each 10 °C. The numbers above are the temperatures at which browning becomes fast enough to matter in a normal bake time. Hold a sugary product for hours at 100 °C and it still browns, which is how dulce de leche works ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)).

</div>

## The four levers on the bench

| Lever | To brown more | To brown less | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reactants at the surface** | More reducing sugar (honey, invert, glucose, brown sugar, lactose/milk powder), more protein (egg wash, milk) | Sucrose only, less protein, water wash | Honey cookies darken faster than all-sucrose cookies ([X1-19](experiments/X1-19-sugar-type.md)); egg wash browns, water wash does not |
| **Surface dryness** | No steam, fan on, thin product, no cover | Steam, cover or tent with foil, higher humidity | Lean bread stays pale while steam is present ([X1-10](experiments/X1-10-steam.md)) |
| **Temperature and time** | Hotter oven, longer bake, dark pan, higher rack near the element | Lower oven, lighter pan, shorter bake, air-cushion or double tray | Enriched dough baked at 175–190 °C instead of 230 °C ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)) |
| **pH** | Alkaline: baking soda in the dough, soda or lye dip | Acid: lemon juice, buttermilk, cream of tartar, sourdough | Pretzels (lye dip) turn mahogany; soda cookies brown more than powder cookies |

**A worked prediction.** You switch R1-11 from baking soda to an equal leavening power of baking powder. Powder contains its own acid and leaves the dough near neutral; soda alone raises the pH. Prediction: paler cookies with less toffee flavour, less spread (proteins set faster at lower pH), puffier and cakier. You will see the pH effect again in [08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md).

<div class="callout warn">

**Acrylamide:** Very dark browning of starchy foods forms acrylamide, a probable human carcinogen, from the amino acid asparagine and reducing sugars ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)). The practical rule for Stage 1: bake cookies, crackers and crusts to golden brown, not dark brown, and do not use high-pH shortcuts to force colour you could get from time and temperature.

</div>

## Caramel: cooking sugar on purpose

A caramel is sucrose heated past its decomposition range until it turns brown and develops flavour. As it cooks, sucrose splits into glucose and fructose, which dehydrate and link into hundreds of compounds: volatile aroma molecules (the buttery, fruity, toasty notes), acids that make the caramel slightly sour, and brown polymers that give colour and, at the end, bitterness. The longer and hotter it cooks, the less sweet and the more bitter it becomes.

Sucrose has no clean melting point: it starts to decompose as it melts, so the "melting point" you will read varies with how fast it is heated (roughly 160–190 °C). What you see in the pan is sugar liquefying and browning at almost the same time.

### Dry caramel and wet caramel

| | Dry caramel | Wet caramel |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Sugar alone in the pan, melted directly | Sugar dissolved in water (~25–30 % of sugar weight), boiled until the water is gone and it colours |
| Speed | Fast (5–10 min for 200 g) | Slower (12–20 min): the water has to boil off first |
| Control | Harder: melts unevenly, hot spots burn while other crystals are still solid | Easier and more even; colour arrives gradually |
| Crystallization risk | Low if you melt it in stages; lumps usually melt | Higher: the syrup is supersaturated as the water boils off, and one seed crystal can set the whole pan grainy |
| Use | Quick sauces, praline, nougatine, when you are confident | Learning, large batches, precise colour; when you want to add glucose syrup |

### Colour stages

Judge caramel by colour, confirmed on white paper or a white plate, with temperature as a guide. A thermometer in a thin layer of caramel reads unevenly, and colour runs ahead of the reading at the edges.

| Approx. temperature | Colour | Flavour | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~160 °C (320 °F) | Pale straw | Sweet, faintly caramel | Spun sugar, light glazes |
| ~165–170 °C (330–340 °F) | **Light amber** (honey) | Sweet, mild caramel | Crème caramel lining, light sauces, praline for pale nut pastes |
| ~175–180 °C (345–355 °F) | **Medium amber** (copper) | Full caramel, balanced | Standard caramel sauce, nougatine |
| ~185–190 °C (365–375 °F) | **Dark amber** (mahogany) | Deep, bitter-sweet | Dark caramel for mousses, ice cream, salted caramel with character |
| > ~190 °C (375 °F) | Very dark brown, smoking | Bitter, acrid, burnt | Too far; discard |

### Why caramels crystallize, and how to stop it

Crystallization is the main failure of wet caramel ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)). As the water boils off, the sucrose solution becomes **supersaturated**: it holds more sugar than it could at equilibrium, and it wants to crystallize. Crystals need a **seed** (a nucleus) to start from. Once one forms, it grows, the growth releases more nuclei, and within seconds the pan can turn opaque and sandy.

| Source of seeds or trigger | Control |
|---|---|
| Undissolved sugar crystals in the pan | Dissolve the sugar completely, stirring, *before* the syrup boils |
| Crystals splashed on the pan walls that dry and fall back in | Wash the walls down with a pastry brush dipped in water, or cover the pan for 1–2 min at the boil so condensation washes them down |
| Stirring a supersaturated syrup | Once it boils, stop stirring. Swirl the pan to even the colour |
| A dirty spoon, thermometer or pan (sugar dust, fat, crumbs) | Clean equipment; the thermometer goes in hot water between uses |
| Pure sucrose with nothing to interfere | Add an **interfering agent**: glucose syrup at ~10–20 % of the sugar weight, or a little acid (a few drops of lemon juice), which inverts some sucrose to glucose + fructose. Different molecules get in the way of sucrose molecules lining up into a crystal |

A dry caramel usually looks lumpy partway through; the lumps are sugar that melted, partly crystallized, and will melt again with gentle stirring and heat. That is not the same as a failed wet caramel.

### Stopping the cooking

A caramel keeps cooking after you take it off the heat: the pan holds heat, and 5–10 °C more is common in a heavy pan. Three ways to stop it:

1. **Deglaze.** Add warm liquid (cream, butter, fruit purée). The caramel cools instantly and becomes a sauce. Most caramels in pastry end this way.
2. **Dip the base.** Dip the bottom of the pan in a bowl of cold water for 2–3 seconds. Only with a pan that can handle thermal shock (stainless steel, not enamelled cast iron), and keep your hands clear of the steam.
3. **Pour out.** Pour onto a silicone mat or oiled marble for shards and praline, or into moulds for crème caramel.

Anticipate: take the pan off the heat when the caramel is **one shade lighter** than your target.

### Caramel sauce

Caramel sauce is a caramel diluted with cream and enriched with butter. The standard educational ratio in [R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md) is **200 g sugar : 200 g cream (35 %) : 40 g butter : 1.5 g salt**. The cream must be **warm (60–80 °C)**: cold cream drops the caramel below the temperature at which it can stay liquid, and part of it sets into a hard lump (it "seizes"). The lump is not ruined. Return the pan to low heat and stir until it dissolves back into the cream. When the cream goes in, the mixture boils violently and releases a burst of steam; add it in three parts, off the heat, standing back.

The sauce's **final temperature** sets its thickness. After deglazing, bring it back to a simmer:

| Final sauce temperature | Consistency when cool |
|---|---|
| ~103–105 °C | Pourable sauce, coats a spoon |
| ~106–108 °C | Thick, spoonable, holds a drip on ice cream |
| ~110–112 °C | Very thick; close to a soft caramel spread |

The mechanism is simple: every degree above 100 °C means more water boiled off and a more concentrated sugar solution ([13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md) formalizes the link between boiling point and concentration).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Sugar at 170 °C causes severe burns. It sticks to skin and keeps burning, and it is hot enough to melt some plastics and crack glass. Before you start:
- Wear **long sleeves** and closed shoes; tie back hair; no one else (especially children) near the stove.
- Use a **deep, heavy pan** (at least 3× the volume of the sugar) with a long handle turned inward; a heatproof silicone spatula or wooden spoon; never a plastic one.
- Have a **bowl of ice water** within reach. If caramel touches skin, plunge the area in the ice water or hold it under cold running water, and keep cooling for 20 minutes. Do not try to peel off stuck sugar. Seek medical care for any burn larger than a coin, or on the face, hands or joints.
- **Warm the cream** before it goes in, add it off the heat in three parts, and stand back from the steam.
- Never taste caramel from the spoon. Drip it onto a plate and wait until it has cooled.

</div>

## On the bench

Make [R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md): a small dry caramel poured into shards, a wet caramel taken to three colours (drip samples on paper), and one batch of caramel sauce. Then:

1. **Colour ladder.** Label the paper drips with the temperature and colour name. Taste each after it has cooled: note where sweetness disappears and bitterness appears. This is your personal colour reference; photograph it next to a white card in daylight.
2. **Crystallization demonstration (optional, 10 min).** Make a second, 100 g wet caramel with no glucose syrup. Once it boils, stir it vigorously. Watch what happens and compare with the undisturbed caramel. You will study this properly in [X1-36](experiments/X1-36-sugar-crystallization.md).
3. **Four toasts.** If you did not in SC-07, brush bread slices with water, milk, 1 % soda solution and egg, and toast them together. This is the Maillard lever table in 5 minutes.

## What goes wrong

- **The wet caramel turned white and sandy.** Seeds from undissolved sugar, the walls, or stirring after the boil. See [caramel crystallized or grainy](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=caramel-crystallized-or-grainy).
- **The caramel tastes bitter and acrid.** It went past ~190 °C, often by carry-over after you took it off the heat. See [caramel burnt or bitter](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=caramel-burnt-or-bitter).
- **A hard lump formed when the cream went in.** The cream was cold. See [caramel sauce seized when cream added](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=caramel-sauce-seized-when-cream-added).
- **Cookies or cakes are pale even at the right bake time.** Not enough reducing sugar, too low pH, or a wet surface. See [pale cookies](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=pale-cookies).

## Check yourself

1. A brioche and a lean baguette are both baked to the same crust colour. Why does the brioche need about 190 °C while the baguette needs about 240 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The brioche surface carries far more browning reactants: reducing sugars (from added sugar inverted by yeast, and lactose), egg and milk proteins, plus the egg wash. Maillard browning runs much faster, so it reaches the target colour at a lower temperature, and at 240 °C it would burn before the inside baked. The lean baguette has little free sugar and needs a hotter, dry surface to colour in the bake time.

</details>

2. Your wet caramel crystallizes every time at about 130 °C. Give three changes, in order of what you would try first.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Dissolve all the sugar, stirring, before it boils, and stop stirring once it boils; swirl instead. (2) Wash down the sides with a wet brush, or cover the pan for 1–2 minutes at the boil. (3) Add glucose syrup at 10–20 % of the sugar weight (or a few drops of lemon juice). Also check the pan and tools are clean and the sugar is not contaminated.

</details>

3. Why must the cream for a caramel sauce be warm, and what do you do if the sauce seizes anyway?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cold cream chills part of the 175–180 °C caramel so abruptly that it solidifies into a hard glassy lump before it can dissolve in the cream, and the temperature difference makes the steam burst more violent. If it seizes, put the pan back on low heat and stir; the lump dissolves in the hot cream within a few minutes.

</details>

4. You want a darker cookie without raising the oven temperature. Name two formula changes and one process change, and what else each will change.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Formula: replace part of the white sugar with brown sugar or a little honey/invert (more reducing sugar; also softer and more spread); raise the baking soda slightly (higher pH; also more spread and a risk of soapy flavour); add 2–3 % milk powder (lactose and protein; also slightly more tender). Process: rest the dough 24–72 h in the refrigerator (enzymes release more reducing sugars and amino acids, the surface is drier; also less spread and more toffee flavour), or use a darker pan (more bottom colour, risk of burnt bottoms).

</details>

5. A caramel sauce was taken to 104 °C and is too runny after chilling. How do you fix it, and why does that work?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Return it to the pan and simmer, stirring, to about 106–108 °C. Boiling off more water raises the sugar concentration, and a more concentrated sugar solution is more viscous when cool. The boiling point is a measure of that concentration.

</details>
