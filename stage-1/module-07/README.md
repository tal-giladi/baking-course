# M07 · Sugar, sweeteners and browning; cookies

## Purpose

Up to M06 sugar was a minor ingredient: yeast food and a little colour. In this module it becomes the main variable. You learn what the sugars and syrups on a pastry shelf are made of, what sugar does besides sweeten (tenderize, hold water, lower water activity, brown, spread, aerate, crystallize), how the two browning reactions differ, and how to cook sugar safely into caramel. Then you put it together in the drop cookie, a small, fast, sensitive system that you use as your first real test bench for variable control: three experiments, including your first factorial design. After M07 you can make caramel, caramel sauce and a consistent cookie on purpose, and explain why a change in sugar changes much more than the sweetness.

## Objectives

By the end of M07 you can:

1. Identify granulated, caster, icing and brown sugars, invert sugar, glucose syrup (by DE), honey, maple and malt by composition and choose between them for a stated purpose.
2. Explain the functions of sugar in baked goods (tenderizing, moisture retention, water activity, browning, spread, aeration, yeast food, crystallization) and predict the effect of a ±20 % sugar change on a formula.
3. Distinguish Maillard browning from caramelization and use the four browning levers (reactants, dryness, temperature, pH) to control colour.
4. Make dry and wet caramel to a target colour without crystallization, and a caramel sauce of specified thickness, working safely with hot sugar.
5. Explain cookie spread as a race between flow and set, and predict the effect of fat state, sugar amount and type, flour, egg, leavening, rest, portion, oven and tray.
6. Bake the R1-11 baseline cookie to specification, and design and analyse a 2 × 2 factorial experiment.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Sucrose | Table sugar: a disaccharide of glucose and fructose. Not a reducing sugar until it is split. |
| Reducing sugar | A sugar with a free reactive group that takes part in Maillard browning: glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose. |
| Invert sugar | Sucrose split into equal parts glucose and fructose, by acid, heat or the enzyme invertase. Sweeter, more hygroscopic and faster-browning than sucrose. |
| Glucose syrup | A syrup of glucose, maltose and longer chains made by partly breaking down starch; used to prevent crystallization and add body. |
| Dextrose equivalent (DE) | A measure of how far starch has been broken down in a glucose syrup (pure glucose = 100). Higher DE = sweeter, more hygroscopic, less viscous. |
| Brown sugar | Sucrose with ~3–7 % molasses: brings moisture, invert sugar, minerals and slight acidity. |
| Hygroscopicity | How strongly a substance attracts and holds water. For sugars: fructose > invert/honey > glucose > sucrose > lactose. |
| Water activity (aw) | How available the water in a food is, from 0 to 1. Dissolved sugar lowers it, which slows microbes, drying and staling. |
| Tenderizing | Sugar (and fat) weakening structure: sugar competes for water and delays gluten formation, starch gelatinization and protein coagulation. |
| Maillard reaction | Browning reaction between reducing sugars and amino groups; fast on dry surfaces above ~140 °C and at higher pH. |
| Caramelization | Browning of sugar heated alone; for sucrose from ~160 °C. |
| Wet caramel | Caramel made by dissolving sugar in water (~25–30 % of the sugar) and boiling until the water is gone and it colours. |
| Dry caramel | Caramel made by melting sugar alone in the pan. |
| Supersaturation | A solution holding more dissolved sugar than it could at equilibrium; it crystallizes if seeded. |
| Interfering agent | An ingredient (glucose syrup, invert, acid, fat) that hinders sucrose from crystallizing. |
| Deglazing | Adding liquid to a hot caramel or pan to stop cooking and dissolve it into a sauce. |
| Spread ratio | Cookie diameter ÷ thickness; the standard way to quantify spread. |
| Amorphous sugar | Sugar in a non-crystalline state (syrup or glass). Plasticized by water it is chewy; dry it is glassy and crisp. |
| Factorial design | An experiment that combines every level of two or more factors, so interactions between them can be measured. |
| Interaction | When the effect of one factor depends on the level of another (e.g. resting reduces spread more for melted-butter dough). |
