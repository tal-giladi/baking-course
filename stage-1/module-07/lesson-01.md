## Why this lesson exists

Most home bakers think of sugar as the sweet ingredient, so when a recipe is "too sweet" they cut it by a third and are surprised when the cookies come out pale, domed, dry and stale by the next day. In a baked product sugar is a structural and water-controlling ingredient first and a sweetener second. This lesson gives you a working model of what sugar does besides taste sweet, what distinguishes the sugars you will buy, and how to predict what happens when you change the amount or the type. It is the basis for the cookie system in [07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md), the cake formula balance in [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md) and the syrups in [13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md).

## The sugars on your shelf

All the sugars below are carbohydrates ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)). The ones that matter in baking are **sucrose** (table sugar, a disaccharide of glucose and fructose), its two halves **glucose** and **fructose**, **maltose** (two glucoses, from starch), and **lactose** (from milk). A **reducing sugar** has a free reactive group that takes part in Maillard browning. Glucose, fructose, maltose and lactose are reducing; sucrose is not, until it is split (inverted) by acid, heat or the enzyme invertase.

| Product | What it is | Composition (typical) | What it is chosen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Granulated sugar | Refined sucrose crystals, ~0.3–0.6 mm | > 99.8 % sucrose | Default sweetener; creaming; crisp textures |
| Caster (superfine) sugar | Smaller sucrose crystals, ~0.1–0.3 mm | > 99.8 % sucrose | Dissolves faster; creams more evenly; meringues, sponges, shortbread |
| Icing (powdered, confectioners') sugar | Sucrose milled to a powder | Sucrose plus an anti-caking agent, commonly ~3 % starch | Icings, glazes, short pastry (sablé, sucrée) where you want no crystal texture and little aeration |
| Light brown sugar | Sucrose crystals coated in molasses (or less refined) | ~3–5 % molasses; ~2–4 % moisture; small amounts of invert sugar, minerals, organic acids | Moist, chewy cookies; caramel flavour; a little acidity that reacts with baking soda |
| Dark brown sugar | As light, more molasses | ~6–7 % molasses, slightly more moisture and acid | Stronger molasses flavour, darker, softer texture |
| Muscovado, panela, jaggery | Unrefined or partly refined cane sugar | Variable, up to ~10 % non-sucrose solids and more moisture | Flavour; behaves like a very dark brown sugar, varies batch to batch |
| Invert sugar (invert syrup, trimoline) | Sucrose split into equal glucose and fructose, usually as a syrup | ~70–80 % solids (check the label) | Keeps products soft, prevents crystallization, lowers aw, browns faster |
| Glucose syrup (corn syrup in the US) | Partly hydrolysed starch: glucose, maltose and longer chains | ~75–85 % solids; described by DE (below) | Anti-crystallization in caramel, ganache and sorbet; body and chew; less sweet than sucrose |
| Honey | Bee-concentrated nectar | ~17–18 % water; ~38 % fructose, ~31 % glucose, small amounts of sucrose and maltose | Flavour, softness, fast browning; natural invert syrup |
| Maple syrup | Concentrated maple sap | ~33 % water; the sugar is mostly sucrose | Flavour; behaves like a sucrose syrup, not like honey |
| Malt syrup / malt extract | Starch converted by malted barley | Mostly maltose; diastatic versions contain active amylase | Flavour and colour in bread and bagels; diastatic malt feeds yeast ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) |
| Lactose (in milk, milk powder) | Milk sugar | — | Browning (reducing); yeast cannot ferment it ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)) |

### Dextrose equivalent (DE)

Glucose syrups are made by breaking starch into shorter chains. **Dextrose equivalent** measures how far that has gone: pure glucose (dextrose) is 100, unbroken starch is about 0. A 42 DE syrup, the common confectionery grade, is a mix of glucose, maltose and longer chains. Use DE as a dial:

| As DE rises (e.g. 38 → 42 → 63) | Effect |
|---|---|
| More small molecules | Sweeter, more hygroscopic, lowers aw more, browns faster |
| Fewer long chains | Lower viscosity, less body and chew, slightly weaker anti-crystallization "interference" |

For the caramel in [07.2](stage-1/module-07/lesson-02.md), any 38–45 DE glucose syrup works. Buy the clear, neutral-tasting kind. Supermarket "golden syrup" is a partly inverted sucrose syrup, not a glucose syrup; it also works against crystallization but browns faster.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** In professional kitchens syrups are weighed like everything else, and their **solids** are what count when you substitute. 100 g honey carries about 82 g sugar and 18 g water. 100 g glucose syrup at 80 % solids carries 80 g carbohydrate. When you replace sucrose with a syrup, replace sugar-solids for sugar-solids and account for the water it brings, or your hydration changes without your noticing.

</div>

## Relative sweetness

Perceived sweetness depends on concentration, temperature and what else is present, so the numbers are ranges. Use them as a rank order.

| Sugar | Relative sweetness (sucrose = 1.0) |
|---|---|
| Fructose | ~1.2–1.7 (sweetest cold, in solution) |
| Invert sugar | ~1.0–1.3 |
| Sucrose | 1.0 |
| Glucose (dextrose) | ~0.7 |
| Maltose | ~0.3–0.5 |
| Glucose syrup, 42 DE | ~0.4–0.5 |
| Lactose | ~0.2–0.4 |

Two consequences for the bench. First, you can add a lot of glucose syrup before a product tastes much sweeter, which is why confectioners use it for texture. Second, swapping sucrose for honey in the same weight gives a *slightly* sweeter, much browner, much softer product, not just "a honey-flavoured" one.

## What sugar does besides sweeten

Every function below is a consequence of three properties: sugar dissolves in water and holds on to it, sugar crystals are hard and sharp, and sugars brown under heat.

| Function | Mechanism | What you see on the bench |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenderizing** | Dissolved sugar competes with flour proteins and starch for water. Gluten forms more slowly and less completely; starch gelatinization and egg-protein coagulation move to **higher temperatures** | Softer, more tender crumb; cakes and cookies set later and rise or spread further before they set |
| **Moisture retention** | Sugars are **hygroscopic**: they attract and hold water. Ranking: fructose > invert/honey > glucose > sucrose > lactose | Higher-sugar and honey/invert products stay soft longer; crisp sugary items pick up moisture from humid air |
| **Lowering water activity** | Dissolved molecules tie up water, so less is available to microbes and to evaporation. Smaller molecules (fructose, glucose) lower aw more per gram than sucrose | Longer mould-free shelf life; slower drying out ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)) |
| **Browning** | Reducing sugars drive Maillard browning with proteins; all sugars caramelize at high temperature | Golden to dark crust and edges; more sugar and more reducing sugar = faster colour ([07.2](stage-1/module-07/lesson-02.md)) |
| **Spread in cookies** | Crystals that did not dissolve in the cold dough dissolve as it heats; the dough's liquid phase grows and the dough flows | More sugar = more spread, thinner cookies ([07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md)) |
| **Aeration in creaming** | Sharp sugar crystals cut into plastic fat and trap tiny air cells, which later become nuclei for leavening gas | Paler, lighter butter–sugar mass; finer, higher cake crumb ([10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md)) |
| **Yeast food** | Yeast invertase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, which yeast ferments; at high levels sugar slows yeast by osmotic stress | Faster start at 1–5 %; slower above ~10 % ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)) |
| **Crystallization and crunch** | Sugar that dries out or cools from a concentrated syrup can form crystals or a hard glass | Crackly tops on brownies and cookies, crunchy sugar crust, crisp edges; also graininess when unwanted ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)) |
| **Stabilizing foams** | Dissolved sugar thickens the water phase of egg foams and slows drainage | More stable, finer meringue ([13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md)) |

### Tenderizing, in more detail

Tenderizing deserves the extra attention because it has several steps that change what happens in the oven.

1. **In the mixer.** Gluten proteins need water to hydrate and link up ([02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md)). Sugar dissolved in the same water holds some of it and makes the solution more concentrated. Gluten forms more slowly, and with little mixing (as in a cookie) it barely forms at all.
2. **In the oven, starch.** Starch granules need water and heat to gelatinize. In a concentrated sugar solution they gelatinize at a higher temperature: in pure water wheat starch gelatinizes around 55–70 °C; in a high-sugar batter the range moves up by 10–25 °C or more depending on concentration ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)).
3. **In the oven, eggs.** Egg proteins coagulate at higher temperatures in the presence of sugar ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md) uses this in custards).

Steps 2 and 3 mean the structure sets later. In a cake that gives more time to expand, so volume increases up to a point, then collapses if sugar is so high that the structure never sets properly. In a cookie it gives more time to flow, so the cookie spreads more.

### Hygroscopicity and water activity are not the same thing

**Hygroscopicity** is how strongly a sugar pulls water from the air or from other ingredients. **Water activity** (aw) describes how available the water in a product is ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)). They are linked: the more strongly the dissolved sugars hold water, the lower the aw at a given moisture. Two practical rules follow.

- **At the same moisture, more dissolved sugar means lower aw.** That is why a high-sugar cake keeps longer than a low-sugar one, and jam with 30–35 % water is shelf-stable.
- **Per gram, small molecules count more.** aw falls with the *number* of dissolved molecules. Splitting one sucrose into glucose + fructose roughly doubles that number for the same weight, so invert sugar and honey lower aw more than sucrose, and keep products softer for longer.

Typical values: a crisp cookie is at aw ~0.2–0.5; a soft, chewy cookie ~0.5–0.7; a cake ~0.85–0.95; bread crumb ~0.95–0.97. Most moulds need aw above ~0.80, most bacteria above ~0.90. A cookie is safe on the counter because its aw is low. A soft cookie made softer with more invert can creep toward the range where moulds grow, which is one of the limits of "keep it soft".

## Dissolved or not? The water-phase calculation

A cookie dough has little water. How much of its sugar is actually dissolved before baking? Do the arithmetic for [R1-11](recipes/R1-11-chocolate-chip-cookie.md) (per 250 g flour):

| Source of water | Grams | Water fraction (typical) | Water (g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Butter | 190 | ~16 % | ~30 |
| Whole egg | 95 | ~75 % | ~71 |
| Brown sugar | 140 | ~3 % | ~4 |
| **Total water** | | | **~105** |

Sugar is 140 + 110 = 250 g. Sucrose dissolves at about **2.0 g per g of water at 20 °C**, 2.6 g/g at 50 °C and about 4.8 g/g at 100 °C. At room temperature ~105 g water can hold ~210 g sucrose, *if* it were not also hydrating flour. The flour takes a large share of that water, so in practice most of the sugar in cookie dough stays as crystals until the oven heats it. As the dough warms, sugar dissolves, the syrup phase grows, the dough softens and flows. That is the physical reason sugar makes cookies spread, and why more sugar, finer sugar, or syrup sugars spread more ([07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md)).

Compare the concentration in the water phase with the 06.1 calculation for bread: milk bread has sugar at ~12 % of its water phase; a cookie dough is well above 70 %. Same ingredient, completely different system.

## Why "reduce the sugar by 30 %" breaks a recipe

This is the key transfer of the lesson: stop reading sugar as "sweetness" and start reading it as a variable that moves water, structure-setting temperature, spread and colour at the same time. Take R1-11 and cut both sugars by 30 % (sugar from 100 % to 70 % of flour) without changing anything else. Predict each change:

| Property | Direction | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sweetness | Less sweet, but less than 30 % less as perceived | Perception is not linear; salt, chocolate and browning notes become more obvious |
| Spread | Much less; thicker, domed | Less sugar to dissolve and liquefy; structure sets earlier |
| Texture | Cakier, bread-like, drier; less crisp edge, less chew | More free water for flour: more gluten, more gelatinized starch; less syrup to make chew |
| Colour | Paler | Less sugar at the surface for Maillard and caramelization |
| Shelf life | Stales and dries faster; hard within 1–2 days | Higher aw, less hygroscopic sugar to hold water, faster starch retrogradation |
| Batch yield | ~7 % less dough per batch | 75 g of 1044 g removed; if you portion by weight, each cookie has more flour |

The fix is not "put the sugar back". A formulator who must lower sugar treats each lost function separately: spread (fat state, hydration, soda), tenderness (fat, egg yolk, less mixing), browning (a little honey or malt, milk powder, pH), moisture retention (a humectant such as glycerol or a hygroscopic sugar), and bulk (fibre or polyols). That is Stage 3 work, but the habit starts here: **list the jobs an ingredient does before you change its amount.** You will measure this directly in [X1-18](experiments/X1-18-cookie-sugar-level.md).

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Sugar is a water-management ingredient. Most of what it does (tenderness, spread, softness, shelf life, even much of browning) comes from where it puts the water. Change the sugar and you have changed the water.

</div>

## What goes wrong

- **Reduced-sugar cookies came out thick, pale and dry.** Sugar was cut without compensating for its structural jobs. See [cookies do not spread](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=cookies-do-not-spread) and [pale cookies](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=pale-cookies).
- **Cookies spread into one sheet.** Sugar too high, or a syrup or very fine sugar added without adjusting. See [excessive spreading](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=excessive-spreading).
- **Soft cookies turned hard overnight.** Moisture was lost and sucrose recrystallized. See [cookies harden overnight](troubleshooting/cookies.md?id=cookies-harden-overnight).
- **Honey substitution gave dark, sticky, cakey cookies.** Honey was swapped by weight for sugar, adding water and fast-browning fructose. Substitute on a solids basis and lower the oven by 10 °C ([X1-19](experiments/X1-19-sugar-type.md)).

## Check yourself

1. You replace 50 g of granulated sugar with 50 g of honey in a cookie formula. Name three things that change besides flavour, and say how you should have made the substitution.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The dough gains ~9 g water and loses ~9 g sugar solids (50 g honey ≈ 41 g sugars + 9 g water). The cookies brown faster (fructose and glucose are reducing and fructose caramelizes at a low temperature), spread more and stay softer (more hygroscopic sugars, extra water, lower aw), and may be slightly sweeter. Substitute on solids: ~61 g honey replaces 50 g sugar, and remove ~11 g of water or egg elsewhere if you want the hydration unchanged. Lower the oven ~10 °C or shorten the bake if colour runs ahead.

</details>

2. Why does a high-sugar cake set later in the oven than a low-sugar cake, and what can that do to volume?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dissolved sugar raises the temperatures at which starch gelatinizes and egg proteins coagulate. The batter stays fluid longer, so it can expand more before it sets: volume rises, up to a point. At very high sugar the structure sets so late, or so weakly, that the cake collapses on cooling or sinks in the centre.

</details>

3. Rank these for keeping a cookie soft for longest, and explain the ranking in one sentence: sucrose, honey, glucose, fructose.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fructose > honey > glucose > sucrose. The more hygroscopic the sugar and the more dissolved molecules per gram, the more strongly it holds water and the lower the aw, so the cookie loses water more slowly and resists recrystallization.

</details>

4. A cookie dough has 250 g sucrose and about 105 g water. Is most of the sugar dissolved when the dough goes into the oven? What does this predict about spread?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Sucrose dissolves at about 2 g/g water at 20 °C, and the flour is taking up much of the water, so most of the sugar is still crystalline. As the dough heats, solubility rises steeply and the crystals dissolve, turning the dough more fluid: that is when it spreads. More sugar, or sugar that dissolves more easily (finer crystals, syrups), gives more spread.

</details>

5. Give one reason a baker would use glucose syrup instead of more sucrose, and one property that changes as its DE rises.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

To prevent sucrose crystallizing (in caramel, fondant, ganache, sorbet) and to add body and chew without much extra sweetness. As DE rises the syrup becomes sweeter, more hygroscopic, browns faster, and is less viscous.

</details>
