## Why this lesson exists

A layered cake is the purest test of component thinking: every part is soft, so the structure has to be designed. Génoise, syrup and buttercream are each easy; a cake that is level, straight-sided, moist but not wet, and cuts into twelve identical slices with clean layers is not. The skills here (splitting, soaking, filling with a dam, crumb coating, finishing on a turntable, assembling in a ring with acetate) are the ones every Stage 2 entremet is built on. The lesson closes with the basics of composing a plated dessert, because a slice of cake or tart on a plate is itself an assembly of components.

## You will be able to

- Level and split a génoise into three even layers and soak each with a measured weight of syrup (about 30–40 g per 20 cm layer).
- Fill and stack layers with buttercream, or with diplomate or Chantilly and fruit contained by a dam, so the cake is level and does not slide or bulge.
- Crumb coat and final coat a cake on a turntable to straight sides and a sharp top edge, or assemble it in a ring with acetate.
- Pipe a simple border and décor, and cut 12 equal portions with a hot knife.
- State holding times and temperatures for buttercream cakes and cream-filled cakes, and prevent condensation and melting at service.

## The layered cake as a system

| Component | Job | Weight in a 20 cm cake ([R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md)) |
|---|---|---|
| Génoise, 3 layers | Structure; carries syrup and filling | ~390 g after levelling (one [R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md) layer, ~410 g baked) |
| Soaking syrup | Moisture, sweetness, flavour | ~105 g (30–40 g per layer) |
| Filling | Moisture, fat, flavour; mortar between layers | 300 g buttercream + 200 g fruit (or diplomate + fruit) |
| Coating | Seals the cake (slows drying), finish | ~370 g buttercream (crumb coat + final coat) |
| Décor | Signals flavour, marks portions | ~80 g buttercream + ~50 g fruit |

The génoise is **deliberately dry**: it is a lean egg foam with little fat ([10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md)) and would taste dry on its own. Its open, springy crumb is designed to take up syrup and to stay in shape under the weight of the layers above. That is the design logic of the whole product: the sponge supplies structure; the syrup and fillings supply moisture and richness.

## Step 1: level and split

Cut a génoise the day after baking if you can: the crumb has firmed and cuts with fewer crumbs. Chilling it for 30 min, or using a frozen-and-thawed layer, helps even more.

1. **Level.** Put the génoise on a turntable. With a long serrated knife held level, rest the blade against the side at the height where the dome starts, and turn the cake against the knife in short sawing strokes, cutting in toward the centre. Remove the dome (and the top skin if it is thick or dark).
2. **Mark.** Measure the height (typically 4.0–4.5 cm after levelling). For three layers, mark every 1.3–1.5 cm with toothpicks around the side, or use two identical boards or rulers as knife guides.
3. **Split.** Cut the same way, turning the cake against the knife. Lift each layer on a cake board or the base of a tart tin, not by hand.
4. **Assign.** The **bottom layer of the baked génoise** (the flattest, with the firmest crust) goes **on top**, cut side down, to give a flat, square top. The original top layer becomes the base.

## Step 2: soak with a measured weight

Soaking syrup is a 40 °Brix flavoured syrup ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md) B), used at room temperature. Weigh it; do not soak by eye.

| Layer | Syrup (20 cm, ~130 g layer) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom | 30 g | Sits on the board; too much makes it soggy and liquid collects under it |
| Middle | 35 g | Soaked from both sides by the fillings too |
| Top | 40 g | Cut surface faces down into the filling; top dries most in the refrigerator |
| **Total** | **105 g**, about 25 % of the sponge weight | Starting point for a génoise; adjust by tasting |

Brush from the edges in (edges dry out first), in two passes, letting the first soak in for a minute. Put the layer on the scale and brush until the target weight is added. A properly soaked layer is moist and cool-feeling but does not release syrup when pressed with a finger.

**Why weigh:** under-soaking is the cause of the "dry despite syrup" cake, and over-soaking of the soggy base that slides. The right amount depends on the sponge's density and the moisture of the filling: a Chantilly or diplomate filling gives water to the sponge; buttercream gives little. Reduce the syrup by about a third with wet fillings.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Syrup moves into the sponge by capillary action along the open cell walls; the sugar lowers the syrup's water activity, so the soaked crumb stays moist longer than it would with plain water and drains less, and the dissolved sugar plasticizes the starch–protein walls so the crumb feels tender. Syrup at 40 °Brix has an aw of roughly 0.95; the génoise ends up close to that after equilibrating overnight. Alcohol, if used, adds flavour and a small preservative effect but evaporates from the surface ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md), [R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)).

</div>

## Step 3: fill and stack

### With Swiss meringue buttercream (the main method)

Swiss meringue buttercream ([R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md), [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md)) is an emulsion of meringue-based water phase in a continuous butterfat phase. It is spreadable at **20–22 °C**, firm at 4 °C, and a good moisture barrier because water crosses fat slowly. Working temperature is the whole game:

| Buttercream temperature | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| < 18 °C | Stiff, curdled look, tears the sponge and drags crumbs |
| **20–22 °C** | Smooth, silky, spreads and holds a sharp edge |
| > 24 °C | Soft, greasy, slides; does not hold layers or piping |

Stacking sequence:

1. Put a dab of buttercream on the board, then the bottom layer; soak it.
2. **Pipe a dam:** a ring of buttercream (10 mm plain tip) just inside the edge, about 1 cm tall.
3. Spread 150 g of buttercream inside the dam, level with an offset spatula; press **100 g of fruit** (raspberries, halved; or sliced strawberries, dried) into it.
4. Place the middle layer, press gently and level with a board on top; soak it. Repeat the dam, filling and fruit.
5. Place the top layer cut side down; soak it.
6. Check level with a small spirit level or by eye at eye height. Correct lean now by pressing down gently on the high side.
7. Chill 15–20 min so the fillings firm before coating.

### With diplomate or Chantilly and fruit

A lighter cake uses gelatin-set crème diplomate ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)) or stabilized Chantilly ([R1-27](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md)) and fruit. These are soft and wet: they need containment. Either pipe a firm dam of buttercream or stiff Chantilly and chill it before filling, or better, **assemble in a ring with acetate** (below) and let the diplomate set 3–4 h at ≤ 5 °C before unmoulding. Reduce the syrup by about a third. The cake is now a chilled, cream-filled product: ≤ 5 °C, eat within 24–48 h.

## Step 4: crumb coat and final coat

A **crumb coat** is a thin layer of buttercream (about 1–2 mm, ~120 g on a 20 cm cake) spread over the top and sides to glue down loose crumbs and fill gaps. Chill it **20–30 min** until firm to the touch (buttercream ≤ 10 °C). Crumbs are now locked in and cannot contaminate the final coat.

**Final coat** (about 4–5 mm, ~250 g):

1. Put a generous mound on the top and spread it outward, pushing it past the edge so it overhangs.
2. Load the sides with buttercream from the bowl, working from the bottom up.
3. **Smooth the sides:** hold a bench scraper vertical, at about 30–45° to the surface, touching the board; rotate the turntable in one continuous movement with the other hand. Scrape the excess off the scraper into a separate bowl (it may contain crumbs). Fill holes and repeat; two or three passes are normal.
4. **Sharp top edge:** the sides leave a raised crown above the top. With a clean offset spatula held nearly flat, pull the crown in toward the centre in short strokes, wiping the spatula after each.
5. Chill 20–30 min.

For a sharper finish, warm the scraper in hot water, dry it, and make a last pass over the chilled coat.

## The ring-and-acetate method: a bridge to entremets

Stage 2 entremets are assembled in rings lined with acetate, not on turntables. You can do the same with this cake:

1. Put a 20 cm × 6–7 cm ring on a flat board lined with parchment. Line the inside with an acetate strip 1–2 cm taller than the ring.
2. Trim each génoise layer 2–3 mm all round so it fits inside the acetate (remove the crust for clean sides).
3. Assemble upside down or right way up: sponge, soak, filling to the acetate, fruit (keep fruit 1 cm from the acetate so it does not show as gaps), sponge. The filling fills the space to the acetate and sets as a smooth wall.
4. Finish the top with a layer of filling levelled with a palette knife across the ring, and chill until set (3–4 h for gelatin-set fillings, 1 h for buttercream).
5. Unmould: lift off the ring, then peel the acetate in one continuous movement. The sides show the clean layers: a naked, layered look without a final coat.

Advantages: perfectly straight sides, soft fillings contained without a dam, very little skill needed for a sharp look. Disadvantages: you need a ring and acetate, time to set, and the sides show every uneven layer.

## Step 5: piping and simple decoration

Keep décor simple and purposeful: it should say what is inside and mark the portions.

- **Border:** a shell border (star tip, 8–10 mm) at the base hides the join with the board; a rosette or shell border at the top edge.
- **Portion rosettes:** 12 rosettes, one per slice, placed with the help of a 12-portion marker lightly pressed onto the top. Each slice gets one: portion control and decoration at once.
- **Fruit:** the fruit used inside, a few pieces on each rosette.
- **Chocolate:** a few tempered chocolate pieces ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)) or a chocolate collar poured on acetate and wrapped round the chilled cake.

Pipe at 20–22 °C buttercream; warm hands warm the bag, so fill bags half full and swap them.

## Cutting, portioning and holding

- **Cut cold but not hard.** A buttercream cake cuts cleanest at 10–14 °C (30–45 min out of the refrigerator). Straight from 4 °C the buttercream cracks; at room temperature it smears.
- **Hot knife.** Long, thin knife, dipped in hot water, wiped, one downward stroke; pull out from the bottom; clean between cuts.
- **Portions:** 12 slices of about 125 g from a 20 cm cake of about 1.5 kg; mark with a portion marker.

| Cake type | Hold at | Keeps | Serve at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buttercream fill and coat, syrup, no fresh cream | Refrigerated (quality is best) or a cool room ≤ 20 °C for a few hours | 3 days refrigerated, well covered (the coat seals the sponge) | 30–60 min out of the refrigerator (buttercream ~16–20 °C) |
| Buttercream with fresh fruit inside | ≤ 5 °C | 2 days (fruit weeps and the sponge absorbs it) | As above |
| Diplomate or Chantilly filling | **≤ 5 °C** | **24–48 h** | 15–20 min out of the refrigerator at most |

Buttercream cakes freeze well (unwrapped until the coat is hard, then wrapped; thaw in the refrigerator overnight in the wrapping to limit condensation). Cream-filled cakes do not.

**Condensation:** a cake at 4 °C taken into a warm, humid room is below the dew point of the air; water condenses on the surface within minutes, spotting the coat and softening chocolate décor. Keep it in its box while it warms, or let it warm in a cool, dry place, and add chocolate and sugar décor last.

## Plated dessert basics

A plated dessert is a small assembly on a plate: the same component thinking, served in minutes. A simple and classical composition you can make with this module's products:

| Element | Example | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Main element | A slice of layer cake, or a wedge of chocolate tart | Structure, main flavour |
| Sauce | 30–40 g crème anglaise ([R1-24](recipes/R1-24-creme-anglaise.md)), raspberry coulis or caramel sauce ([R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)) | Moisture, a second flavour, colour |
| Crisp element | A tuile, a caramel or isomalt shard ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md) D), toasted nuts, cocoa nibs | Texture contrast, height |
| Fresh element | A few berries, citrus segments, herbs | Acid, colour, freshness |
| Optional cold element | A quenelle of Chantilly | Temperature contrast |

Principles:

- **One focal point.** The main element sits slightly off-centre or at the centre; everything else supports it. Use the odd-number rule for small items (3 or 5 berries, not 4).
- **Height.** Lean the crisp element on the main element or stand it upright: height draws the eye and keeps the crisp element away from sauce, which would soften it within minutes (moisture migration on a plate).
- **Colour.** Contrast the main element: red fruit against chocolate, a dark sauce against a pale cake. Keep the rim of the plate clean; wipe drips with a damp cloth.
- **Temperature contrast.** Cold with warm (a warm chocolate sauce with a chilled slice, or a warm tart with cold Chantilly) is the plated dessert's advantage over a cake on a counter; it only works if the dish goes out within a minute.
- **Portion.** 100–150 g in total is a typical restaurant portion; sauce in a pool or dots of 30–40 g, not a flood.
- **Order at the pass.** Sauce first, main element, fresh element, crisp element last, then out.

## On the bench

1. Session 1 (day before): bake the génoise ([R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md)); make the soaking syrup ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md) B) and the buttercream ([R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md)).
2. Session 2: assemble [R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md) with buttercream and fruit on a turntable. Weigh every component in. Photograph the side after the crumb coat and after the final coat.
3. Cut the cake into 12 with a marker; weigh the slices; check that the layers are level (measure each layer at both ends of a slice).
4. Plate one slice with anglaise and a crisp element; photograph and critique against the principles.
5. Optional: make a second cake in a ring with acetate and a diplomate–fruit filling; compare the look, the texture and the holding.

## What goes wrong

- **Layers slide or the cake leans.** Soft filling, no dam, warm buttercream, too much syrup, uneven layers. See [Layer cake layers sliding](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=layer-cake-layers-sliding) and [Cake leaning or bulging](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=cake-leaning-or-bulging).
- **Dry despite syrup, or soggy.** Syrup not weighed; too little on the top layer; wet filling plus full syrup. See [Cake too dry despite syrup](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=cake-too-dry-despite-syrup) and [Cake soggy from syrup](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=cake-soggy-from-syrup).
- **Crumbs in the finish.** No crumb coat, or it was not chilled; scraper excess returned to the bowl. See [Crumbs in the buttercream finish](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=crumbs-in-the-buttercream-finish).
- **Water spots and melting décor.** Condensation and warm service. See [Condensation on a chilled cake](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=condensation-on-a-chilled-cake) and [Decorations melting or weeping](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=decorations-melting-or-weeping).

## Check yourself

1. A 20 cm génoise weighs 420 g. Using 25 % of its weight as the syrup target and splitting 30:35:40 across bottom, middle and top, how much syrup goes on each layer? How would you change it for a diplomate filling?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total 420 × 0.25 = 105 g. Split 30:35:40 (sum 105): bottom 30 g, middle 35 g, top 40 g. For a diplomate filling, reduce by about a third (about 70 g total: 20, 23, 27 g), because the wet filling gives water to the sponge.

</details>

2. Your buttercream is at 26 °C when you start the final coat. What will you see, and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It is soft and greasy: it slides, will not hold a sharp edge, smears when scraped and may drag the crumb coat. Chill the bowl for 5–10 min and re-beat until it is at 20–22 °C and smooth, and chill the cake so the crumb coat is firm.

</details>

3. Why do you put the original bottom of the génoise on top of the cake, cut side down?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It is the flattest surface, with sharp edges from the pan, so it gives a level, square top; the cut side faces down into the filling where it absorbs syrup; and the firmer crust makes a stable surface for the final coat.

</details>

4. A cake filled with Chantilly and strawberries has bulged at the sides by the next day. Explain and give two fixes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The soft, wet filling was not contained and flowed outward under the weight of the layers above, helped by strawberry juice and syrup softening the sponge. Fixes: pipe a firm dam (buttercream or stiff stabilized Chantilly) and chill it before filling; use stabilized Chantilly or gelatin-set diplomate; assemble in a ring with acetate and set 3–4 h before unmoulding; reduce syrup; dry the fruit.

</details>

5. On a plate, you put the tuile flat in the anglaise. What happens within 5 minutes, and what principle did you break?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The tuile absorbs water from the high-aw sauce and goes soft and bendy: moisture migration. Keep crisp elements out of sauces, lean them on or stand them in the main element for height, and add them last just before the plate goes out.

</details>
