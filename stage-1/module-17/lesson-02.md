## Why this lesson exists

The fruit tart and the chocolate tart are the two canonical French tarts, and they are opposite solutions to the same engineering problem: how to put a moist, creamy filling in a crisp shell and keep the shell crisp until it is eaten. The fruit tart uses a very wet filling (pastry cream, fresh fruit) and so depends on a dry, sealed shell and a short schedule. The chocolate tart uses a filling whose water is bound and surrounded by fat, so it can sit for days. Making both, from components you already know, teaches the order of work, the temperatures that matter at assembly, and how to judge a finished tart by cutting it.

## You will be able to

- Fully bake and seal a sucrée shell and state the end-point for "fully baked" in colour, sound and texture.
- Assemble a fruit tart in the right order and within the time window that keeps the shell crisp, the cream firm and the fruit glossy.
- Make and pour a ganache filling at 35–40 °C so it self-levels, sets glossy and slices cleanly; or bake a chocolate custard filling to a just-set end-point at 150 °C.
- Choose and apply a glaze (apricot nappage, neutral glaze, chocolate glaze) at the right temperature and thickness.
- Slice and hold each tart correctly, and state how long each keeps and why.

## The shell: fully baked, sealed, cool

Both tarts in this lesson use a **fully baked** shell from [R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md): neither filling is baked long enough (or at all) to finish the pastry. Review [11.2](stage-1/module-11/lesson-02.md) for lining and blind baking. The end-points that matter now:

| Check | Target for a shell that will take an unbaked filling |
|---|---|
| Colour | Even golden on rim, walls **and the centre of the base** (lift one side and look underneath: light golden, no grey or pale patches) |
| Surface | Dry and matt inside; no shine except where sealed |
| Sound and feel | Tap the base: a hollow, crisp sound. A test offcut snaps cleanly |
| Walls | Straight, no cracks through which filling could leak; small cracks patched with raw dough and baked 2 min |
| Temperature at filling | Room temperature. A warm shell softens cocoa butter barriers and melts ganache at the edge |

A pale base is the most common fault: sucrée browns fast on the rim, so the cook stops early while the base, shielded by weights, is still underbaked. Bake on a preheated tray on the lower-middle rack and remove weights for the last 8–12 minutes.

### Sealing: three options, three strengths

From [17.1](stage-1/module-17/lesson-01.md): the shell is at aw ~0.3, the pastry cream ~0.97. Water will move. Your choices, in increasing protection:

| Seal | How | Protection (what to expect, [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md)) | Taste |
|---|---|---|---|
| **None** | — | Noticeably softer within 1–2 h under pastry cream; soggy by the next day | — |
| **Egg wash** | Yolk + about 10 % cream (or whole egg), brushed thinly on the hot, fully baked shell; 2–3 min at 165–170 °C until set and glossy | Slows migration: good for a few hours; soft next day | Neutral, slightly richer colour |
| **Cocoa butter or chocolate** | Melted cocoa butter (about 35–40 °C) or tempered chocolate ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)) brushed in a thin, complete film, 10–20 g for a 22 cm shell, set 5–10 min | Strong: shell stays crisp overnight under pastry cream if coverage is complete | Cocoa butter neutral; dark chocolate noticeable (good in the chocolate tart, a choice in the fruit tart); white chocolate sweet, milky |

The weak point of every film is the **corner** between base and wall, where the brush skips and where the cream sits deepest. Brush the corner first and check it against the light.

## The fruit tart (R1-39)

### Components and proportions

For a 22 cm × 2 cm ring ([R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md) has the full procedure):

| Component | Source | Weight | Share of finished tart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sucrée shell, fully baked and sealed | [R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md) ×0.5; about 250 g dough lined | ~220 g baked | ~20 % |
| Crème pâtissière (or diplomate) | [R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md) ×0.5 | 350–400 g | ~34 % |
| Fresh fruit, prepared | Berries, stone fruit, citrus segments, kiwi, figs | 400–500 g | ~40 % |
| Glaze (apricot nappage or neutral) | Heated jam or commercial neutral glaze | 60–80 g | ~6 % |

### The cream layer

Crème pâtissière is the classic filling. It must be firm enough to carry the fruit and to cut, and it is at its best on day 1–2 after cooking (no amylase breakdown if it was boiled properly; full cold set). Whisk it for only 20–40 s before use ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)): enough to make it smooth and pipeable, not so much that the starch gel breaks down and weeps into the shell. Pipe it with a 12–14 mm plain tip in a spiral from the centre, then level with a small offset spatula, keeping the surface **5–7 mm below the rim** so the fruit sits partly inside the shell. Piping gives an even layer and avoids tearing the barrier; spreading with a spoon drags crumbs up.

**Crème diplomate** (pastry cream lightened with whipped cream and 1 % gelatin) is lighter and less sweet-tasting. It has to set in the shell for 1–2 h at ≤ 5 °C before it will carry fruit without spreading, and it lasts 24 h. Choose it when the tart will be served within the day and you want a lighter slice.

A variant used in many pastry shops: bake a thin layer of crème d'amande ([R1-23](recipes/R1-23-creme-damande.md), about 150 g) in a par-baked shell, cool it, then add a thinner layer of pastry cream and the fruit. The almond cream is a baked, fat-rich cake layer between shell and cream: a sponge for any juice and a flavour accent. It also makes the tart more forgiving to hold.

### The fruit

Fruit brings acid, colour and juice, and it is the component that degrades fastest.

- **Dry after washing.** Wet fruit brings free water straight onto the cream and makes glaze slide off. Wash berries only if needed, drain on paper towel, and let the surface dry (15–20 min).
- **Cut close to assembly.** A cut surface releases juice as cells are broken, and sugar in the glaze draws more out by osmosis. Cut strawberries, stone fruit and kiwi within the last hour.
- **Arrange from the outside in.** Start with a ring at the rim, cut faces all pointing the same way, then work inward; each ring slightly overlaps and leans on the previous one. Place whole berries into the cream by 2–3 mm so they are anchored. Keep the pattern tight: gaps show cream, which dries and looks tired.
- **Protease fruit and gelatin.** Raw kiwi, pineapple, papaya, mango and fig contain protein-digesting enzymes ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) that liquefy gelatin and can make dairy taste bitter over hours. On pastry cream (a starch gel) the effect is small and slow; on a gelatin-set diplomate, keep these fruits for tarts served within 2–3 h, or use cooked or canned fruit.

### The glaze

A glaze (nappage) seals the fruit surface against drying and oxidation, holds loose pieces in place and gives shine. Two practical options:

| Glaze | Preparation | Application | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Apricot glaze** | Apricot jam + 10–20 % water, brought to a boil, sieved | Brush thinly **while hot** (about 80–90 °C, just off the boil); it gels within a minute on the fruit | The pectin in the jam re-dissolves when boiled and sets again on cooling. Amber colour: best on apricots, pears, mixed fruit; slightly muddies red berries |
| **Neutral glaze** (nappage neutre, commercial) | Hot-process: add the stated water (often 10–30 %), bring to a boil. Cold-process: use as supplied | Brush or spray at the stated temperature | Clear; based on pectin or carrageenan with glucose syrup. Follow the maker's dilution: too little water and it goes thick and cloudy, too much and it slides |
| **Red currant jelly** | Warm with 5–10 % water to 70–80 °C | Brush | Good on red fruit |

Use a soft brush, dab rather than stroke on delicate fruit, and keep the coat thin: you are glazing, not jamming. 60–80 g is enough for a 22 cm tart. If the glaze is cooler than about 60 °C it has already begun to gel and goes on streaky and cloudy; reheat it.

### Order of work and how long it holds

The sequence that keeps every component at its best:

1. Day before (or earlier): dough; pastry cream; shell baked, sealed with egg wash, cooled and stored airtight.
2. Up to 4 h before service: optional cocoa butter or chocolate film; set 5–10 min.
3. Wash and dry fruit; heat glaze; re-texture cream.
4. Pipe cream; arrange fruit; glaze.
5. Refrigerate at ≤ 5 °C; serve within about **4–6 h** for best texture, within **24 h** at most (food safety and quality).

With an egg-wash seal only, expect a crisp shell for about 4–6 h after filling; with a complete cocoa butter film, the shell is still acceptably crisp at 24 h. Measure your own numbers in [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md).

## The chocolate tart (R1-40)

### Why it holds

The chocolate tart is a crisp shell filled with a ganache ([16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md), [R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md)): an emulsion of cream's water phase in a continuous matrix of melted and then partly crystallized cocoa butter and cocoa solids. At a 1:1 ratio of dark chocolate to cream the water phase is heavily loaded with sugar and cocoa solids, its aw is well below that of pastry cream, and the fat slows exchange. The shell stays crisp for two to three days. That is why the chocolate tart is a make-ahead product and the fruit tart is not.

### Shell choices

- **Plain sucrée** ([R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md)): contrast of colour and a buttery note against the chocolate.
- **Chocolate sucrée:** replace about 12 % of the flour with cocoa powder (for a half batch, 110 g flour + 15 g cocoa). Cocoa absorbs water and makes the dough a little firmer and shorter; it also hides browning, so judge doneness by time, a dry matt base and a toasted-cocoa smell, not by colour. Bake the same way.

Either shell can be sealed with a thin coat of the same dark chocolate you use for the ganache: no flavour cost, extra protection for a tart that will be held for days.

### Filling A: ganache (set, unbaked)

A good tart ganache is 1:1 dark chocolate (60–70 % cocoa solids) to cream (35 %), with about **10 % butter** on the combined weight added at the end for a softer, more melting texture and gloss, a little glucose syrup or honey (about 10 % of the chocolate) for shine and smoothness, and a pinch of salt. [R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md) has the full formula and quantities for a 22 cm shell (about 570 g).

The critical numbers:

| Step | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cream | Heated to 80–85 °C (steaming, bubbles at the edge) | Enough heat to melt the chocolate completely |
| Emulsify | Stir from the centre in small circles (or hand blender, head submerged) until glossy and elastic | Builds a fine emulsion; a coarse one is grainy and dull ([16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md)) |
| Butter | Added at 35–40 °C, soft (18–20 °C), blended in | Hotter melts the butter to oil; colder leaves lumps |
| **Pour** | **35–40 °C** into a room-temperature shell | Fluid enough to self-level; not so hot that it melts the shell's butter or seal, forms a skin or splits |
| Set | 2–3 h at 16–20 °C, or 30–60 min in the refrigerator then back to room temperature | Slow setting at room temperature gives the best gloss; condensation from the refrigerator dulls the surface |
| Serve | 18–22 °C | Ganache is sliceable and melting; straight from the refrigerator it is hard and flavourless |

Bubbles are the most common cosmetic fault. Blend with the head fully submerged, pour from low height into the centre, tap the tart once on the bench, and pop remaining bubbles with a pin or a brief pass of a blowtorch held well away.

### Filling B: baked chocolate custard

A softer, more custard-like option: ganache enriched with whole egg (and often milk), poured into the fully baked shell and baked at a low temperature. The egg proteins coagulate around 70 °C, setting the filling to a silky, just-trembling texture ([09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md), [12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md)).

- Mix the egg into the ganache when it has cooled to about **40–45 °C** (hotter starts setting the egg in threads).
- Bake at **150 °C (300 °F)**, still oven, for about **15–25 min**, until the edge 3–4 cm is set and the centre 5–6 cm still wobbles as a unit when the tray is nudged; probe **68–72 °C** in the centre.
- Cool at room temperature for at least 1 h. The centre sets as it cools.

It holds refrigerated for about 2 days; serve at room temperature. Overbaking gives a domed, cracked surface and a grainy, eggy texture, exactly the curdling of an overcooked baked custard ([R1-25](recipes/R1-25-creme-brulee.md)).

### Glaze and décor

A chocolate tart can be left plain (a well-poured ganache is already glossy) or finished with a thin pouring glaze over the chilled surface for a mirror finish: warm cream, water and glucose syrup poured over chopped chocolate, emulsified, used at 32–35 °C and poured in one motion onto a surface chilled for 20–30 min, then tilted to cover ([R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md)). Décor should be small and structural: a tempered chocolate disc ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)), a few flakes of salt, cocoa nibs, a caramel shard ([R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)).

### Slicing

Ganache drags and smears a cold knife. Use a long, thin knife dipped in hot water (60–70 °C), wiped dry, and cut in one downward motion; then press through the shell with the tip. Clean, dip and wipe between every cut. Cut at 16–20 °C: too cold and the ganache cracks, too warm and it smears. The same method gives clean fruit-tart slices, but cut through the fruit first with a serrated knife.

## What goes wrong

- **Soggy base.** Underbaked shell, no seal, wet fruit, or filled too early. See [Soggy tart base](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=soggy-tart-base).
- **Fruit weeps, glaze slides or looks cloudy.** Wet or long-cut fruit; glaze too thin, too thick or too cold. See [Fruit weeping or glaze sliding off](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=fruit-weeping-or-glaze-sliding-off) and [Glaze cloudy or too thick](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=glaze-cloudy-or-too-thick).
- **Cream too runny to slice.** Pastry cream under-boiled or over-whisked, diplomate not set. See [Tart cream too runny to slice](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=tart-cream-too-runny-to-slice).
- **Chocolate tart cracked, grainy or dull.** Poured too hot, cooled too fast, overbaked custard, broken emulsion, condensation. See [Cracked chocolate tart surface](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=cracked-chocolate-tart-surface) and [Grainy or dull chocolate tart](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=grainy-or-dull-chocolate-tart).

## Check yourself

1. Your fruit tart shell was sealed with egg wash and filled at 10:00. At 18:00 the base is soft. Give two changes that do not involve changing the time of filling.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Bake the shell more fully (golden centre of the base); add a thin, complete cocoa butter or chocolate film over the egg-wash seal, especially in the base–wall corner; use a firmer, properly boiled and minimally re-whisked pastry cream; dry the fruit thoroughly; or put a baked crème d'amande layer or a thin jam layer between shell and cream. Two of these suffice.

</details>

2. Why must apricot glaze be brushed on hot, and what happens if you let it cool to 50 °C first?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The pectin gel in the jam is re-dissolved by boiling and re-forms as it cools. Brushed at 80–90 °C it flows into a thin, even film and sets on the fruit. At 50 °C it has already begun to gel: it drags, goes on thick and streaky, looks cloudy and can pull delicate fruit out of place.

</details>

3. A chocolate tart ganache is poured at 55 °C. Predict two consequences.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It can soften or melt the cocoa butter seal and the butter in the shell edge; it takes longer to set, is more prone to fat separation (an oily sheen) and to a skin and surface cracks as it cools and contracts; more bubbles rise and set in the surface. Pour at 35–40 °C.

</details>

4. You bake a chocolate custard tart at 180 °C instead of 150 °C. Describe the likely result and explain it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Domed, cracked surface, a set ring at the edge overcooked before the centre sets, and a grainy, eggy texture. At higher oven temperature the outside of the filling overshoots the egg coagulation range (about 70 °C) while the centre catches up; over-coagulated proteins contract and squeeze out water and fat (as in an overbaked baked custard), and steam and contraction crack the top.

</details>

5. Why is a 1:1 ganache tart served at 18–22 °C rather than straight from the refrigerator?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 4 °C the cocoa butter and butterfat are largely solid: the ganache is hard, cuts with cracks, does not melt quickly in the mouth, and cold suppresses its aroma and sweetness. At 18–22 °C it is soft, sliceable and melts on the tongue.

</details>
