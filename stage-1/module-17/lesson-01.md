## Why this lesson exists

Up to now every recipe produced one thing: a shell, a cream, a sponge, a ganache. A finished pastry is several of those things, made on different days, at different temperatures, put together in a set order and eaten hours later. Most failures of finished pastries are not failures of any single component; they happen at the interfaces (a shell that was crisp at 10:00 is soggy at 16:00, a filling that tasted right on a spoon tastes flat inside a cake) or in the schedule (the pastry cream is still warm when the shell must be filled). This lesson gives you the model a pastry chef uses to design and plan an assembled product, so that the three recipes in this module, and every entremet in Stage 2, are engineered rather than hoped for.

## You will be able to

- Break any finished pastry into base, filling, accents and finish, and state the job each component does (structure, moisture, flavour, appearance).
- Predict which components will exchange water, in which direction and how fast, from their water activity, and choose a barrier or a schedule that prevents the damage.
- Build a production timeline backward from service, sorting components into "1–3 days ahead", "same day", "at assembly" and "freezes".
- Portion and weigh components for a target finished weight and a target number of servings.
- Keep a cream-filled product in the safe zone: ≤ 5 °C, eaten within 24–48 h, with limited time out of refrigeration.

## Every pastry is a system of components

Take any classical pastry apart and you find the same four roles:

| Role | Job | Typical components (this course) | Texture it brings |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | Carries the load, gives crunch or a soft structure to cut through | Sucrée shell ([R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md)), génoise ([R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md)), choux, puff pastry | Crisp, short, or soft and springy |
| **Filling** | Brings moisture, creaminess, most of the volume and much of the flavour | Crème pâtissière and diplomate ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)), ganache ([R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md)), buttercream ([R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md)), Chantilly ([R1-27](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md)), crème d'amande ([R1-23](recipes/R1-23-creme-damande.md)) | Creamy, melting, moist |
| **Accents** | Contrast in flavour or texture, in small amounts | Fresh fruit, soaking syrup ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)), caramel ([R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)), nuts, a pinch of flaky salt, citrus zest | Juicy, chewy, crunchy, sharp |
| **Finish** | Protects the surface, signals quality, often adds shine | Nappage, chocolate glaze, buttercream coat, tempered chocolate ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)), piped décor | Smooth, glossy, snap |

Two rules follow from this table. First, **each component has one main job**, and you judge it against that job, not against an ideal of its own: a génoise that is slightly dry on its own is correct if it is going to take 35 g of syrup per layer. Second, **the components interact**, so a finished product is specified by its components *plus* the order, timing and temperature of assembly.

### Contrast of textures

A mouthful that is uniformly soft is boring; a mouthful that is uniformly crisp is hard work. The classical products each combine at least two of: **crisp** (shell, tuile, nut, caramel), **creamy** (pastry cream, ganache, buttercream), **soft** (sponge, mousse), **chewy or juicy** (fruit, caramel, dried fruit). A practical check when you design: name the crisp element and the creamy element. If there is no crisp element, the product relies on contrast of temperature or airiness instead (a mousse cake), and that is a Stage 2 skill.

Texture contrast is also a **proportion**. In a well-balanced fruit tart the shell is roughly 20 % of the weight and the cream about a third; a shell at 35 % reads as a biscuit with a topping, a shell at 10 % collapses under the fork. The recipes in this module give component weights and the percentage of the finished product for this reason.

### Flavour balance

| Taste or factor | Where it comes from | What it does in an assembly | Typical control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sweet** | Sugar in every component | Carries flavour; too much flattens everything | Keep one component less sweet (Chantilly at 8 % rather than 10 %, syrup at 30 °Brix rather than 40) when others are sweet |
| **Acid** | Fruit (most around pH 3–4), lemon, fermented dairy | Makes sweetness taste lighter and fruit taste "fresh" | Choose sharper fruit (raspberry, redcurrant) against a sweet cream; add zest or juice to a syrup |
| **Bitter** | Dark chocolate, cocoa, caramel taken dark, coffee | Adds depth; balances sugar | Cocoa solids % of chocolate; how dark you take the caramel |
| **Salt** | Salt in doughs, creams, caramel | Suppresses bitterness, lifts sweetness and aroma | 0.2–0.5 % of a cream or ganache; 0.8–1 % in sucrée |
| **Fat** | Butter, cream, cocoa butter, yolks, nuts | Carries aroma: most aroma compounds are more soluble in fat than in water and are released slowly as fat melts in the mouth | Enough fat that flavour lasts; too much coats the palate and mutes acid |

Temperature changes all of this. Cold suppresses perceived sweetness and aroma: a pastry cream tasted warm from the pan will taste flatter at 4 °C. **Always taste components at the temperature at which the finished product is served**, and taste them together on one spoon, in the proportions of the product.

### Structural logic: what holds what

Every assembly has a load path, like a building. In a tart, the shell is the container, the cream is held by the shell and must be firm enough to hold the fruit without oozing when sliced. In a layer cake, the sponge layers carry the load, the buttercream is the mortar, and anything soft (fruit, jam, diplomate) must be contained by a firmer ring of buttercream (a **dam**) or by a cake ring and acetate. Ask three questions of every design:

1. **What is the weakest component, and what contains it?** Soft fillings need a wall (the shell, a dam, a ring).
2. **What sets it?** Starch gel (pastry cream), fat crystals (buttercream, ganache), gelatin (diplomate, stabilized Chantilly). Each has a setting temperature and a time. A gelatin-set diplomate needs 1–2 h at ≤ 5 °C before it bears weight; a buttercream crumb coat needs 20–30 min in the refrigerator.
3. **What happens as it warms?** Butter-based components soften sharply above about 20 °C; ganache softens above about 25–28 °C; gelatin gels melt near body temperature. The service temperature is part of the structure.

## Moisture migration: the interface problem

Read the moisture migration section of [SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md) now. The core idea: water does not move toward equal *moisture*, it moves toward equal **water activity** (aw). When two components with different aw touch, or even share the air in a closed box, water moves from the high-aw one to the low-aw one until their aw are close to equal. The low-aw component gains water and changes texture; the high-aw one loses a little and may dry or crust.

| Component | Approximate aw | Moisture | Behaviour at an interface |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baked sucrée shell | ~0.3 | 2–5 % | Loses crispness once it passes roughly aw 0.4–0.5 ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)) |
| Tempered chocolate, cocoa butter | Very low; essentially no free water | < 1 % | Fat film: water crosses it only very slowly |
| Jam, apricot glaze | ~0.80–0.85 | 30–35 % | Sugar-bound water; moderate driver |
| Génoise | ~0.90 | 25–35 % | Takes up syrup; dries to crusts in the air |
| Ganache 1:1 dark | High 0.8s to about 0.9 (depends on sugar and ratio) | 20–25 % | Fat-rich; migrates slowly |
| Buttercream | High water activity in the water droplets, but fat-continuous | 15–20 % | The fat phase slows exchange; a good barrier on sponge |
| Crème pâtissière, Chantilly, fresh fruit | ~0.97–0.99 | 60–85 % | Strong driver: will soak a dry shell in hours |

Values are typical of the product type, not measurements of your batch; aw meters are a Stage 3 tool.

The **rate** matters as much as the direction. Migration is fastest where a wet, low-viscosity water phase is in direct contact with a porous, dry solid: pastry cream on a bare sucrée base is the worst case, and you can feel the softening within an hour. It is slowed by: a **fat layer** (cocoa butter, tempered chocolate, buttercream) because water is poorly soluble in fat; a **protein film** baked on (egg wash); a **sugar-rich layer** that has an intermediate aw (jam); **lower temperature**; and **less contact time**.

### Your four tools against migration

| Tool | How | Where you use it | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bake the base dry** | Fully bake shells until the centre of the base is golden, not pale | Every tart with an unbaked filling | A dry shell still absorbs water; it just starts further from the soggy point |
| **Seal** | Egg wash (yolk + a little cream) brushed on the hot shell and baked 2–3 min; or a thin layer of cocoa butter or tempered chocolate (about 0.1–0.3 mm, 10–20 g on a 22 cm shell); or a thin jam layer under fruit | Fruit tart ([R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md)); choux; tartlets held for a day | Egg is a protein film: it slows water, it does not stop it. Fat layers need full coverage, including the base–wall corner |
| **Match water activity** | Lower the filling's aw with sugar or replace some water with fat (ganache, buttercream) | Chocolate tart ([R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md)) keeps its shell crisp for days | Changes the product's sweetness and texture: a design decision, not a fix |
| **Schedule** | Assemble close to service | Any product with a wet filling on a crisp base | Costs you labour at the worst time; plan it in the timeline |

You will measure how much each seal buys you in [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md). Write your predictions before you run it.

Moisture migration is not always the enemy. A génoise is designed to take up water from a syrup and a moist filling: the day-old layer cake is often better than the fresh one because the sponge has equilibrated with the syrup and buttercream. The design question is always *which interfaces should exchange water, and which must not*.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Why does a crisp shell go soft rather than just damp? The baked sucrée matrix (starch, sugar, protein) is in a rigid, glassy state because it contains very little water. Water acts as a plasticizer: a few percent more moisture lowers the glass transition temperature of the matrix below room temperature, and the solid changes from glassy (snaps) to rubbery (bends). That is why the change is sudden: 1–3 % moisture gain is enough ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md), [SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)).

</div>

## The production timeline: work backward from service

Components have different shelf lives, setting times and quality peaks. Planning starts from the moment the product is served and works backward, exactly like back-scheduling bread in [18.1](stage-1/module-18/lesson-01.md).

### Sort every component

| Category | Components (this module) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **3 days to 3 months ahead (freezes)** | Sucrée dough (raw, 3 months frozen); baked, unfilled shells (1 month frozen); génoise (1 month frozen, well wrapped); Swiss meringue buttercream (3 months frozen, 1 week refrigerated); apricot glaze | Stable, low aw or high fat; freezing does not damage the structure |
| **1–3 days ahead (refrigerated)** | Sucrée dough rested ≥ 2 h, ideally overnight; crème pâtissière (2–3 days, never frozen: it weeps); soaking syrup (1 week); ganache for piping; génoise (best cut on day 2) | Need resting or setting time; quality holds |
| **Same day** | Baking and sealing shells (or day before, airtight); diplomate (use within 24 h); Chantilly (2–4 h; stabilized 24–48 h); the chocolate tart ganache | Short quality life, or needs to be poured into its final container |
| **At assembly (last 1–4 h)** | Cutting fruit; filling fruit tarts; glazing; final decoration | Fastest-degrading steps: fruit weeping, shell softening, glaze setting |

### A worked backward schedule: fruit tart for service at 19:00 on Saturday

| When | Step | Endpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Thursday evening | Mix sucrée ([R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md)), wrap, refrigerate | Dough ≤ 20 °C at wrapping |
| Friday 18:00 | Make crème pâtissière ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)), film in contact, chill | Centre < 21 °C within 2 h, ≤ 5 °C overnight |
| Friday 18:30 | Roll, line, freeze 30 min; blind bake; seal with egg wash | Fully golden base; seal set and glossy |
| Friday 20:00 | Cool shell, store airtight at room temperature | Crisp, cool |
| Saturday 15:30 | Optional cocoa butter layer; prepare glaze | Barrier set (5 min at room temperature) |
| Saturday 16:00 | Wash, dry and cut fruit; re-texture cream 20–40 s | Fruit dry to the touch; cream smooth, ≤ 10 °C |
| Saturday 16:30 | Fill, arrange fruit, glaze | Glaze set to a thin film |
| Saturday 17:00 | Refrigerate ≤ 5 °C | — |
| Saturday 18:45 | Out of refrigerator; slice at 19:00 | Cream still cold; shell crisp |

Notice what the schedule does: every step with a long setting time is early; every step that starts a degradation clock (filling, cutting fruit) is late; and the worst-timed work (filling and fruit) is only about 30 minutes. Write your own for each recipe in this module before you start it. [18.1](stage-1/module-18/lesson-01.md) turns this into a full production plan with several products.

## Mise en place for assembly

Assembly is fast, messy and temperature-sensitive. Before you start:

- **Components at working temperature.** Pastry cream ≤ 5 °C, re-textured just before use; buttercream at 20–22 °C (smooth and spreadable, not shiny or greasy); ganache for pouring at 35–40 °C; génoise chilled for cleaner cutting; hot glaze just off the boil.
- **Tools set out in the order of use:** turntable, cake board, serrated knife, offset spatulas (small and large), bench scraper, piping bags already filled and tipped, pastry brush, sieve, acetate strips, cake ring, a jug of hot water and a cloth for hot knives, scale.
- **The whole product planned on paper:** layer order, weight of each component per layer, décor sketch.
- **A clean, cold landing space** in the refrigerator, level, with nothing strong-smelling (butterfat absorbs odours).
- **A tare sheet.** Put the product on the scale during assembly and record weights as you go. This is how you learn your real portions, and it is how you standardize the product in [18.2](stage-1/module-18/lesson-02.md).

## Portioning and weights

Plan an assembly by weight, not by eye:

1. Decide the number of portions and the target weight per portion (typical plated pastry portion: 100–150 g; a tart slice 120–150 g; a layer-cake slice 110–140 g).
2. Multiply to get the finished weight, then split by component percentages (see the formula tables in [R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md) and [R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md)).
3. Add losses: dough trim (15–25 % of rolled sucrée is trimmed), baking loss (sucrée loses roughly 8–12 % of its weight; génoise 10–15 %), cream left in bowls and bags (5–10 %), fruit trim.
4. Weigh each component into the product; after assembly, weigh the whole and divide.

Round items cut into equal portions only if you cut them with a guide: an 8-portion marker, or scored lines at 45°, or a paper template. Measure the finished portions for your first two products: a spread of more than ±5 % from target means your cutting, not your recipe, needs work.

## Tasting and adjusting

Treat the first assembly of any product as a prototype:

- Taste every component alone, at service temperature, and write one line on each: sweetness (1–5), acidity, dominant flavour, texture.
- Taste a full cross-section: a slice with all layers in one mouthful. Does any component disappear (too little of it or too mild) or dominate?
- Adjust **one component at a time** in the next iteration and record the change: a 20 % cut in the sugar of the Chantilly; lemon zest in the syrup; 0.3 % salt in the ganache.
- Taste at two times: just assembled and at the end of the holding time. Many products improve over 4–12 h as flavours and moisture equilibrate; some (fruit tarts) only get worse.

This is the formulation loop of [19.1](stage-1/module-19/lesson-01.md) at small scale.

## Food safety of cream-filled products

Pastry cream, diplomate, Chantilly and custards are high-aw (above 0.95), near-neutral pH, protein-rich foods: ideal for bacteria. Filled pastries are time and temperature controlled for safety ([SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md), [00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md), [FDA-FoodCode]).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Keep cream-filled pastries at **≤ 5 °C** (41 °F). Standard professional practice is to sell or eat them **within 24 h**, 48 h at most, for pastry cream and diplomate items; the pastry cream itself should be no more than 2–3 days old at assembly, and the product's life counts from when the cream was made, not from assembly. Limit time out of refrigeration to about **2 h** in total (display, service). Never re-refrigerate a filled product that has sat warm for a long time, and never top up an old batch of cream with a new one. Label components with contents and date; declare allergens (gluten, egg, milk, nuts, and gelatin source).

</div>

Ganache tarts and buttercream-finished sponges with no fresh cream fillings are more forgiving (lower aw, more fat), but cream, fresh fruit and diplomate inside them bring them back into the chilled category.

## What goes wrong

- **Crisp at assembly, soggy at service.** An unprotected high-aw filling on a dry base. See [Soggy tart base](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=soggy-tart-base).
- **Assembled product tastes flat.** Components tasted warm or alone; too much sugar everywhere; no acid or salt. Re-taste at service temperature and adjust one component.
- **Schedule collapses on the day.** Components with long setting times started late (pastry cream still warm, buttercream not made, génoise not cooled). Plan backward; nothing that needs more than 1 h to set belongs on the day of service.
- **Filling oozes when sliced.** Structural logic ignored: the filling was softer than the load it carries. See [Tart cream too runny to slice](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=tart-cream-too-runny-to-slice) and [Layer cake layers sliding](troubleshooting/assembled-pastries.md?id=layer-cake-layers-sliding).

## Check yourself

1. A café wants its fruit tartlets filled at 07:00 for sale until 18:00. What do you change in the components and the process so the shells are still crisp at 18:00?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Bake the shells fully until the centre of the base is golden; seal them, ideally with a thin, complete layer of cocoa butter or tempered chocolate (an egg-wash seal alone slows migration but will not hold 11 h); consider a thin jam layer under the fruit; use a firm pastry cream and fruit that has been dried after washing; glaze the fruit to limit weeping; keep them at ≤ 5 °C. Better still, change the schedule: fill in two waves (07:00 and 12:00). This is exactly what [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md) quantifies.

</details>

2. Why does a chocolate ganache tart stay crisp for two or three days while a pastry-cream tart goes soggy within hours, even though ganache contains 20–25 % water?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Migration follows water activity and is slowed by fat. Ganache has a lower aw than pastry cream (its water is held by the sugar and cocoa solids) and a large fat fraction that slows water exchange across the interface. Pastry cream is about aw 0.97 with a mobile water phase in direct contact with the porous shell.

</details>

3. Sort into "freezes", "1–3 days ahead", "same day" and "at assembly": génoise, Chantilly (unstabilized), crème pâtissière, cut strawberries, Swiss meringue buttercream, soaking syrup, blind-baked unfilled sucrée shell.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Freezes: génoise, Swiss meringue buttercream, baked unfilled shell (also sucrée dough). 1–3 days ahead: crème pâtissière (never frozen), soaking syrup (up to a week). Same day: unstabilized Chantilly (2–4 h). At assembly: cut strawberries.

</details>

4. You want 10 portions of 130 g of a fruit tart whose finished product is 20 % shell, 34 % cream, 40 % fruit and 6 % glaze. How much of each component goes into the tart, and how much pastry cream should you make if you lose 8 % in the bowl and bag?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Finished weight 10 × 130 = 1,300 g. Shell 260 g, cream 442 g, fruit 520 g, glaze 78 g. Pastry cream to make: 442 ÷ 0.92 = about 480 g.

</details>

5. A plated tart tastes perfect when you taste the cream warm, but diners find it bland. Give two reasons.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cold suppresses perceived sweetness and aroma, so a cream balanced warm tastes flat at 4–8 °C; and tasting a component alone ignores how the shell and fruit dilute it in a full mouthful. Taste at service temperature and as a cross-section, then adjust (more vanilla, a little salt, sharper fruit).

</details>
