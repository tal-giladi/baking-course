## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** What does "short" mean in a short dough, and what are the three levers that keep gluten short?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

"Short" means crumbly and tender, breaking cleanly, because the gluten exists only as short, discontinuous strands. The levers: fat coating the flour (shielding proteins from water and interrupting strands), scarce water, and minimal mixing after the liquid goes in.

</details>

**A2.** Give typical baker's percentages for butter, sugar, egg and added water in pâte brisée and pâte sucrée.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Brisée: butter 50–60 (to ~75 rich), sugar 0–5, egg or yolk 0–10, water 20–25. Sucrée: butter 50–60, icing sugar 35–40, whole egg ~20, water 0–5 (plus almond flour 0–15).

</details>

**A3.** Contrast sablage and crémage: butter temperature, order of addition, and the typical effect on tenderness, handling and shrinkage.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sablage: cold butter (4–8 °C) rubbed into the dry ingredients before the liquid; more tender and sandy, more fragile to handle, usually less shrinkage. Crémage: soft butter (16–18 °C) mixed with sugar, then egg, then flour; smoother, more cohesive, easier to line, firmer snap; more gluten and shrinkage if mixed long after the flour.

</details>

**A4.** What is fraisage, and why does it homogenize a dough without developing gluten?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Smearing portions of dough across the bench once with the heel of the hand, then gathering. Each portion is compressed and sheared once, distributing butter and moisture evenly, without the repeated stretching and folding that aligns and builds a gluten network.

</details>

**A5.** Name the three things that happen while a short dough rests in the refrigerator, and the minimum rest time.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Hydration evens out; stretched gluten relaxes; butter firms. At least 1–2 hours for a 2 cm disc; overnight is better.

</details>

**A6.** Explain the mechanism of tart-shell shrinkage and the two strategies that prevent it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Rolling and lining stretch gluten strands, storing elastic strain. In the oven, the butter melts before egg and starch set the structure; in that window the strands recoil and the walls drop. Prevention: reduce the strain (little gluten, rest before rolling, never stretch while lining) and shorten the window (chill or freeze the lined shell so the butter stays solid longer).

</details>

**A7.** When do you fully bake a shell, and when do you par-bake it? Give one example of each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fully bake when the filling will not be baked (fruit tart on pastry cream, lemon curd tart, chocolate ganache tart). Par-bake when the shell goes back into the oven with a filling (quiche, crème d'amande tart).

</details>

**A8.** How do butter pieces produce flaky layers in pie dough? What happens if they soften during mixing?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cold flattened butter pieces separate thin sheets of dough. In the oven the dough sets while the butter melts; the butter's water and nearby dough water become steam that pushes the sheets apart, and the fat fries them crisp. If the butter softens, it smears into the flour: no barriers, so no layers; the crust becomes mealy or greasy and tough.

</details>

**A9.** Why is "thick bubbling in the centre" the doneness endpoint for a fruit pie? Why must the pie then cool for hours?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Starch in a sugary fruit juice only thickens fully near boiling, and the centre is the last point to get there; bubbling in the centre proves it did. The starch paste sets further into a gel as it cools, so a hot pie runs even when correctly baked.

</details>

**A10.** What is the ratio for crème d'amande, and how does frangipane differ from it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Equal weights of butter, sugar, almond flour and eggs (1:1:1:1), often with a little flour (about 10 % of the almond weight). Frangipane is crème d'amande combined with crème pâtissière, usually about 2 parts crème d'amande to 1 part pastry cream; it is softer and moister.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling sucrée for tartlets.** You need 12 tartlets of 8 cm, each lined with 45 g of dough, and you lose 20 % of the rolled dough as trim. Using R1-21 (flour 100, butter 60, icing sugar 38, almond flour 12, egg 20, salt 0.8), calculate the dough to mix and each ingredient.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough in shells = 12 × 45 = 540 g. With 20 % loss: 540 ÷ 0.80 = **675 g** to mix.
Total formula % = 230.8 %. Flour = 675 ÷ 2.308 = 292.5 → **292 g**.
Butter = 292 × 0.60 = **175.2 g**. Icing sugar = 292 × 0.38 = **111.0 g**. Almond flour = 292 × 0.12 = **35.0 g**. Egg = 292 × 0.20 = **58.4 g**. Salt = 292 × 0.008 = **2.3 g**.
Check: 292 + 175.2 + 111.0 + 35.0 + 58.4 + 2.3 = 673.9 g ≈ 675 g.

</details>

**B2. A water audit.** A learner's brisée contains 400 g flour, 240 g butter, 8 g salt and 120 g water, no egg. The shells are tough and shrink. (a) Calculate added water % and total water % including butter (16 % water). (b) Rewrite the formula to R1-20 proportions (butter 60, salt 2, yolk 8, water 20) on the same flour, and calculate the new total water %.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Added water = 120 ÷ 400 = **30 %**. Butter water = 240 × 0.16 = 38.4 g. Total = 158.4 ÷ 400 = **39.6 %**.
(b) Flour 400 g, butter 240 g, salt 8 g, yolk 32 g, water 80 g (total 760 g). Total water = 80 + 38.4 + (32 × 0.5 = 16) = 134.4 g → 134.4 ÷ 400 = **33.6 %**. Added water falls by a third (120 → 80 g), so less gluten and less shrinkage; the yolk adds binding and richness with half as much water per gram.

</details>

**B3. Fruit pie filling.** You have 1,200 g of frozen mixed berries. Sugar at 17 % of fruit. Calculate the sugar, and the thickener range for (a) cornstarch and (b) instant tapioca, using the frozen-fruit ranges from 11.3. Which would you choose if you plan to freeze the baked pie?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sugar = 1,200 × 0.17 = **204 g**.
(a) Cornstarch 5–6 % → **60–72 g**. (b) Tapioca 6–7 % → **72–84 g**.
For a pie that will be frozen, tapioca: cornstarch gels weep water after freezing and thawing; tapioca is freeze-stable.

</details>

**B4. Crème d'amande for service.** You need three 22 cm pear tarts, each with 410 g of crème d'amande (R1-23: butter, sugar, almond flour, egg 100 each; flour 10). (a) Calculate each ingredient. (b) After M12 you convert one tart to frangipane at 2 : 1. How much crème d'amande and pastry cream make 410 g of frangipane?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Total = 3 × 410 = 1,230 g. Formula total = 410 % (almond flour = 100). Almond flour = 1,230 ÷ 4.10 = **300 g**; butter, sugar and egg **300 g each**; flour **30 g**. Check: 4 × 300 + 30 = 1,230 g.
(b) 410 × 2/3 = **273 g** crème d'amande; 410 × 1/3 = **137 g** pastry cream.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner makes pâte sucrée on a hot day (kitchen 27 °C). They cream butter and sugar on medium for 5 minutes "until fluffy", add the egg, then mix the flour in on medium for 2 minutes until the dough is smooth. They rest it 20 minutes, roll it, line a 22 cm ring by pushing the dough down the walls, and bake it immediately at 190 °C with beans covering the base only. Result: walls 6 mm lower than the ring and slumped in two places, small blisters all over the base, rim dark brown while the base is pale, and the pastry is hard rather than crisp. Diagnose each symptom and list the corrections in priority order.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Hard texture and shrinkage:** 2 minutes of mixing after the flour, plus a warm dough, developed gluten; only a 20-minute rest, and dough stretched down the walls while lining, stored elastic strain that recoiled in the oven.
- **Slumped walls:** the shell went in warm (no chill), and the beans did not support the walls.
- **Blisters:** over-aeration from 5 minutes of creaming on medium.
- **Dark rim, pale base:** 190 °C is too hot for a 38 % sugar dough; the rim browned before the base baked.

Corrections in priority order: (1) mix the flour on the lowest speed for ≤ 40 s; cream butter and sugar on low only until smooth; (2) rest at least 2 h, ideally overnight, with butter at 16–18 °C at the start and dough ≤ 20 °C; (3) line by lifting the dough into the corner, never pushing it down; (4) freeze the lined shell 20–30 min; (5) fill weights to the rim; (6) bake at 165–170 °C on a preheated tray. In a 27 °C kitchen, chill the dough between steps and roll between parchment.

</details>

**C2.** A blueberry pie made with frozen berries and 3 % cornstarch is baked on the middle rack at 190 °C for 50 minutes, until the top crust is golden, then cut after 45 minutes of cooling. The filling floods the plate, the bottom crust is pale and wet, and the top crust has good layers. What went wrong, and what would you change?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Three separate causes of the runny filling: too little starch for frozen berries (3 % instead of 5–6 %), no evidence the centre reached a boil (the endpoint used was crust colour, not bubbling in the centre), and cut far too early (45 minutes, not 3–4 hours). The pale, wet base: too little bottom heat (middle rack, no preheated tray, no hot start) plus a filling that stayed liquid and soaked it.
Changes: 5–6 % cornstarch (or pre-cook the juice); start at 220 °C on the lowest rack on a preheated tray for 20 min, then 190 °C until thick bubbling in the centre vents (tent the top if needed); cool 3–4 h. Consider a mealy or hybrid bottom crust. The good top layers show the dough itself was made correctly.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Replacing half the added water in pâte brisée with 40 % ABV vodka makes the baked shell more tender and reduces shrinkage, because ethanol does not hydrate gluten proteins the way water does."

Design an experiment: state the independent and dependent variables, controlled variables, batch size, how you make the comparison fair, what you measure and how, and one confounding factor.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** liquid composition: A all water (R1-20 control, 20 % added water); B half the water replaced by the same weight of vodka. Optionally C: the same total *water* as B but no vodka (about 16.5 % added water: 10 % water plus the ~6.5 % water contained in 10 % vodka) to separate "less water" from "ethanol present".
- **Why C matters:** 40 % ABV vodka is about two-thirds water by weight, so B contains less water than A. Without C, a difference could be caused simply by less water, not by ethanol.
- **Dependent variables:** diameter shrinkage of 10 cm discs and wall height loss in identical rings (as in X1-32); break load of bars (as in X1-30); blind tenderness panel; handling scores (cracking, stickiness).
- **Controlled:** same flour, butter lot and temperature, mixing method and time, rest time (e.g., 2 h), rolled thickness (3 mm guides), chill before baking, oven and bake times, cooling before measuring.
- **Batch:** 150 g flour per variant (285 g dough), enough for three 10 cm discs and four bars, or one 16 cm ring.
- **Fairness:** mix in randomized order with timing offsets so each variant rests the same time; bake all on one tray; code samples for the panel; replicate on a second day.
- **Confounders:** the water content difference above; ethanol evaporating from an open bowl during mixing (weigh quickly, cover); dough temperature differences (record them).
- **Expected:** B more tender and slightly less shrinkage than A; the comparison with C shows how much of that is simply lower water. The ethanol evaporates during baking.

</details>
