## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Why does the temperature of a boiling sugar syrup tell you its concentration? Under what condition is that true?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dissolved sugar lowers water's vapour pressure, raising the boiling point; as water boils off, concentration and boiling point rise together, so each boiling temperature corresponds to one concentration. It holds at a given air pressure: at altitude every stage arrives at a lower temperature (about 1 °C lower per 300 m), and your thermometer must be calibrated.

</details>

**A2.** Give the sea-level temperature range and a typical use for soft ball, firm ball and hard crack.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Soft ball 112–116 °C: fondant, fudge. Firm ball 118–120 °C: Italian meringue, pâte à bombe, soft caramels. Hard crack 146–154 °C: brittle, lollipops, spun sugar, sugar glass.

</details>

**A3.** What is 50 °Brix, and why do you measure a flavoured soaking syrup before adding the liqueur?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

50 g of sugar per 100 g of syrup, a 1:1 simple syrup by weight. A refractometer reads all dissolved solids and alcohol shifts the reading, so a syrup with liqueur does not give a true sucrose value. Measure the sugar syrup first, then add the spirit by weight.

</details>

**A4.** List four techniques that prevent a cooked syrup from crystallizing in the pan, and say which two control *nucleation* and which one controls *supersaturation or crystal growth*.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dissolve completely before boiling; stop stirring after the boil; wash down the walls with a wet brush (or cover to steam them); add glucose syrup, invert sugar or a little acid. Washing down/complete dissolution and not stirring prevent nucleation (no seeds, no agitation). Interfering agents hinder crystal growth and nucleation by getting in the way of sucrose molecules and raising viscosity (they also lower the effective sucrose supersaturation).

</details>

**A5.** Explain why beating a syrup at 45 °C gives smooth fondant while beating it at 100 °C gives a grainy mass.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 45 °C the syrup is highly supersaturated and viscous, so agitation creates huge numbers of nuclei at once and each crystal grows little: many tiny crystals, too small to feel. At 100 °C supersaturation is low and molecules move freely, so few nuclei form and they grow large: grainy.

</details>

**A6.** Rank French, Swiss and Italian meringue by stability and by specific gravity (lightest first). Give the mechanism in one sentence.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stability: Italian ≥ Swiss > French. SG: French (lightest) < Italian ≤ Swiss (densest). Fully dissolved sugar and heat that partly unfolds the proteins (60–70 °C in Swiss, a 118–121 °C syrup in Italian) give finer bubbles with thicker, less mobile films: denser but much more stable than the raw French foam with partly undissolved sugar.

</details>

**A7.** What temperature makes a Swiss meringue pasteurization-safe, and why is an Italian meringue not considered a pasteurization step?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

71 °C (160 °F), the FDA recommendation for egg dishes. In the Italian method the syrup heats the whites briefly and unevenly; the foam is not held at a verified temperature. Use pasteurized whites for uncooked uses served to vulnerable people.

</details>

**A8.** Distinguish weeping from beading in a meringue, with one cause each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Weeping: syrup leaking out, often pooling underneath; caused by undissolved sugar, a French meringue left standing, or an unset underside on a pie. Beading: small amber droplets on the surface; caused by overbaking (overcoagulated proteins squeezing out syrup) or undissolved sugar.

</details>

**A9.** A buttercream looks like cottage cheese. Another is glossy and runny. Name the cause of each in terms of butterfat and the fix.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cottage cheese = curdled: too cold (below about 17 °C), fat too solid to flow around the syrup. Warm the bowl gently while beating. Glossy and runny = soupy: too warm (above about 24–25 °C), too much fat melted to hold air and syrup. Chill 10–15 min and rewhip.

</details>

**A10.** Why does a crème mousseline get its butter in two stages?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The first half melts into the hot pastry cream and enriches it without needing to hold air. The second half, beaten into the cream at 18–22 °C while plastic, builds the light, fat-continuous structure that lets it hold a shape. Adding all the butter to cold cream curdles it; adding all to hot cream gives a greasy, dense cream.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Italian meringue at scale.** You need Italian meringue from **250 g** of egg white at 1 : 2, with 10 % of the sugar whipped into the whites and the rest cooked with water at 30 % of the syrup sugar weight to 118 °C (about 87 % sugar). Calculate: the sugar in the whites and in the syrup, the water, the weight of finished syrup, the water evaporated, the weight of finished meringue and its sugar percentage.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total sugar = 2 × 250 = 500 g. In the whites: 50 g. In the syrup: **450 g**. Water = 0.30 × 450 = **135 g**.
Finished syrup = 450 ÷ 0.87 = **517 g** (containing 67 g water). Evaporated = 135 − 67 = **68 g**.
Meringue = 250 + 50 + 517 = **817 g**. Sugar = 500 ÷ 817 = **61 %**.

</details>

**B2. Soaking syrup by °Brix.** You need **600 g** of soaking syrup at **35 °Brix** for three génoise layers, made from your stock of 1:1 simple syrup (50 °Brix) and water. Then you add 30 g of rum. How much simple syrup and water, and what is the sugar percentage after the rum?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sugar needed = 0.35 × 600 = 210 g. Simple syrup = 210 ÷ 0.50 = **420 g**. Water = 600 − 420 = **180 g**.
After 30 g rum: 210 ÷ 630 = **33 %** sugar by weight.

</details>

**B3. Scaling SMBC.** Your SMBC formula is whites 100, sugar 160, butter 280, salt 1, vanilla 5 (total 546 g). You need **1,200 g** to fill and coat a 20 cm three-layer cake, plus 5 % for bowl and bag losses. Calculate each ingredient.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Target = 1,200 ÷ 0.95 = **1,263 g** (or 1,200 × 1.05 = 1,260 g; either approach is acceptable if stated). Factor = 1,263 ÷ 546 = **2.313**.
Whites = 231 g · Sugar = 370 g · Butter = 648 g · Salt = 2.3 g · Vanilla = 11.6 g.
Check: 231 + 370 + 648 + 2.3 + 11.6 = 1,262.9 g.

</details>

**B4. Correcting targets and checking dryness.** In your kitchen, water boils at **96.0 °C** on your thermometer. (a) What do you cook an Italian-meringue syrup (target 119 °C) and a sugar glass (target 150 °C) to? (b) A tray of 1 : 2 French meringue nests weighed **420 g** piped. What baked weight range means they are fully dried?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Correction = 100 − 96 = 4 °C. Italian syrup: **115 °C** on your thermometer. Sugar glass: **146 °C**.
(b) Target loss 25–28 %: 420 × 0.75 = 315 g; 420 × 0.72 = 302 g. Fully dried at **about 302–315 g**. Heavier than about 328 g (loss under ~22 %) means wet centres.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A student bakes French meringue kisses at 130 °C for 45 minutes. They come out beige, with fine cracks, crisp outside but sticky in the centre. The student stores the rest in a box in the refrigerator; the next day they are soft and tacky all through. Diagnose every fault and give the corrected process.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Beige colour and cracks:** oven far too hot (130 °C, possibly more if the oven runs hot): browning reactions and rapid expansion of the air before the surface could stretch.
- **Sticky centre:** too short and too hot; the outside set and coloured before the inside dried.
- **Soft next day:** the refrigerator is humid and dried meringue is a hygroscopic sugar glass; it absorbed water.

Corrected: oven measured at **90–100 °C**, kisses **60–90 min**, then 1 h in the switched-off oven with the door ajar; check weight loss (25–28 %) and a broken test piece; store airtight at room temperature with a desiccant, never refrigerated. See [baked meringue cracked or browned](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=baked-meringue-cracked-or-browned) and [baked meringue sticky or chewy](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=baked-meringue-sticky-or-chewy).

</details>

**C2.** A student making SMBC heats the whites and sugar to 55 °C, whips for 6 minutes (the meringue reads 38 °C), then adds butter at 21 °C. The cream becomes soupy. They refrigerate the bowl for 45 minutes and beat it straight out: now it looks curdled and lumpy. When it is finally smooth, it feels slightly gritty on the tongue. Diagnose the three faults and give the fixes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Gritty:** at 55 °C the sugar did not fully dissolve (and the meringue was not pasteurized). Heat to 65–70 °C (71 °C for safety) until no grit is felt; the grit cannot be removed later.
2. **Soupy:** the meringue was at 38 °C, well above the ≤25 °C target, and the butter was at the warm end of its range; the butterfat melted. Whip the meringue until ≤25 °C (10–15 min) before adding butter at 18–20 °C.
3. **Curdled after 45 min chilling:** over-corrected; the fat went too solid. Chill for 10–15 min only, check the temperature, and rewhip; if curdled, warm the bowl sides gently while beating to reach 19–22 °C.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis:** *An Italian meringue made with syrup cooked to 121 °C holds piped rosettes better over 4 hours than one made with syrup at 115 °C.* Design an experiment to test it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:

- **Independent variable:** syrup endpoint: 115, 118 and 121 °C (three levels show a trend, not just a difference), corrected by calibrating the thermometer.
- **Controlled:** pooled whites weighed into equal portions (e.g. 60 g each) at 20 °C; same sugar (120 g per portion: 12 g in whites, 108 g in syrup with 36 g water); same pan, mixer, speeds; whites started at the same syrup temperature relative to each endpoint (e.g. 8 °C before it); same pouring technique; whip to the same final temperature (30 °C); same tip and rosette weight (10 g); same room conditions; order randomized or rotated.
- **Dependent:** SG; rosette height and ridge definition at 0, 1, 2, 4 h; drainage from 50 g in a strainer; appearance of weeping.
- **Replication:** at least two batches per level, on different days if possible, to see whether differences exceed batch-to-batch variation.
- **Analysis:** plot height retention (% of initial) over time for each level; the hypothesis is supported if retention rises consistently with syrup temperature beyond the replicate spread. Note confounders: higher syrup temperature also lowers water in the meringue, so record finished meringue weight.

</details>
