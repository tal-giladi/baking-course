## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** After a standard (one-fat-layer) lock-in, a dough receives 2 double turns and 1 single turn. How many fat and dough layers does it have? Write the general formula.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

F = 3^s × 4^d = 3 × 16 = **48** fat layers; D = F + 1 = **49** dough layers. General: F = 3^s × 4^d (× 2 for an English lock-in), D = F + 1.

</details>

**A2.** Describe what butter does under the rolling pin at 7 °C, 14 °C and 21 °C, and what each does to the baked product.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

7 °C: brittle; cracks into plates and shards; the dough stretches over the gaps. Baked: irregular rise, big holes beside fused dense zones, leakage through the gaps. 14 °C: plastic; flows as a continuous sheet. Baked: distinct layers, good lift. 21 °C: soft and greasy; smears into the dough and squeezes out. Baked: bready interior, few distinct layers, greasy, leakage.

</details>

**A3.** Puff pastry is baked at 210–220 °C, then at 180–190 °C. What does each phase do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The hot phase heats the surface layers quickly so that they set while the steam generated from the water in the dough and butter is trapped between fat-separated leaves: maximum lift, and the butter has no time to run out before the leaves are rigid. The lower phase dries the inner leaves without burning the outside; without it the inner leaves stay wet and collapse as the steam condenses on cooling.

</details>

**A4.** Give four rules for cutting and finishing puff pastry so that it rises straight, with the reason for each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Sharp blade, straight down, no dragging: a dragged or crushed edge seals the layers. (2) Trim all folded edges: a fold has no open layers. (3) Egg wash on the top only: wash on an edge glues the layers. (4) Rest cut pieces 30 minutes, cold: relaxed gluten does not shrink or pull the piece lopsided. Also: roll in both directions; flip rounds so the cleaner cut is on top; even sheet thickness with guides.

</details>

**A5.** Why is croissant détrempe mixed only to moderate development, when a lean bread dough is mixed to a good windowpane?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The turns and the final sheeting stretch and develop the gluten a great deal more. A détrempe fully developed in the mixer becomes too elastic: it fights the rolling pin (forcing, warming and smearing the butter), and the triangles shrink after cutting. Moderate development leaves room for the lamination to finish the job.

</details>

**A6.** What sets the proof ceiling of about 27–28 °C for croissants, and what happens above it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The butter, not the yeast. Above about 28 °C a large fraction of the butterfat is liquid; the butter layers soften, merge with the dough and seep out of the edges. Layers are lost before baking: butter on the tray, less lift, bready crumb, greasy base.

</details>

**A7.** Why do croissants use 12–27 fat layers while puff pastry uses 256–729?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Croissant dough layers must contain yeast gas cells to form a honeycomb, so they must stay relatively thick (about 100–220 µm at 4 mm). Hundreds of layers would make each dough leaf only micrometres thick, thinner than a starch granule; the leaves would fuse into a fine, bready crumb. Puff has no yeast: its leaves only have to separate and fry, so many thin layers give a fine, even lift.

</details>

**A8.** Blitz and classic puff contain the same butter. Give two reasons why blitz rises less and less evenly.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Its butter is in discontinuous, overlapping streaks, not continuous sheets: where there is no butter, leaves fuse; where streaks overlap, a thick layer forms, so fewer effective barriers trap steam and the rise is uneven. More flour is left uncoated by fat, so more gluten forms: more shrinkage and tilting.

</details>

**A9.** Why is a Danish proofed to 1.5–2× and its open fillings added after the proof?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A less expanded dough is stronger: it holds the weight of the filling and keeps sharp shapes. Filling after the proof stops the filling sliding off as the dough grows, and lets you re-press a clean well into the soft, proofed centre.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1.** A café orders 30 croissants of 60 g raw weight. Your trim and loss run at 15 % of the laminated dough. Scale [R1-34](recipes/R1-34-croissant.md) (total 1,156 g) and give the grams of every ingredient. How would you split the work by hand?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough needed = 30 × 60 ÷ 0.85 = **2,117.6 g**. Factor = 2,117.6 ÷ 1,156 = **1.832**.
Bread flour 250 × 1.832 = **458 g**; all-purpose **458 g**; water **256.5 g**; milk **256.5 g**; sugar **100.7 g**; salt **18.3 g**; instant yeast **11.0 g**; détrempe butter **55.0 g**; beurrage **503.7 g** (total ≈ 2,117.7 g).
A block of 2.1 kg is too large to laminate and sheet by hand on a home bench. Make **two blocks** (each at ×0.916, or simply two full R1-34 batches if you accept a little surplus), staggered by 30–40 minutes so one rests while you turn the other.

</details>

**B2.** A croissant dough with butter at 28 % of the volume is sheeted to 3.5 mm. Calculate the nominal butter-layer and dough-layer thickness for 3 single turns and for 1 double + 1 single. Which would you expect to give the more open honeycomb?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Butter total 0.28 × 3.5 = 0.98 mm; dough 2.52 mm.
3 singles: F = 27 → 0.98 ÷ 27 ≈ **36 µm** butter; D = 28 → 2.52 ÷ 28 = **90 µm** dough.
1D + 1S: F = 12 → 0.98 ÷ 12 ≈ **82 µm** butter; D = 13 → 2.52 ÷ 13 ≈ **194 µm** dough.
1D + 1S gives thicker dough leaves that can hold larger gas cells: a **more open** honeycomb with fewer, bolder steps. 3 singles gives a finer, more even structure.

</details>

**B3.** A classic puff recipe uses 360 g of beurrage. You want an 8 mm plaque. What square size do you shape (butter density 0.92 g/cm³), and what size of détrempe square do you need for an envelope lock-in?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Volume = 360 ÷ 0.92 ≈ 391 cm³. Area = 391 ÷ 0.8 ≈ 489 cm² → side √489 ≈ **22 cm**. Envelope: dough side ≈ butter diagonal = 22.1 × √2 ≈ 31.3 cm → roll the détrempe to about **32 × 32 cm**, slightly thicker in the centre.

</details>

**B4.** You raise the beurrage of [R1-35](recipes/R1-35-danish.md) from 60 % to 70 % of flour. Give the new butter weight, the new total and the butter share of the laminated dough. If the plaque stays 18 × 18 cm, how thick is it? What size would keep it at 8 mm? Name two process consequences.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Beurrage 400 × 0.70 = **280 g** (+40 g). Total 734 + 280 = **1,014 g**. Share 280 ÷ 1,014 ≈ **27.6 %** (from 24.6 %).
Plaque volume 280 ÷ 0.92 ≈ 304 cm³; at 18 × 18 cm (324 cm²) → **9.4 mm**. For 8 mm: 304 ÷ 0.8 = 380 cm² → **19.5 × 19.5 cm** (and a détrempe of about 20.5–21 × 40 cm for the open lock-in).
Consequences: more butter to keep cold, so more frequent rests and tighter temperature control; thicker fat layers at the same turns, so more risk of leakage; flakier, richer product.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** Croissants made in July in a 29 °C kitchen. The plaque was left on the bench for 30 minutes before lock-in (it read 21 °C) and butter squeezed out of the ends during the double turn. To save time, the block rested 15 minutes in the freezer after each turn; it read 7 °C before the single turn and "crackled" when rolled. Proof: oven with the light on, 80 minutes (a thermometer later showed 31 °C). Bake: 170 °C conventional for 25 minutes "because the tops were browning". Result: a pool of butter under every croissant, low and spread shapes, a crumb that is bready in some zones with large irregular holes in others, and pale creases. Diagnose in timeline order and give the corrections.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Lock-in and double turn, butter too warm (21 °C):** butter smeared and squeezed out; layers partly lost → the bready zones. Correction: plaque at 12–15 °C; in a hot kitchen temper it for only a few minutes, chill bench and pin.
2. **Freezer rest, butter too cold (7 °C):** the butter shattered during the single turn → the large irregular holes and fused zones, and gaps through which butter later ran. Correction: 20–30 minute refrigerator rests; no freezer.
3. **Proof at 31 °C:** above the proof ceiling; butter softened and seeped, more layers fused → leakage, spread, bready crumb. Correction: 24–27 °C with a thermometer in the box; expect 2–3 h.
4. **Bake at 170 °C:** too cool; butter ran before the leaves set (more leakage), and the inner layers never dried (pale creases, will collapse and soften). Correction: 200 °C conventional (190 °C fan) for 16–20 minutes, to deep colour including the creases; if the tops darken, tent with foil rather than lowering the temperature this much.
Change the process back to the standard temperature budget for the next batch, log every temperature, then use X1-40 to confirm which step mattered most if needed.

</details>

**C2.** Vol-au-vent cut from a 5 mm sheet of classic puff rose only to 12–15 mm, leaned to one side, and sank slightly as they cooled; the inside was pale and pasty. The cook used a slightly blunt fluted cutter with a twisting motion, egg-washed the whole piece including the sides, baked immediately after cutting, and baked at 190 °C for 18 minutes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Low and leaning: the blunt, twisted cutter sealed the edges, and egg wash on the sides glued the layers (the side with more wash rose least); the pieces were not rested, so stretched gluten pulled them out of shape. Low rise overall also from the cool start: 190 °C is too low for the lift phase. Sinking and pasty centre: underbaked; no drying phase. Corrections: sharp plain cutter pressed straight down, flip the pieces; wash the top only; rest cut pieces 30 minutes in the refrigerator; bake at 210–220 °C for about 15 minutes, then 180–190 °C for 10–15 minutes until dry inside; scoop out any soft centre.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Using an 84 %-fat butter as beurrage instead of an 80 %-fat butter gives croissants with a more open honeycomb and less butter leakage, with the same process."

Design the experiment: state the variables, batch plan, what you measure and how, and the main confounders.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** beurrage butter fat content, 80 % vs 84 % (two brands, or one brand's standard and "extra" butter), same weight (for example R1-34 at ×0.5, 137.5 g each). Record the fat content from the labels; ideally unsalted both.
- **Batch plan:** one détrempe mixed as one batch and divided in two (to remove détrempe variation), retarded together. Two plaques of identical dimensions. Laminate one after the other with identical temperatures (plaque 12–15 °C measured, dough 4–8 °C), rests and turns (1D + 1S). Sheet both to 4 mm, 8 triangles each, weights balanced. Proof together at 24–26 °C to the same endpoint; bake together, positions alternated.
- **Dependent variables:** handling during lamination (cracking, smearing, notes), fat on pre-weighed foils after baking (g per croissant), baked height and weight, honeycomb score from lengthwise cross-sections (scored blind), panel for flakiness and greasiness (coded samples).
- **Confounders:** butter brands differ in more than fat content (crystal structure, season, cultured or not), so the plasticity at the same temperature may differ; measure firmness (bend test at the same temperature) and consider it part of the result. The 84 % butter contains less water, so there is slightly less steam from the butter. The order of lamination (the second block waits longer): alternate the order in a repeat. Replicate the experiment on a second day before drawing a conclusion.
- **Expected:** the drier butter is typically more plastic and less prone to smearing, which tends to give slightly better layer definition and less leakage, but the effect may be small compared with temperature control. The experiment tells you whether it is large enough to matter for your process.

</details>

## E. Practical assessment

**Product:** in one production (two days), from your own lamination: 6 croissants and 3 pains au chocolat from [R1-34](recipes/R1-34-croissant.md) (×0.5), and 6 squares of classic puff pastry from [R1-33](recipes/R1-33-puff-pastry.md) (×0.5), with a full production log (temperatures at lock-in, block temperatures before and after every turn, proof temperature and time, oven temperature by thermometer, bake times).

**Specification:**
- Croissants: raw weight 55–60 g (± 3 g between pieces); 3–4 distinct steps; baked height ≥ 6 cm; deep golden-brown to mahogany including creases and base; open honeycomb to the centre (score ≥ 4 on the X1-40 scale); no pooled butter on the tray (a thin film is acceptable).
- Pains au chocolat: seam underneath, batons centred, same colour and crumb standard; no chocolate leaking from the ends.
- Puff squares: 6 × 6 cm from a 3 mm sheet; mean height ≥ 18 mm (lift ≥ 6×); difference between highest and lowest edge ≤ 4 mm; straight sides; dry, crisp inner leaves.

| Criterion | Pass | Merit | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature control | Plaque 12–15 °C at lock-in; block ≤ 16 °C at every turn; proof ≤ 27 °C | Every reading logged, with the rest times; block within 10–15 °C throughout | Any unrecorded stage, or butter shattered or smeared during lamination |
| Layer arithmetic | Fat-layer count stated for both doughs, correctly | Nominal layer thickness calculated for both at final sheet thickness | Missing or wrong |
| Croissant shape and weight | Within weight tolerance; 3–4 steps; tip underneath | Uniform set: height range ≤ 1 cm | Outside tolerance, unrolled, misshapen |
| Proof | 2–2.5× with jiggle; time recorded | Photos at start and end with volume ratio estimated | Under- or overproofed (split sides, collapse) |
| Croissant bake | Deep colour including creases; light and crisp | Colour even across the tray; bake loss recorded | Pale creases, or burnt |
| Crumb | Honeycomb score ≥ 4 on a cross-section photo with ruler | Score 5; open to the centre | Bready or dense core (≤ 3) |
| Butter on tray | Thin film only | Fat on foil measured for one piece | Pools of butter |
| Puff | Mean height ≥ 18 mm, evenness ≤ 4 mm, dry inside | Height ≥ 24 mm with fine, regular leaves | Low, lopsided or doughy |
| Diagnosis | One fault (or the weakest attribute) identified with evidence (tray, cross-section, log) and a correction | A one-variable test designed to confirm it | No diagnosis, or a guess without evidence |
| Safety | Knife handled safely; raw dough not tasted; oven burns avoided | — | Any unsafe practice |

Pass requires "Pass" or better in every row. Safety "Not yet" is a fail regardless of the rest.
