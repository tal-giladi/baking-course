# M03 assessment · Yeast and fermentation: lean bread

Covers [03.1](stage-1/module-03/lesson-01.md)–[03.4](stage-1/module-03/lesson-04.md), [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md), [R1-02](recipes/R1-02-rolls-pan-loaf.md), [X1-07](experiments/X1-07-fermentation-temperature.md)–[X1-09](experiments/X1-09-proof-level.md). Work without notes first, then check the answers. Pass mark: 80 % on A–D, plus a pass on E.

## A. Knowledge check

1. Give the working conversion ratio between fresh, active dry and instant yeast, and one reason it is only approximate.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fresh : active dry : instant ≈ 1 : 0.5 : 0.33. Published ranges run 0.4–0.5 for active dry and 0.33–0.4 for instant. It varies with brand and strain, with the moisture content (fresh yeast ~70 %), and with age and storage.

</details>

2. In a lean dough without added sugar, which sugar feeds the yeast for most of bulk fermentation, and where does it come from?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Maltose, produced from damaged starch by the flour's amylases (mainly β-amylase). The yeast takes it up and splits it with maltase. Flour's own free sugars and sucrose (split by invertase) are used up early.

</details>

3. Name the two main products of yeast fermentation and what happens to each in the oven.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

CO₂ expands in the gas cells and drives oven spring, and it escapes as the crumb sets and the loaf cools. Ethanol vaporizes (boiling point ~78 °C), adds to the oven spring, and leaves the loaf.

</details>

4. Give approximate temperatures for: optimum dough fermentation (flavour/rate balance), fastest gas production, and yeast death.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

~24–27 °C, ~32–35 °C, ~55–60 °C.

</details>

5. State the rule of thumb for how fermentation rate changes with temperature in the working range.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It roughly doubles for every 8–10 °C increase (and halves for every 8–10 °C decrease), between about 10 and 32 °C.

</details>

6. What volume increase marks the end of bulk for a lean yeasted dough, and why use a straight-sided container?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

+50–75 %. In a straight-sided container, volume is proportional to height, so the % rise can be read from a mark. Flared bowls mislead the eye.

</details>

7. Describe the poke-test response for under-, well- and over-proofed dough, and give one situation where the test misleads you.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Under: springs back quickly and fully. Ready: springs back slowly and partly, leaving a small dent. Over: the dent stays, or the dough deflates. It misleads with cold retarded dough (firm, springs back even when ready), with very wet or very stiff doughs, and with very strong or very weak flour.

</details>

8. Why is a bench rest needed between pre-shaping and shaping?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Pre-shaping stresses the gluten network. The dough is viscoelastic, and given 15–30 min the stress relaxes, so the final shape can build a taut skin without tearing.

</details>

9. What angle and depth do you score at to get an ear, and what does scoring control?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 30–45° to the surface, ~5–8 mm deep, in one stroke. Scoring controls where the loaf opens, how fully it can expand, the shape of the opening (the ear), and the appearance.

</details>

10. Why does dry salt left on dispersed yeast harm it, while salt mixed into flour with instant yeast does not?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A concentrated salt solution around the cells pulls water out of them (osmotic stress) and can damage them. When salt is spread through the flour and mixed promptly, the local concentration never gets that high.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1.** A formula for one loaf lists 12 g fresh yeast. You only have instant yeast. Give the instant quantity by the working rule and by the 0.4 rule, and decide what to use.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Working rule: 12 × 0.33 = 3.96 ≈ 4 g. By the 0.4 rule: 12 × 0.4 = 4.8 g. Use 4 g for a normal bulk time. If your instant yeast is old or the kitchen is cool, use up to 4.8 g. Watch the rise and record the result for next time. The 12 g fresh yeast contained ~8.4 g water, so adding ~8 g water keeps the hydration identical. At a 500 g flour batch this is 1.7 % and optional.

</details>

**B2.** Scale R1-01 (100 / 70 / 2 / 0.8 %) to make **three loaves of 900 g dough each**, allowing **2 % process loss**. Give each ingredient in grams (rounded to whole grams) and check the total.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough required: 3 × 900 = 2700 g. With 2 % loss: 2700 / 0.98 = 2755 g.
Total % = 172.8. Flour = 2755 / 1.728 = 1594.4 → **1595 g**.
Water = 0.70 × 1595 = 1116.5 → **1117 g**. Salt = 0.02 × 1595 = 31.9 → **32 g**. Instant yeast = 0.008 × 1595 = 12.76 → **12.8 g** (13 g on a 1 g scale).
Total = 1595 + 1117 + 32 + 12.8 = **2756.8 g** ≥ 2755 g. ✓ Divide into 3 × 900 g; ~57 g covers the loss.

</details>

**B3.** You want a DDT of 25 °C. Flour 20 °C, room 23 °C, friction factor (stand mixer, measured) 6 °C. What water temperature do you use? The dough comes out at 27 °C anyway. By roughly how much should you expect the bulk time to change, and what does that do to a planned 2 h bulk?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water = 3 × 25 − 20 − 23 − 6 = 75 − 49 = **26 °C**.
At 27 °C instead of 25 °C (+2 °C), the rate factor is ≈ 2^(2/9) ≈ 1.17. Bulk is about 15 % faster: ~2 h → ~1 h 45 min. Start checking the marked container at 1 h 30 min. Next time, the friction factor you measured was too low. Recalculate it from the actual result: friction = 3 × 27 − 20 − 23 − 26 = 12 °C.

</details>

**B4.** A colleague hands you: flour 1000 g, water 720 g, salt 25 g, fresh yeast 15 g. Express it in baker's %, give the instant-yeast equivalent, and comment on anything unusual.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water 72 %, salt 2.5 %, fresh yeast 1.5 % (≈ 0.5 % instant = 5 g). Total 176 %. Hydration is fine for bread flour. **Salt at 2.5 % is high** for a lean bread. It will taste salty and ferment more slowly, so check whether it is intentional. The yeast is on the low side, so expect a longer bulk (~2.5–3.5 h at 24 °C). That suits a flavour-driven schedule.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting case

**Case 1.** A learner reports: "Dough mixed at 21 °C in a 19 °C kitchen. Bulk 2 h as the recipe said, and it rose maybe a third. Shaped fine, felt tight. Proofed 45 min, springy poke. The loaf burst along one side near the base, the score barely opened, and the crumb is dense at the bottom with a couple of big tunnels near the top. The crust is well browned."

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Diagnosis:** under-fermentation in both bulk and proof, caused by low temperature.
- The dough at 21 °C in a 19 °C room ran ~30–40 % slower than the recipe assumed, so 2 h gave only ~+30 % instead of +50–75 %.
- A springy poke after 45 min means under-proof. The dough had strong elasticity and unused fermentation potential, so in the oven it sprang violently and ruptured at the weakest point (a side blowout), not at the score.
- A dense base with tunnels near the top is the typical under-proof crumb. Good browning fits too: plenty of residual sugar.

**Corrections:** set the water temperature from DDT so the dough starts at 24 °C, and ferment in a warmer spot (oven with the light on, or a cooler with warm water). Judge bulk by +50–75 % on the container mark and proof by a slow partial spring-back, not by time. Score slightly deeper if the dough is still a little under.

</details>

**Case 2.** "Kitchen was 30 °C. Used the recipe's 0.8 % yeast. Dough more than doubled in 1 h 40 min and the top went flat. It was sticky and hard to shape and smelled like beer. The loaf spread when I turned it out, the score didn't open, and the crust is pale. The crumb has a band of big holes under the top."

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Diagnosis:** over-fermentation in bulk (and likely proof), caused by high temperature.
- At ~30 °C, fermentation runs roughly 1.5× faster than at 24 °C. The dough passed +75 %, peaked (flat top) and started to decline. The beer smell is ethanol accumulating.
- The slack, sticky dough couldn't hold tension, so the loaf spread. The network was over-stretched, so there was no oven spring and the score didn't open. Cells merged under the skin, giving the band of holes under the crust.
- The pale crust comes from depleted residual sugar.

**Corrections:** cold water, so the dough reaches 24 °C. Ferment somewhere cooler (a cooler with a cold pack), or cut the yeast to ~0.4–0.5 %, or both. End bulk at +50–75 %. Start proof checks at 25–30 min. When over-proof is unavoidable, score shallowly and bake at once.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "A 12-hour retarded final proof at 4 °C gives a better-tasting R1-01 than a same-day 1 h proof at 24 °C."

Design an experiment that tests this fairly. State the variables, the batch plan, how you will handle the timing, and how you will assess "better-tasting".

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Good design:**
- **Independent variable:** final proof regime. Same-day (≈1 h at 24 °C to a slow partial spring-back) vs retarded (15 min at room temperature, then 12 h at a measured 3–5 °C, baked from cold).
- **Controlled:** one R1-01 batch mixed and bulk-fermented together (×2, 1000 g flour), divided into 2 × 864 g, shaped by the same person with the same shape and banneton type. Same score and same bake programme and vessel, baked to ≥96 °C. Log the fridge temperature where the dough sits.
- **Timing problem:** the two loaves bake 11 h apart, so they would be tasted at different ages. Fix: taste both at the **same age after baking** (for example, both cooled 3 h), or freeze the first loaf and thaw both identically. Better still, run the design both ways on two days to balance out day effects.
- **Dependent measures:** blind triangle test or ranked tasting by at least 3 tasters (slices coded, order randomized): aroma, wheatiness, acidity, overall liking. Also crust colour, blistering, height/width ratio, and crumb photo.
- **Replication:** repeat at least once. One pair of loaves can't separate the treatment effect from bake-to-bake variation ([SC-14](stage-1/science/sc-14-measurement-sensory.md) covers this in M18/M19).
- **Pre-registered prediction:** write down which loaf you expect to win, and on which attribute, before tasting.

</details>
