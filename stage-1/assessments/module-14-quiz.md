# M14 assessment · Choux pastry

Work on paper or in your lab notebook first, then open the answers. Allow about 75 min for sections A–D. Section E is a two-day practical (pastry cream and craquelin on day 1).

## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** What is the only leavening agent in choux, and where does it come from? What two components set the expanded shell?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Steam, from the water in the paste (from the water, milk, butter and, above all, the eggs), which vaporizes when the interior reaches about 100 °C. The shell is set by coagulated egg proteins and the dried, gelatinized starch–gluten matrix of the panade.

</details>

**A2.** Why must the butter be completely melted at the moment the liquid reaches a full boil?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

If the liquid boils while butter is still melting, water evaporates for an uncontrolled time, so the panade holds less water than planned and needs a different amount of egg. Also, at a rolling boil the fat is dispersed through the liquid, so the flour hydrates and gelatinizes evenly.

</details>

**A3.** Give three things that happen while you dry the panade (dessèchement), and one sign that you have gone too far.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Starch gelatinization is completed (paste at 75–80 °C); water is driven off (typically 15–35 g for R1-31), making room for more egg; the gluten proteins are worked and partly heat-set, giving elastic stretch. Too far: butter beads or pools on the paste, the paste looks greasy and grainy (split).

</details>

**A4.** Why should the paste be at 60 °C or below before the first egg, and why not much colder than 40 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Above about 62–65 °C egg-white proteins begin to coagulate on contact, giving cooked specks and less protein available to stretch and set in the oven. Much colder, the butterfat firms and the paste stiffens, the egg incorporates slowly and the paste is less smooth and glossy.

</details>

**A5.** Describe the V test and the trench test for a correct paste. Why is the egg in the formula given as a range?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

V test: paste lifted on a spatula falls slowly and leaves a hanging V-shaped tail of 3–5 cm that holds without dripping. Trench test: a trench drawn through the paste closes slowly, over several seconds. The egg is a range because the paste's absorption depends on how much water was lost in drying, the flour, the egg's yolk/white balance and the paste temperature; the endpoint, not the formula, sets the amount.

</details>

**A6.** Why must the oven stay closed for about the first 20 minutes, and why can a golden-brown choux still collapse on cooling?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Until the egg proteins and starch have set, the expanded shape is held up by steam pressure inside a soft paste; opening the door cools the surface and drops the pressure support, and the walls sag. A golden-brown shell can still have thick, moist walls: colour comes from surface Maillard reactions, dryness from long drying. When the steam inside condenses on cooling, underdried walls cannot carry the shape.

</details>

**A7.** Explain how craquelin gives a round, even rise.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The cohesive, weighted disc holds the top surface together as the paste expands; it cracks into a fine, even crackle, spreading the stress over the whole top so steam does not break out at a single weak point. It also shades and insulates the top so the surface stays extensible a little longer.

</details>

**A8.** Name two effects of replacing the milk in R1-31 with water, and two effects of replacing bread flour with cake flour.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water only: paler (less lactose and milk protein for browning), crisper and more rigid shell; slower colour, so it can dry thoroughly. Cake flour: less egg absorbed, weaker paste and less stretch; lower rise and a greater tendency to collapse.

</details>

**A9.** Why does fondant heated to 45 °C set dull?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 45 °C the smallest sucrose crystals dissolve. As the glaze cools, the sugar recrystallizes onto fewer, larger crystals, which scatter light and form a brittle layer: dull, grey, sometimes cracked. Work fondant at 35–37 °C and thin it with syrup.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling for production.** You need 40 éclairs at 45 g of raw paste each and expect to lose 5 % of the paste in the bag and bowl. One batch of R1-31 (760 g of ingredients) yields about 740 g of paste. What batch factor do you need, and what are the ingredient weights (whole grams; eggs as a range)?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Paste to pipe: 40 × 45 = 1,800 g. With 5 % loss: 1,800 ÷ 0.95 = 1,894.7 g. Factor: 1,894.7 ÷ 740 = 2.56 → use **2.6**.

| Ingredient | R1-31 (g) | × 2.6 (g) |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 125 | 325 |
| Milk | 125 | 325 |
| Butter | 110 | 286 |
| Salt | 5 | 13 |
| Sugar | 5 | 13 |
| Bread flour | 140 | 364 |
| Eggs | 250 (220–270) | 650 (572–702) |
| **Total** | **760** | **1,976** |

Expected paste ≈ 740 × 2.6 = 1,924 g, which covers the 1,895 g needed. At this size use a stand mixer, and consider two batches of ×1.3 if your pan is small: a large panade is harder to dry evenly.

</details>

**B2. Drying and egg.** Your panade usually loses 15 g of water in drying and your paste then takes 250 g of egg. Today you dried it harder and it lost 35 g. Assuming the paste passes the V test at the same total water content, and whole egg is about 76 % water, estimate how much egg it will take. Why is the real number likely to differ a little?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Extra water lost: 35 − 15 = 20 g. Egg needed to replace it: 20 ÷ 0.76 ≈ 26 g. Estimated egg: 250 + 26 ≈ **276 g**, which is just above the R1-31 range (220–270 g); have an extra egg ready. It will differ because egg is not only water: its protein and yolk fat also change the paste's consistency, and a harder-dried panade has a slightly different gel. The estimate tells you the direction and size; the V test still decides.

</details>

**B3. Reading a formula.** A chef's choux formula is: water 200 g, milk 200 g, butter 160 g, salt 4 g, sugar 8 g, bread flour 220 g, eggs about 400 g. Convert it to baker's percentages (one decimal), compare it with R1-31, and predict two differences in the shells.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

| Ingredient | Grams | Baker's % | R1-31 % |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 200 | 90.9 | 89.3 |
| Milk | 200 | 90.9 | 89.3 |
| Butter | 160 | 72.7 | 78.6 |
| Salt | 4 | 1.8 | 3.6 |
| Sugar | 8 | 3.6 | 3.6 |
| Flour | 220 | 100 | 100 |
| Eggs | 400 | 181.8 | 178.6 (157–193) |
| **Total** | **1,192** | **541.8** | **≈ 543** |

Liquid and egg are nearly identical in proportion. Butter is lower (72.7 vs 78.6 %): slightly firmer, crisper, less rich shells. Salt is half: milder flavour, and marginally less gluten tightening (a small effect). Otherwise it will behave very like R1-31.

</details>

**B4. Craquelin and bake loss.** (a) You need craquelin for 60 profiteroles; each 3.5 cm disc at 1.5 mm weighs about 2 g, and you expect to need 50 % extra dough for cutting losses. Give the butter, brown sugar and flour at 1 : 1.2 : 1. (b) A tray of 24 profiteroles weighed 360 g raw and 234 g baked. A second tray weighed 360 g raw and 270 g baked. Calculate both bake losses and say which tray you would fill.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Discs: 60 × 2 = 120 g; with 50 % extra: 180 g. Ratio total 1 + 1.2 + 1 = 3.2 parts; one part = 180 ÷ 3.2 = 56.25 g. Butter **56 g**, brown sugar **67.5 g** (1.2 × 56.25), flour **56 g**. Check: 56.25 + 67.5 + 56.25 = 180 g.

(b) Tray 1: (360 − 234) ÷ 360 × 100 = **35 %**. Tray 2: (360 − 270) ÷ 360 × 100 = **25 %**. Fill tray 1: it is within the 35–45 % target. Tray 2 is underbaked (walls still wet) and will soften or collapse and go soggy quickly; if it has not collapsed, re-bake it at 160–170 °C for 8–10 min after piercing.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1. The flat Saturday.** A baker makes R1-31 in a non-stick pan. Butter was added in one 110 g block; the liquid boiled for about 3 minutes while it melted. The panade was stirred 30 s over heat ("there was no film, so I stopped"). All 250 g of egg went in in two additions. The paste was glossy and "soft like thick yoghurt". The profiteroles spread to 4.5 cm on the tray, baked into flat, smooth domes 2 cm high, looked golden at 25 min at 200 °C fan, and wrinkled on the rack. Diagnose it and list the corrections in order of importance.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Primary fault: **over-egged, runny paste** (failed V test), which spread before baking and could not rise upward. It was driven by three process errors: (1) 250 g of egg added blind in two big additions instead of stopping at the V test with a reserve; (2) the panade was barely dried, because the baker waited for a film that a non-stick pan never shows, so it could absorb less egg than usual; (3) this is partly offset by the 3-min boil, which lost water, but uncontrollably. Secondary fault: **underbaked** in a fan oven that was too hot: colour at 25 min before the walls dried, so the pieces collapsed on cooling.

Corrections: reserve 10–20 % of the egg, add in 4–6 additions and stop at the V test; in a non-stick pan dry for 1–3 min to 75–80 °C and a weight loss of 15–35 g; cube the butter so it melts as the liquid boils; bake at 170–180 °C fan for 25–30 min, to 35–45 % bake loss, pierce and cool on a rack. See [Paste too runny](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-too-runny) and [Choux collapsed after baking](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=choux-collapsed-after-baking).

</details>

**C2. The warped éclairs.** Éclairs piped with a 14 mm plain tip, egg-washed generously, baked in a conventional oven at 200 → 170 °C on the bottom shelf with the door opened at 15 min to rotate the tray. Result: several éclairs curved into a banana shape, with one deep split along one side; the bases are dark and some are glued to the parchment on one side; heights vary from 2.5 to 4 cm. Diagnose.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Several causes add up. **Plain tip without fork marks:** no controlled lines of weakness, so each éclair split at its weakest point, often along one side, and curved away from it. **Heavy egg wash** ran onto the parchment and glued one side of the base, restraining expansion there, which adds to the curving and lopsided rise. **Bottom shelf:** too much bottom heat, dark bases, and an uneven rise. **Door opened at 15 min:** the walls were not yet set; pieces sagged unevenly, adding to the variation in height (also check piping consistency: were the pieces weighed?). Corrections: 16–18 mm French star tip or fork marks; thin wash that does not reach the tray; middle shelf; door closed for 25 min for éclairs, rotate only after that if needed; pipe to 45 ± 2 g. See [Misshapen or curved shells](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=misshapen-or-curved-shells) and [Lopsided or uneven rise](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=lopsided-or-uneven-rise).

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** *"Choux made with bread flour are taller and have a larger cavity than choux made with all-purpose flour."*

Design an experiment to test it. State the independent, dependent and controlled variables, how you will handle the egg quantity, the batch plan, what you will measure and how, how many repeats, and what result would support or refute the hypothesis. Write your design before opening the answer.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:

- **Independent variable:** flour type, bread flour (record protein %, e.g. 12.5 %) vs all-purpose (e.g. 10.5 %).
- **The egg decision (the key design choice).** If you fix the egg weight, one paste will be at the V test and the other not, and you will be measuring egg consistency, not flour ([X1-37](experiments/X1-37-choux-eggs.md) showed ±20 % egg changes everything). The better design brings **each paste to the V test** and records the egg absorbed as a dependent variable: that is how a baker would actually use each flour. (A second, separate experiment could fix egg to test the flour's effect at equal composition.)
- **Batch:** two panades of the same formula (e.g. R1-31 at ×0.5, 70 g flour each), made one after the other in the same pan, dried to the same time and the same weight loss (±3 g), eggs from one beaten mix, paste temperature 50–60 °C at the first egg.
- **Controlled:** formula other than flour; drying loss; egg mix; paste temperature at the V test; piece weight (15 g balls, ±1 g) and tip; no egg wash; both pastes on **one tray**, alternating positions to cancel oven gradients; one bake at 200 → 170 °C; same total time; cooling on one rack.
- **Dependent variables:** egg absorbed (g and % of flour); height and diameter at pull and after 30 min (collapse); bake loss; cavity (% of cut section) and wall thickness; crack score; optionally volume by seed displacement (millet or rapeseed in a container of known volume).
- **Replication:** 6 balls per flour per bake, and at least **two independent bake days** (fresh panades), because one pair of panades cannot separate the flour effect from panade-to-panade variation.
- **Interpretation:** supported if bread-flour choux are consistently taller and have larger cavities on both days, by more than the spread between balls within one batch and between days; refuted if the difference is within that spread or reversed. Note also whether bread flour absorbed more egg, which is part of the mechanism.

A weak design uses the same egg weight for both, or bakes the two flours on different days or trays, which confounds flour with egg consistency or oven variation.

</details>

## E. Practical assessment

**Product:** a box of **6 coffee éclairs** and **12 profiteroles with craquelin**, filled and finished following [R1-32](recipes/R1-32-eclairs-profiteroles.md), with the paste from [R1-31](recipes/R1-31-pate-a-choux.md) and pastry cream and diplomate from [R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md), plus the batch record.

**Specification:** éclairs 13–14 cm baked, straight, even width, fine even cracks, deep golden-brown including in the cracks, 40–50 g filling each, thin glossy fondant with clean edges. Profiteroles about 5 cm across, round and uniform (height ÷ diameter ≥ 0.7), even craquelin crackle, 18–20 g diplomate each. Shells crisp at tasting (1–4 h after filling), no soggy base, no collapse.

**Submit:** panade weight loss; paste temperature at the first egg; egg used and a photo of the V tail; raw weights of 5 éclairs and 5 profiteroles; oven mode, profile and oven-thermometer readings; bake loss per tray; height and diameter of 3 pieces of each type at pull and after 30 min; a cut section of one of each with a ruler; filling weights; fondant temperature; time of filling and time of tasting; a short self-evaluation naming one fault and its most likely cause.
