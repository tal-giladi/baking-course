# M17 assessment · Finished pastries: components and assembly

Covers [17.1](stage-1/module-17/lesson-01.md), [17.2](stage-1/module-17/lesson-02.md), [17.3](stage-1/module-17/lesson-03.md), recipes [R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md), [R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md), [R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md) and experiment [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md). Write your answers before opening each one.

## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Name the four roles components play in a finished pastry, and give one example of each from a fruit tart.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Base (structure, crunch): the sucrée shell. Filling (moisture, creaminess, volume): crème pâtissière. Accent (contrast of flavour or texture): the fresh fruit (acid, juiciness). Finish (protection, shine, appearance): the apricot or neutral glaze.

</details>

**A2.** Water moves from pastry cream into a tart shell even though the shell is surrounded by the cream's water. State the rule that predicts the direction, and give approximate values for both components.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water moves from high to low water activity, toward equal aw rather than equal moisture. Pastry cream is about aw 0.97; a baked sucrée shell about 0.3. Water moves into the shell until they approach each other; the shell loses its snap after only a small gain.

</details>

**A3.** Compare an egg-wash seal and a cocoa butter film as barriers: what each is made of, how it works, and roughly how long each keeps a shell crisp under pastry cream.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Egg wash is a thin, baked film of coagulated egg protein: hydrophilic, somewhat porous, it slows water entry but lets it diffuse through; a few hours (about 4–6 h) of acceptable crispness. Cocoa butter (or chocolate) is a continuous fat film in which water is nearly insoluble: it blocks migration where coverage is complete and can keep a shell crisp for about 24 h; its weak point is thin or missing coverage, usually the base–wall corner.

</details>

**A4.** Sort these into "can be made 1–3 days ahead or frozen" and "same day or at assembly": sucrée dough, baked unfilled shell, crème pâtissière, crème diplomate with gelatin, cut fruit, génoise, Swiss meringue buttercream, soaking syrup, unstabilized Chantilly, apricot glaze.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Ahead or frozen: sucrée dough, baked unfilled shell, crème pâtissière (1–3 days, never frozen), génoise, Swiss meringue buttercream, soaking syrup, apricot glaze. Same day or at assembly: crème diplomate (within 24 h), cut fruit (last hour), unstabilized Chantilly (2–4 h).

</details>

**A5.** Why is ganache for a chocolate tart poured at 35–40 °C? Give one consequence of pouring at 55 °C and one of pouring at 28 °C.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 35–40 °C it is fluid enough to self-level and close enough to setting that it sets evenly, without melting the shell's seal or butter. At 55 °C: more bubbles, a skin and surface cracks, possible fat separation and a softened seal. At 28 °C: it is thick, does not level, shows spatula marks and ripples, and traps bubbles.

</details>

**A6.** What is the working temperature of Swiss meringue buttercream for filling and coating a cake, and what do you see at 17 °C and at 26 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

20–22 °C: smooth, silky, spreads and holds a sharp edge. At 17 °C: stiff, curdled-looking, tears the sponge and drags crumbs. At 26 °C: soft, greasy, slides, will not hold an edge or piping.

</details>

**A7.** What is the purpose of a crumb coat, and why must it be chilled before the final coat?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A thin layer of buttercream that glues loose crumbs and fills gaps. Chilled (buttercream firm, ≤ 10 °C), it cannot be dragged by the spatula, so the final coat goes on crumb-free.

</details>

**A8.** State the holding rules for a cream-filled pastry (temperature, maximum life, time out of refrigeration), and one reason the product's life counts from when the cream was made.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

≤ 5 °C; eat within 24 h (48 h at most for pastry cream and diplomate products); limit time out of refrigeration to about 2 h in total. The cream is the high-aw, protein-rich component in which bacteria grow; any time it spent in storage before assembly has already used part of its safe life.

</details>

**A9.** Why is a génoise for a layer cake deliberately made "dry", and how much syrup do you give a 20 cm, 3-layer cake as a starting point?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It is a lean egg-foam sponge with an open, springy crumb designed to take up syrup and carry the layers above without collapsing; moisture comes from the syrup and fillings. About 25 % of the sponge's weight: roughly 100–105 g, about 30/35/40 g for bottom, middle and top layers; about a third less with wet fillings.

</details>

**A10.** Give four principles for composing a simple plated dessert.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Any four of: one focal point (main element); height (stand or lean the crisp element); keep the crisp element out of sauces (moisture migration) and add it last; colour contrast and a clean plate rim; temperature contrast (warm with cold) served immediately; controlled portion (100–150 g total, 30–40 g sauce); odd numbers of small items.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling a tart.** The fruit tart in [R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md) is designed for a 22 cm ring: about 250 g sucrée lined, 350 g pastry cream, 450 g fruit and 70 g glaze in a 1,100 g tart. You need a 26 cm tart. Calculate the scaling factor and each component, the finished weight, and how many portions of about 137 g it gives.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Scale by area: (26 ÷ 22)² = 1.397. Sucrée lined: 250 × 1.397 = **349 g** (make about 400 g dough for trim). Pastry cream: 350 × 1.397 = **489 g** in the tart (make [R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md) ×0.7, about 525 g, to allow bowl loss). Fruit: 450 × 1.397 = **629 g**. Glaze: 70 × 1.397 = **98 g**. Finished weight: 1,100 × 1.397 = **about 1,537 g**. Portions: 1,537 ÷ 137 = 11.2, so **11 portions** (or 12 slightly smaller portions of about 128 g, which are easier to cut).

</details>

**B2. Ganache for a smaller tart.** The ganache for a 22 cm chocolate tart ([R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md)) is 250 g dark chocolate, 250 g cream, 25 g glucose syrup, 50 g butter and 1 g salt (576 g). (a) Scale it for an 18 cm ring. (b) You only have 55 % chocolate. What change do you make to keep a sliceable set, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Factor (18 ÷ 22)² = 0.669. Chocolate 167 g, cream 167 g, glucose 17 g, butter 33 g, salt 0.7 g (about 385 g). (b) A 55 % chocolate has less cocoa butter and cocoa solids per gram, so at 1:1 the ganache sets softer and will not slice cleanly at room temperature. Reduce the cream to 80–90 % of the chocolate (for 167 g chocolate, about 135–150 g cream), or increase the chocolate. Expect it to be sweeter.

</details>

**B3. Soaking syrup.** Your génoise weighs 400 g after levelling. (a) Using 25 % of its weight, how much syrup do you need, and how do you split it 30:35:40 across the bottom, middle and top layers? (b) You make 30 % extra syrup at 40 °Brix. How much sugar and water? (c) You then add 10 g kirsch. What is the sugar concentration now?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) 400 × 0.25 = **100 g**. Split 30:35:40 (sum 105): bottom 100 × 30/105 = 28.6 ≈ **29 g**, middle 100 × 35/105 = 33.3 ≈ **33 g**, top 100 × 40/105 = 38.1 ≈ **38 g** (total 100 g). (b) 130 g syrup at 40 % sugar: **52 g sugar + 78 g water**. (c) 52 ÷ (130 + 10) = 52 ÷ 140 = **37.1 %** sugar by weight.

</details>

**B4. Backward timeline.** You must serve the chocolate tart ([R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md), ganache filling) and the layer cake ([R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md), Option A) at a lunch on Sunday at 13:00. You can work Friday evening (3 h), Saturday afternoon (4 h) and Sunday 08:00–11:00. Build the schedule.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good plan (other valid plans exist if the logic holds):

| When | Tasks | Why there |
|---|---|---|
| Friday evening | Sucrée dough (rest overnight); bake génoise (rest overnight, wrapped); make soaking syrup; make Swiss meringue buttercream (refrigerate) | All keep 1–3 days or improve with rest |
| Saturday afternoon | Roll, line, freeze and fully bake the tart shell; seal with chocolate; make ganache, pour at 35–40 °C, set at 16–20 °C; level, split, soak, fill and crumb coat the cake; final coat; chill | The ganache tart holds 2–3 days (crisp shell); the buttercream cake is best after equilibrating overnight |
| Sunday 08:00–11:00 | Re-beat any buttercream for décor; pipe border and rosettes, add fruit; optional glaze on the tart (chill 20–30 min first); décor; box both | Décor last: fruit and piping degrade fastest |
| Sunday 12:00–12:30 | Take the cake out 30–60 min before serving (in its box); tart at room temperature | Service temperatures: buttercream 16–20 °C, ganache 18–22 °C |

The key logic: everything with a long rest or set is early; fruit and décor are last; nothing needing more than 1 h to set is on Sunday.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A fruit tart was assembled on Friday at 17:00 for a Saturday dinner at 20:00. The shell was blind baked until the rim was golden and sealed with egg wash. It was filled with crème diplomate (with gelatin), topped with kiwi slices and halved strawberries that had been washed and arranged straight away, and brushed with apricot glaze that had been heated earlier and had cooled on the stove. At service: the base bends instead of snapping, the cream has become liquid under the kiwi, the glaze is streaky and has slid off the strawberries, and there is a pink puddle at the rim. Diagnose each symptom and give a corrected plan.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Soft base:** the shell was judged by the rim, so the base centre was probably underbaked; egg wash only slows migration; the tart was filled 27 h before service. Fix: full bake (golden centre of the base), egg wash plus a complete cocoa butter film, fill on Saturday afternoon.
- **Liquid cream under the kiwi:** raw kiwi contains a protease (actinidin) that digests gelatin; diplomate sets with gelatin. Fix: use pastry cream (starch gel) under kiwi, or cook the kiwi, or add it within 2–3 h of service.
- **Streaky glaze, slid off:** glaze below ~60 °C had begun to gel before application; strawberries were wet from washing and cut long before service, so juice and water stopped the glaze from adhering. Fix: dry fruit 15–20 min, cut within 1 h, glaze at 80–90 °C in a thin coat.
- **Pink puddle:** juice drawn from cut strawberries (osmosis from the glaze sugar) over 27 h, running down to the rim. Fix: shorter hold, drier fruit, thinner glaze.
- **Also:** 27 h is at the edge of the safe life of a diplomate product that must be eaten within 24 h. Corrected plan: dough Thursday; pastry cream and shell Friday; seal, fill, fruit and glaze Saturday 15:00–16:00; refrigerate ≤ 5 °C; serve 20:00.

</details>

**C2.** A layer cake filled with unstabilized Chantilly and sliced strawberries, 50 g syrup per layer, coated with Swiss meringue buttercream that was "a bit soft" (26 °C), was taken from the refrigerator to a garden party at 28 °C. After 40 minutes: the sides bulge in the middle, the top layer has slid toward one side, the coat is beaded with water, and the rosettes have lost their ridges. Explain each symptom.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Bulging and sliding:** the filling (unstabilized Chantilly and juicy fruit) was soft and uncontained (no dam or ring), the layers were over-soaked (50 g each is too much with a wet filling; about 20–27 g would be right), and the warm buttercream was too soft to act as mortar; warming at 28 °C softened everything further.
- **Water beads:** condensation. The cake at ~4 °C was far below the dew point of warm outdoor air, so water condensed on the surface. It should have stayed in its box while warming, in a cool place.
- **Rosettes lost ridges:** buttercream piped too warm and then exposed to 28 °C; butterfat melts progressively above about 24 °C.
- **Also:** a Chantilly-filled cake is a chilled product; 40 min at 28 °C is acceptable, but it should go back into refrigeration or be eaten promptly. Fixes: stabilized Chantilly or diplomate in a ring with acetate, reduced syrup, buttercream at 20–22 °C, dam, chilled transport in a box, serve in shade within minutes of opening.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

A colleague claims: "A thin layer of apricot jam under the pastry cream protects a tart shell just as well as cocoa butter, and it tastes better." Design an experiment to test this claim for a 12 h hold.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:

- **Hypothesis:** at 12 h at 4 °C under pastry cream, a jam layer keeps a fully baked sucrée shell as crisp as a cocoa butter film (no difference in crispness score or break force).
- **Independent variable:** barrier: (1) none, (2) apricot jam, sieved, 5 g per 8 cm tartlet spread to cover base and corner, (3) cocoa butter film, 3–4 g per tartlet. Include (1) as a reference so you can see how much each barrier helps.
- **Controlled:** one dough and one cream batch; same thickness, rings, bake (all baked together, full bake); jam and cocoa butter applied the same way to base and corner and weighed; same cream weight (40 g), cream temperature, fill time; same refrigerator shelf and box; same tester and scoring method.
- **Replicates:** at least 3 tartlets per treatment at 12 h (9 filled), plus 1 unfilled per treatment as a humidity control; test at 1 h and 6 h too if you want a curve, as in [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md).
- **Measurements:** crispness score (1–5) with a defined scale; press-test break force (g) and snap or crush; shell weight gain after scraping (g, %); wet-layer depth; blind taste preference for flavour (serve coded samples; the jam is likely to be detectable).
- **Analysis:** compare mean scores and forces at 12 h; if the jam mean is within the replicate spread of cocoa butter, the claim is supported for 12 h. Separate the two claims: protection (measured) and taste (preference test, [SC-14](stage-1/science/sc-14-measurement-sensory.md)).
- **Prediction to write down first:** jam (aw about 0.80–0.85) is an intermediate-aw, water-based layer: it slows migration but itself gives water to the shell, so it probably protects less than a continuous fat film over 12 h, but better than nothing.

</details>

## E. Practical assessment

**Product:** one 22 cm fruit tart ([R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md)) served **4 h after filling**, and one 20 cm layer cake ([R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md), Option A), from components you have made yourself. Submit your component sheets and backward timelines beforehand, your weight log during assembly, and photographs of a cut slice of each.

**Specification:**

- Fruit tart: fully baked, sealed shell; 350 g ± 20 g cream, level 5–7 mm below the rim; 450 g ± 25 g fruit in tight rings; thin, clear, even glaze; 8 portions within ±5 % of the mean weight; shell snaps at service.
- Layer cake: three even génoise layers (±2 mm), syrup weighed per layer; level top (±3 mm), vertical sides, sharp top edge, no crumbs; 12 portions within ±5 % of the mean weight; moist but not wet sponge.

**Rubric:**

| Criterion | Pass | Merit | Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planning | Component sheet and backward timeline submitted and followed | Timeline met within 15 min at every step; deviations explained | No plan, or product assembled out of order |
| Tart shell | Fully baked (golden centre of base), sealed, snaps at 4 h | Snaps cleanly with no soft corner; walls straight within 2 mm | Pale base or soft at service |
| Tart cream and fruit | Cream holds a cut face; fruit dry, tightly arranged | Clean vertical cut faces on all 8 slices; no cream visible between fruit | Cream slumps; juice pools on the plate |
| Glaze | Thin, even, shiny | Clear film, no streaks or drips on the rim | Cloudy, thick, slid off |
| Cake structure | Level within 3 mm; no sliding or bulging | Layers equal within 2 mm on cut slices | Leaning, bulging or sliding layers |
| Cake finish | Smooth, crumb-free coat; sharp edge | Vertical sides and crisp edge all round; neat, even border and rosettes | Crumbs, gaps or a rounded, uneven edge |
| Moisture | Sponge moist, not dripping | Syrup log shows target weights; even moisture top to bottom | Dry layers or soggy base |
| Portioning | 8 and 12 portions within ±5 % | Within ±3 % | Outside ±10 % |
| Food safety | Cream held ≤ 5 °C; time out logged ≤ 2 h; allergens labelled | Temperature log with product core temperatures | Cream products held warm or unlogged |
| Tasting and reflection | Tasting notes for each component and the whole | One justified change per product for next time, framed as a single-variable test | No evaluation |

**Pass:** all criteria at Pass or better. **Merit:** Pass overall plus at least six criteria at Merit.
