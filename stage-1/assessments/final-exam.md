**1.** *(T1 · [01.2](stage-1/module-01/lesson-02.md))* Two bakers both write "salt 2 %" for a lean dough of 100 / 70 / 2 / 0.8. One means baker's percentage; the other means 2 % of total dough weight. How much more salt does the second baker actually use, as a baker's percentage, and what will you notice in the dough and the loaf?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total formula = 172.8 %. Two percent of the total = 0.02 × 172.8 = **3.46 % of flour**, which is 1.73 times as much salt. Expect a noticeably slower fermentation (salt stresses yeast osmotically), so less volume at the same clock time; a tighter, stronger dough (salt shields charges on gluten proteins and tightens the network); and a loaf that tastes clearly over-salted. This is why the course always states "baker's %".

</details>

**2.** *(T1, T3 · [01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md))* You have been kneading a lean dough by hand, with a measured friction factor of 4 °C, and hitting DDT 25 °C. You switch to a stand mixer, 8 min at speed 2, and its friction factor turns out to be 13 °C. You keep the same water temperature. Predict the dough temperature and the effect on a planned 2 h bulk. What single change fixes it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough temperature ≈ (flour + room + water + friction) ÷ 3, so +9 °C of friction raises the dough by 9 ÷ 3 = **+3 °C, to about 28 °C**. At the "doubles per 8–10 °C" rule that is 2^(3/9) ≈ 1.26, or 2^(3/8) ≈ 1.30: bulk runs about 25–30 % faster, so **~1 h 30–35 min instead of 2 h**, with risk of over-fermentation if you follow the clock. Fix: **lower the water temperature by the full 9 °C.** In the DDT formula a change in friction factor moves the required water temperature by the same amount in the opposite direction.

</details>

**3.** *(T2 · [02.1](stage-1/module-02/lesson-01.md), [02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md))* Whole-wheat flour often has *more* protein than white bread flour, yet it gives a denser loaf and needs more water. Explain both facts, and predict what to change when you replace 40 % of the white flour with whole wheat.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**More water:** bran fibre (and the germ) absorbs a lot of water, and whole-wheat flour usually has more damaged starch. **Denser loaf:** part of the extra protein comes from the germ and aleurone and does not form gluten; sharp bran particles cut gas-cell walls; and germ components (including glutathione) weaken the network. So there is less effective gluten per gram of protein. **Changes:** raise hydration by about 3–5 points for a 40 % substitution, let the flour soak or autolyse (20–60 min) to soften the bran, expect a shorter, faster bulk (more enzymes and minerals), and accept a lower volume, or compensate with a stronger white flour.

</details>

**4.** *(T2 · [02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md), [10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md))* Predict what happens if you make a génoise with 13 % protein bread flour, and a baguette with 8 % protein cake flour, with no other changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Génoise with bread flour:** folding develops more gluten from the stronger protein. The crumb is tougher, chewier and drier. The batter is harder to fold without deflating, so the cake may also be lower and have a coarser crumb. **Baguette with cake flour:** too little gluten-forming protein to build a network that holds gas at 70 %+ hydration. The dough is slack and sticky, won't hold its shape, spreads during proof, springs little, and bakes flat with a tight or irregular crumb. It would need much lower hydration and still underperform. Each system's flour follows from its structure: gluten builds bread; eggs and starch build foam cakes.

</details>

**5.** *(T3 · [05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md))* Why does a 100 %-hydration poolish tend to make the final dough more extensible, while a 50–60 %-hydration biga tends to make it stronger? Which would you choose for long baguettes that must be shaped without tearing?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

In the wet poolish, enzymes (proteases as well as amylases) and the developing acidity work easily over 12–16 h. They loosen part of the gluten in that flour, and the liquid preferment adds that relaxed, well-hydrated flour to the final dough. The stiff biga has little free water, so enzymic activity is slower and its gluten stays more elastic; it also brings a firm mass into the final dough. **Choice for baguettes:** poolish, because extensibility lets you roll long pieces without tearing. The trade-off is a slacker dough that needs good shaping tension.

</details>

**6.** *(T3 · [05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md), [05.4](stage-1/module-05/lesson-04.md))* Your levain has already peaked and fallen by the time you mix: the dome has collapsed and it smells sharp. Predict the effects on the dough and the loaf, and name two ways to shift the levain's timing.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A levain past peak has **exhausted its food** (yeast activity is falling) and holds **more acid**. Effects: a slower bulk (weaker leavening), a slacker dough (acid plus protease activity weaken the gluten), a sharper sour taste, a flatter loaf with less oven spring, and often a paler crust. **To shift timing:** change the feed ratio (a higher ratio such as 1:5:5 instead of 1:3:3 peaks later) or the temperature (cooler slows it, warmer speeds it up). A stiffer levain also peaks more slowly. Always use it at or just before peak: domed, roughly doubled, pleasantly sour.

</details>

**7.** *(T4 · [04.1](stage-1/module-04/lesson-01.md), [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md))* The same lean dough is baked (a) in a preheated covered pot for 20 min, then uncovered, and (b) on a preheated stone with no steam at all. Predict the differences in volume, score opening, crust thickness and crust gloss, and explain them.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) The pot traps the loaf's own steam. Steam condenses on the cool dough surface, delivering heat quickly and keeping the skin moist and extensible near 100 °C for the first minutes. The loaf expands longer, so you get **more volume, a fully opened score**, and a **thin, glossy, crisp crust** (surface starch gelatinizes in the wet film and then browns once the lid comes off). (b) With no steam, the surface dries and sets early. Expansion stops sooner, so you get **less volume**, a score that opens poorly or tears at the side, and a **thicker, dull, matt crust** that browns unevenly.

</details>

**8.** *(T4 · [04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md), [06.2](stage-1/module-06/lesson-02.md))* Rank by how fast they firm (stale) at room temperature, wrapped: lean baguette, tangzhong milk bread, brioche, pound cake. Justify your ranking.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fastest to slowest: **baguette → (milk bread ≈ brioche) → pound cake.**

- **Baguette:** starch-rich, with no fat, sugar or emulsifier to slow amylopectin retrogradation, and a high crust-to-crumb ratio. It firms within a day.
- **Milk bread with tangzhong:** pre-gelatinized starch holds extra water, and the milk solids, sugar and butter slow firming.
- **Brioche:** a lot of fat and egg (yolk emulsifiers) interfere with starch re-crystallization.
- **Pound cake:** very high sugar and fat and relatively little starch. It stays tender for days.

The middle two are close; either order is acceptable if you justify it.

</details>

**9.** *(T5 · [07.1](stage-1/module-07/lesson-01.md), [07.2](stage-1/module-07/lesson-02.md))* You replace part of the sugar in a cake with honey at equal sugar solids. Predict the colour and keeping quality, explain why, and state two adjustments.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Browns faster and darker:** honey's glucose and especially fructose are reducing sugars, so they take part in Maillard reactions (sucrose must first be split before it can), and fructose caramelizes at a lower temperature. **Stays moist and soft longer:** fructose is very hygroscopic and lowers water activity, so the crumb holds water. **Adjustments:** lower the oven by about 10–15 °C or tent the top; take the honey's ~17 % water out of the other liquids; and if there is soda in the formula, account for honey's mild acidity.

</details>

**10.** *(T5 · [13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md))* You are boiling a syrup to 121 °C (250 °F) for an Italian meringue and see sugar crystals on the pan wall above the syrup. Why is this a threat, and what three things lower the risk of the syrup graining?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A crystal that falls into a supersaturated syrup seeds crystallization, and the whole syrup can turn grainy or seize, especially if it is agitated. Lower the risk by: (1) **washing the pan walls down** with a wet brush, or covering the pan briefly so condensation dissolves the crystals; (2) **not stirring** once the syrup boils (swirl if needed); (3) adding an **interfering agent**, either glucose syrup or a little acid, which inverts some sucrose. The mixed molecules get in the way of an orderly sucrose lattice. Clean utensils and fully dissolving the sugar before boiling also help.

</details>

**11.** *(T6 · [08.1](stage-1/module-08/lesson-01.md), [11.3](stage-1/module-11/lesson-03.md))* You replace the butter in a flaky pie dough with the same weight of vegetable shortening. Predict handling, flakiness, tenderness, flavour and mouthfeel, and explain them.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Shortening is ~100 % fat with a wider plastic range and a higher melting point than butter (~80 % fat, ~16 % water, melting over roughly 30–35 °C). **Handling:** easier; the dough stays workable longer in a warm kitchen. **Flakiness:** the fat pieces stay solid longer in the oven, so the layers set before the fat melts: good flakes. The puff may be slightly lower because there is no butter water to make steam. **Tenderness:** higher, because there is more fat per gram and no extra water to form gluten. **Flavour:** much less, and browning is paler without milk solids. **Mouthfeel:** can feel waxy or coating, because some of the fat does not melt at mouth temperature. This is why many bakers use a butter–shortening blend.

</details>

**12.** *(T6, T7 · [10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md))* In a creamed pound cake you add eggs straight from the refrigerator, and the batter curdles. What has happened physically, what will the cake be like, and how do you prevent it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The creamed butter–sugar mass is a foam of air in plastic fat, and the eggs have to be emulsified into it. Cold eggs chill the butter below its plastic range, so it stiffens and can no longer hold the egg's water in an emulsion. The water phase separates, and air cells coalesce and escape. **Result:** a higher batter SG, lower volume, a coarse or uneven crumb, sometimes a greasy or dense band. **Prevention:** eggs at 18–22 °C, butter at 18–20 °C, eggs added gradually and each addition beaten in until smooth. If it starts to curdle, add a spoonful of the flour, or warm the bowl briefly.

</details>

**13.** *(T7, T5 · [10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md), [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md))* A génoise foam is warmed to 40–45 °C before whipping. An Italian meringue is made by pouring 118–121 °C syrup onto whipped whites. What does each temperature accomplish, and what goes wrong if you exceed the génoise temperature?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Génoise at 40–45 °C:** dissolves the sugar, lowers viscosity and relaxes the egg proteins, so the foam whips faster to a greater and more stable volume. Above ~50 °C egg proteins begin to coagulate: lumps, a foam that won't build, and a heavier cake. **Italian meringue:** syrup at soft-to-firm ball (~85–88 % sugar) brings fully dissolved sugar and a lot of heat. It partly sets the white proteins around the air cells, which gives a dense, fine, very stable meringue that holds for hours and survives folding and piping. It is the most stable of the three meringues.

</details>

**14.** *(T7, T10 · [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md), [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md))* A 2 : 1 dark-chocolate ganache (70 % chocolate : cream) turns oily and grainy when you whisk it at 45 °C. Explain in emulsion terms, and rescue it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Ganache is an emulsion of fat (cocoa butter and milk fat) in a water phase (the cream's water with dissolved sugar), with cocoa solids dispersed through it. At 2 : 1 with a 70 % chocolate the fat and solids are high relative to the water, so the emulsion has little continuous phase. Heat (45 °C) and vigorous whisking, which beats in air and makes droplets collide, let the fat droplets coalesce: oil separates, and the solids look grainy. **Rescue:** cool to about 32–35 °C, add a small amount (~5–10 % of the batch) of warm milk or cream, and emulsify with an immersion blender held below the surface, starting in the centre, until glossy and elastic. Next time, blend rather than whisk, and consider less chocolate for a 70 %.

</details>

**15.** *(T8 · [12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md), [12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md))* Crème pâtissière and crème anglaise both contain milk, yolks and sugar. Why can one be boiled and set firm enough to slice, while the other must stop at 82–84 °C and only coats a spoon?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Pâtissière contains **starch** (~9 % cornstarch on milk). Starch granules swell and gelatinize near the boil and form a gel as they cool: that is the sliceable set. The swollen starch also gets in the way of egg-protein aggregation, so the cream can boil without curdling, and it must boil. Anglaise has **egg proteins only**. At that dilution they thicken the liquid to nappe at 82–84 °C but cannot form a firm gel; above ~85 °C they aggregate into curds (graininess).

</details>

**16.** *(T8 · [12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md))* To avoid scorching, you pull a pastry cream off the heat 15 s after the first bubbles. It is thick and glossy. Predict what it is like the next morning, and why.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It will probably have **thinned, even turned runny**. Egg yolks contain α-amylase. Unless the cream is held at a full boil for **1–2 min**, some enzyme survives and slowly digests the gelatinized starch overnight in the fridge. The starch may also not be fully gelatinized. Fix: whisk constantly and hold a real boil for 1–2 min (2 min with a flour blend) to finish gelatinization and inactivate the enzyme.

</details>

**17.** *(T9 · [14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md))* Predict the baked choux when the paste has (a) too little egg and (b) too much egg, and explain what each ingredient contributes to a hollow puff.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Mechanism:** the cooked panade is gelatinized starch in water and fat. Eggs thin it to a pipeable, extensible paste and add protein. In the oven the paste's water becomes steam that inflates it, the starch–egg film stretches, and the egg proteins and starch set the shell around a hollow. (a) **Too little egg:** a stiff paste that cannot stretch. It rises little, cracks deeply and irregularly, and stays small and dense with a thick wall. (b) **Too much egg:** a slack paste that spreads when piped. It rises then flattens, bakes wide and low, is prone to collapse, and has a wet, eggy interior. Target: the paste falls from the spatula in a slow V-shaped ribbon, and a trough drawn through it closes slowly.

</details>

**18.** *(T10 · [16.3](stage-1/module-16/lesson-03.md))* Your tempered dark chocolate is at working temperature (31–32 °C). To make it more fluid you warm it to 34 °C and use it. Predict the result and explain.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Form V cocoa-butter crystals, the ones that temper builds, melt at about 34 °C ([Beckett]). At 34 °C most or all of the seed crystals melt, so the chocolate **loses its temper**. On cooling it crystallizes in unstable forms and slowly transforms: dull, streaky or grey (bloom), soft, poor snap, sticks in moulds and melts on the fingers. Fix: re-temper (cool and seed again). To thin tempered chocolate, stay at or below 32 °C and adjust fluidity another way (for example, a little cocoa butter).

</details>

**19.** *(T11 · [00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md))* You cook 3 kg of pastry cream and put it, covered, in a deep pot in the refrigerator. What is the food-safety problem, what cooling standard applies, and how do you meet it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A deep mass of cream cools very slowly at the centre. It spends hours in the temperature range where bacteria multiply fastest, in a moist, protein-rich, low-acid food. The Food Code cooling standard ([FDA-FoodCode]) is **57 °C → 21 °C within 2 h, and 21 °C → 5 °C within a further 4 h (6 h total)**. Meet it by spreading the cream ≤ 2–3 cm deep on a lined tray with film pressed onto the surface, optionally over an ice bath first, putting it in the coldest part of the fridge with air around it, and logging the core temperature with a probe. If the log shows a failure, discard the cream.

</details>

**20.** *(T12 · [18.3](stage-1/module-18/lesson-03.md), [19.1](stage-1/module-19/lesson-01.md))* A learner writes: "I switched to a new flour, raised hydration from 70 to 75 % and extended bulk by 30 min. The loaf was much better, so the new flour is better." What can they legitimately conclude, and how should they find out which change mattered?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Only that **the combination, on that day, gave a better loaf once**. Three variables changed together (they are confounded), and a single bake cannot separate a real effect from ordinary bake-to-bake variation. To find out: change **one variable at a time** against a control baked on the same day (or run a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial of 8 small batches if you want the interactions). Replicate each treatment (at least two bakes), define the measurements in advance (specific volume, crumb photo with ruler, a blind taste test), and state what difference would count as real.

</details>

**F1. Changing hydration at constant dough weight.** You make two 900 g loaves (1,800 g dough) from flour 100, water 65, salt 2, instant yeast 0.8. Convert the formula to **72 % hydration keeping total dough weight at 1,800 g**. (a) Give the old and new formulas in grams. (b) What changed, in grams, for flour and water? (c) Why is "just add 7 % more water" the wrong answer here? (d) Give two process changes for the wetter dough.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Old total = 167.8 %. Flour = 1,800 ÷ 1.678 = 1,072.7 g. New total = 174.8 %. Flour = 1,800 ÷ 1.748 = 1,029.7 g.

| Ingredient | 65 % formula (g) | 72 % formula (g) |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 1,072.7 | 1,029.7 |
| Water | 0.65 × 1,072.7 = 697.3 | 0.72 × 1,029.7 = 741.4 |
| Salt | 21.5 | 20.6 |
| Instant yeast | 8.6 | 8.2 |
| **Total** | **1,800.1** | **1,799.9** |

(b) Flour **−43.0 g**; water **+44.1 g**. Salt and yeast fall slightly because they follow the flour.

(c) Adding 0.07 × 1,072.7 = 75.1 g water gives 1,875 g of dough, so the loaves come out 37.5 g heavier or you have 75 g of dough left over. Salt and yeast also stay at their old grams, which is correct as percentages but wrong for the stated constraint. Whenever total dough weight is fixed, recalculate the flour from the new total percentage.

(d) Any two of: hold back 5–8 % of the water and add it after initial development (bassinage); add a set or two of stretch-and-folds in bulk; use a slightly stronger flour; shape with more tension and proof in a banneton; expect the bulk to run a little faster; bake 3–5 min longer to reach ≥ 96 °C internal and a dry crust.

**F2. Scaling to a target number of pieces with two loss factors.** You must deliver **40 dinner rolls of 50 g baked weight**. Measured losses: **12 % bake loss** and **3 % mixing and dividing loss**. Formula: bread flour 100, water 64, butter 8, sugar 6, salt 2, instant yeast 1.2. (a) Divided weight per roll (round up to the whole gram). (b) Dough to mix. (c) Each ingredient. (d) Why do you divide by the loss factors rather than multiply by 1.12 and 1.03?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) 50 ÷ 0.88 = 56.8 g → **57 g** per roll (rounding down would put the mean baked weight under spec).

(b) Divided dough = 40 × 57 = 2,280 g. Dough to mix = 2,280 ÷ 0.97 = **2,350.5 g**.

(c) Total formula = 100 + 64 + 8 + 6 + 2 + 1.2 = 181.2 %. Flour = 2,350.5 ÷ 1.812 = 1,297.2 → **1,298 g** (round up).

| Ingredient | % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100 | 1,298 |
| Water | 64 | 830.7 |
| Butter | 8 | 103.8 |
| Sugar | 6 | 77.9 |
| Salt | 2 | 26.0 |
| Instant yeast | 1.2 | 15.6 |
| **Total** | **181.2** | **2,352.0** |

Check: 2,352.0 × 0.97 = 2,281 g ≥ 2,280 g; 57 × 0.88 = 50.2 g baked ✓.

(d) A loss is a fraction of the *larger* starting quantity. 56 g × 0.88 = 49.3 g, so multiplying 50 × 1.12 = 56 g falls short. Likewise 2,280 × 1.03 = 2,348 g is 2 g short of 2,350.5 g. Dividing by (1 − loss) is exact.

</details>

**F3. DDT with a preferment, and ice.** Baguette final dough with a poolish. DDT 25 °C. (a) Flour 21 °C, room 23 °C, poolish 18 °C, measured friction factor 14 °C. What water temperature? (b) A summer day: flour 28 °C, room 30 °C, poolish 26 °C, friction 14 °C. What water temperature? (c) The final dough takes 350 g of water and your tap runs at 18 °C. How much ice (at 0 °C) and tap water give 350 g at the temperature from (b)? Ignore the bowl. (d) What else would you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Four terms: water = 4 × 25 − (21 + 23 + 18 + 14) = 100 − 76 = **24 °C**.

(b) Water = 100 − (28 + 30 + 26 + 14) = 100 − 98 = **2 °C**.

(c) Heat gained by the ice (melting, then warming to 2 °C) = heat lost by the tap water (18 → 2 °C):
m_ice × (334 + 4.18 × 2) = m_water × 4.18 × (18 − 2)
m_ice × 342.36 = m_water × 66.88 → m_ice / m_water = 0.1953.
With m_ice + m_water = 350 g: m_ice = 350 × 0.1953 ÷ 1.1953 = **57.2 g ice**, m_water = **292.8 g tap water**.
Check: 57.2 × 342.36 = 19,583 J; 292.8 × 66.88 = 19,582 J ✓. Weigh the ice as part of the water, and make sure it melts completely early in the mix.

(d) A 2 °C water target leaves no margin. Also chill the flour (for example, flour at 12 °C gives water = 100 − (12 + 30 + 26 + 14) = 18 °C, which is easy), ripen the poolish somewhere cooler, reduce friction (a shorter or slower mix plus folds), ferment in a cooler place, and **measure the actual dough temperature** to correct the friction factor for next time.

</details>

**F4. True hydration of a rich brioche.** Formula: bread flour 1,000 g, whole egg 450 g, egg yolk 100 g, whole milk 100 g, butter 700 g, sugar 140 g, salt 22 g, osmotolerant instant yeast 15 g. (a) True hydration. (b) Sugar concentration in the water phase, and whether the yeast choice is right. (c) You replace 100 g of the whole egg with milk, keeping true hydration constant. How much milk? Predict two effects on the loaf.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Water: egg 450 × 0.75 = 337.5; yolk 100 × 0.50 = 50.0; milk 100 × 0.87 = 87.0; butter 700 × 0.16 = 112.0. Total **586.5 g** → true hydration **58.7 %** (586.5 ÷ 1,000). (Compare [R1-09](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md) at about 54 %: this dough is wetter as well as richer, and will be very soft.)

(b) 140 ÷ (140 + 586.5) = 140 ÷ 726.5 = **19.3 %** of the water phase. That is in the osmotolerant range (sugar at 14 % of flour, well above ~10 %), so osmotolerant yeast at 1.5 % is right. Expect a slow proof anyway, because of 70 % butter as well as the sugar.

(c) 100 g egg removes 75 g water. Milk to add = 75 ÷ 0.87 = **86.2 g**. The dough loses 13.8 g and about 25 g of egg solids (roughly 12–13 g protein and 10 g fat). Predictions: a paler, less yellow crumb, and a slightly softer, less springy structure with a little more spread, because there is less coagulating egg protein. Crust colour stays similar or browns slightly more from the lactose.

## Section 4. Experimental design

Write your full design before opening the answer. Each answer ends with a 10-point checklist for marking.

**E1. Fermentation.** Hypothesis: *"In the R1-06 sourdough, raising the pre-fermented flour from 15 % to 20 % while lowering the DDT from 26 °C to 23 °C gives the same bulk time, but a more sour loaf."* Design an experiment that tests both halves of this claim, and explains *why* it holds if it does.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**A good design:**

- **Treatments.** The hypothesis is about a *combination*, so the two key treatments are A (control: PFF 15 %, DDT 26 °C) and B (PFF 20 %, DDT 23 °C). To explain *why*, add C (20 %, 26 °C) and D (15 %, 23 °C): a 2 × 2 factorial, with four doughs.
- **Batch.** 250 g total flour per dough ([R1-06](recipes/R1-06-sourdough-loaf.md) proportions: 90 % bread flour, 10 % wholemeal, 74 % water, 2 % salt). Adjust final-dough flour and water so total hydration stays 74 % at both PFF levels. Bake each as a small bâtard on the same stone with the same steam, not in the large pot (R1-06 advises against half loaves in a 24 cm pot). Put a 100 g sample of each dough in a marked straight-sided jar for the rise measurement.
- **Controlled.** One levain build, used at peak for all four; same flour lots; same mixing and fold schedule; each dough fermented **at its own DDT** in a controlled box (a cooler with a water bath), because a dough at 23 °C in a 26 °C room drifts. Same end-of-bulk criterion (+60 % in the jar), same shaping, same retard (12 h at a measured 3–5 °C), same bake to ≥ 96 °C, same cooling (3 h).
- **Timing.** Mix in a staggered order with 15-min offsets; log every dough temperature.
- **Measurements.** (1) Time to +50 % and +60 % in the jar. (2) pH of the dough at the end of bulk and of the crumb, with a calibrated pH meter (strips are too coarse). (3) Sensory: a **triangle test** A vs B for difference, then a ranking of sourness across all four, coded samples, randomized order ([Lawless]). (4) Height/width ratio and a crumb photo, so that "more sour" does not come at an unnoticed cost.
- **Replication.** Repeat the whole set on a second day.
- **Decision rule (pre-registered).** "Same bulk time" = within ±15 min or ±10 %, whichever is larger, on both days. "More sour" = crumb pH lower by ≥ 0.1 on both days **and** the panel distinguishes A from B in the triangle test (with 6 tasters, at least 5 correct is significant at the 5 % level). The ranking should put B above A.
- **Interpretation.** Comparing A with C isolates the effect of PFF at a fixed temperature; A with D isolates temperature. If B's bulk time matches A's, it is because the extra inoculum (+33 %) roughly offsets the ~20–25 % slower rate at 3 °C cooler. Any sourness difference then comes from the extra pre-fermented acid and the temperature effect on bacterial metabolism.
- **Confounds.** Levain age differing between doughs (use one build and mix within 45 min); fermentation environment; fridge temperature; the taster knowing which is which.

**Checklist (1 point each):** combination treatments A and B; factorial or other way to separate the two factors; batch size stated and appropriate; hydration held constant across PFF levels; each dough fermented at its DDT; one levain for all; objective bulk end-point; pH measurement; blind sensory with a stated test; pre-registered decision rule with replication.

</details>

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**A good design:**

- **Independent variable:** seal type. U = unsealed; U+ = unsealed but returned to the oven for the same minutes as the egg-wash shells (controls for the extra drying); E = egg wash (yolk + a little cream) brushed on and returned to the oven 2–3 min at 170 °C; C = melted cocoa butter at ~35–40 °C brushed on the cooled shell, with its mass recorded (for example 2–3 g per 10 cm tartlet).

(2) **Break force:** a three-point bend on a 2 × 6 cm strip cut from the base, loading a cup on a scale with water until it breaks (the X1-30 method), recording the break mass. (3) **Blind sensory crispness** on a 1–5 anchored scale by at least 3 tasters, coded samples. (4) Note any flavour from the cocoa butter or egg.
- **Replication.** Repeat on a second day.
- **Decision rule (pre-registered).** "As crisp as egg wash" = C's mean break mass and moisture gain within ±20 % of E's at 24 h, and a mean crispness score within 0.5 points. "Both crisper than unsealed" = E and C above both U and U+ on break mass and sensory on both days.
- **Confounds.** The extra bake for egg wash (hence U+); the amount of coating (weigh it); cream temperature at filling; condensation when cold shells meet room air before testing (test all at the same minute after removal from the fridge).

**Checklist (1 point each):** four treatments including the extra-bake control; one dough batch and one cream batch; coating mass controlled; fill weight controlled; time points including 24 h; moisture-gain measurement; break-force measurement; blind sensory; replication; pre-registered equivalence rule.

</details>

## The production plan (submit before Day 1)

Write it, then "submit" it at least **48 h before Day 1**: email it to yourself, to a mentor, or commit a dated file. Once submitted, it cannot be changed; deviations are logged during the exam. **No plan, no pass.**

The plan must contain:

3. **A back-scheduled timeline** for Day 0 evening to presentation, in clock times, with one lane each for **oven** (temperature and occupancy), **refrigerator/freezer** (space and what is in it), **your hands**, and **fermentation**. Oven temperature changes take time; show them.

7. **Equipment and mise en place list,** including thermometers checked against ice water and boiling water in the week before.

## Self-evaluation instructions

Do the evaluation at the presentation time, in this order, before you taste anything. Grade from the evidence, not from memory of how the bake felt.

**1. Photographs.** Use the same spot, the same light (daylight or one fixed lamp, no flash), a neutral background, and a ruler in frame. Number each photo and refer to it in your ratings.

| Product | Required photos |
|---|---|
| Sourdough | Container at the end of bulk (mark visible); shaped loaf; scored loaf; baked loaf top and side; crumb cut lengthwise |
| Brioche / croissants | Pieces on the scale (a sample); shaped; end of proof; baked tray; crumb cross-section (croissant: cut crosswise at the centre) |
| Fruit tart | Lined shell; baked shell with the 4 wall-height measurements; cream in the cooling tray with the probe; finished tart from above; a lifted slice side-on; the slump test at 0 and 10 min |
| Cake | Batter in the SG cup; baked cake side-on with ruler; cut face |
| Bench | Mid-session bench on Day 2 |

**2. Measurements.** Record them in a table before rating anything.
- Weights: every piece, on a 0.1 g scale for pieces under 100 g; every finished product.
- Dimensions: height, width or diameter, and height/width ratio for the loaf; wall height at 4 points for the tart; the génoise top at centre and edge.
- Temperatures: all CCPs from the live log (DDT, bulk, butter and dough, proof, boil time, cooling curve, internal temperatures at pull).
- Tests: the tart slice-lift and snap at ≥ 2 h; the cream slump at 10 min; batter SG.

**3. Tasting.** Taste every product at its evaluation time, in a fixed order (bread, cake, brioche or croissant, tart), with water between. If someone else is available, have them taste coded samples blind against the Flavour descriptors, and record both ratings. When they differ by more than one level, take the lower.

**4. Rating.** Fill in one rubric grid per product, and the whole-exam grid. For every cell write the level **and** the evidence (photo number or measurement). When between two levels, take the lower one.

**5. Planned vs actual.** Tabulate each planned step against its actual clock time. Explain every deviation over 30 min: cause, and what you did about it.

**6. Diagnosis.** For your two weakest cells overall, write the most likely root cause, the evidence, one change for next time, and the predicted effect, in the format of Section 3.

## Grading and passing, Part 2

| Rule | |
|---|---|
| **Pass** | Every product is **Competent or better in all its critical dimensions** (specification, structure, process), the whole-exam **documentation** and **hygiene** dimensions are Competent or better, and **no cell anywhere is Not yet** |
| **Automatic fail** | Plan not submitted before Day 1, or the pastry cream cooling standard failed or not logged. Food safety is not traded against quality |
| **Partial resit** | If exactly **one** product fails, resit that product alone within 4 weeks, with a new one-product plan submitted 48 h ahead. The rest of Part 2 stands |
