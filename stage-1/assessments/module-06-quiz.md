# M06 assessment · Enriched doughs

Covers [06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)–[06.4](stage-1/module-06/lesson-04.md), [R1-07](recipes/R1-07-milk-bread.md), [R1-08](recipes/R1-08-challah.md), [R1-09](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md) and [X1-15](experiments/X1-15-sugar-in-dough.md)–[X1-17](experiments/X1-17-brioche-butter.md). Work the written sections without notes first, then check. Water contents for calculations: whole milk 87 %, whole egg 75 %, yolk 50 %, butter 16 %, honey 17 %.

**Time:** about 1.5 h written, plus the practical bake (two days for brioche).

## A. Knowledge check

1. Milk bread browns faster than the same loaf made with water, even though baker's yeast has had hours to consume sugar. Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Milk contains lactose (about 4.8 %), which baker's yeast cannot ferment because it lacks lactase. All the lactose is still in the dough at baking. Lactose is a reducing sugar and, with milk protein, drives Maillard browning at the crust.

</details>

2. Give two reasons why a small amount of sugar (about 2–5 %) can make a dough rise faster, but 20 % makes it rise much slower.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At low levels, sucrose is split by the yeast's invertase into glucose and fructose, which the yeast ferments immediately, so gas production starts faster. At high levels, dissolved sugar lowers the water activity of the dough water, so water leaves the yeast cells by osmosis and fermentation slows (osmotic stress). The inversion of sucrose also doubles the number of dissolved sugar molecules, which adds to the osmotic load.

</details>

3. When should you use osmotolerant instant yeast, and what else would you change for a 15 % sugar dough?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Above about 10 % sugar on flour, or more precisely when the sugar in the water phase is high (roughly 15 % or more). For a 15 % sugar dough: raise the dose to about 1.5–2.5 % instant (less if retarding), ferment warmer (24–27 °C), and consider a sponge or late sugar addition.

</details>

4. Why is butter added to brioche only after the gluten has been developed, and what endpoint tells you the base dough is ready?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat coats flour proteins and prevents them from linking into a gluten network. At 50 % butter, adding it early would prevent development in any reasonable mixing time. The base is ready when it cleans the sides of the bowl, slaps around the hook, and stretches into a thin membrane you can see light through (a good windowpane).

</details>

5. List the four jobs eggs do in an enriched yeast dough.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Structure (proteins coagulate from about 62–70 °C and reinforce the gluten network); emulsification and tenderness (yolk lipids and lecithin disperse fat and give a finer, softer crumb); colour and flavour (yolk carotenoids in the crumb, fast browning at the crust); and water (about 75 % of whole egg, counted in true hydration).

</details>

6. Give the flour : water ratio and endpoint temperature for a tangzhong, the typical share of total flour, and one sentence on why it works.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1 : 5 flour to water (or milk), cooked to 65–70 °C until it thickens and holds trails, typically 5–10 % of total flour. Heating starch in excess water gelatinizes it, so it binds much more water than raw starch, and the dough carries more water without becoming sticky and bakes into a moister, softer crumb that takes longer to firm.

</details>

7. What is the internal temperature endpoint for milk bread, challah and brioche, and why is it lower than for R1-01?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

88–93 °C, compared with ≥96 °C for a lean loaf. Egg proteins coagulate and fat shortens the crumb, so the structure sets at lower temperatures. The aim is a moist, soft crumb, and baking further dries it. The sugar-, milk- and egg-rich crust would also over-brown.

</details>

8. In the braid instruction "1 over 3", what exactly do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Take the strand that is now in position 1 (leftmost), lift it over the strands in positions 2 and 3, and lay it down so it becomes position 3. The strands it crossed shift one position left. Then re-number the positions from left to right before the next move.

</details>

9. Give the target temperatures for brioche: base dough before butter, butter, final dough, proof. What happens if the proof is 32 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Base dough ≤24 °C; butter 13–16 °C (plastic); final dough 24 °C, maximum 26 °C; proof 24–27 °C. At 32 °C a large share of the butterfat melts and seeps out, so grease pools under the moulds, the dough weakens, and the crumb bakes greasy and coarse.

</details>

10. The old instruction to scald milk: what problem does it solve, and does it still matter?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Unheated milk contains a heat-sensitive whey-protein component that weakens dough and lowers loaf volume (often described as a glutathione-like effect; the exact mechanism is still discussed). Scalding to about 82–85 °C removes it. Pasteurized milk has been heated only mildly, so the effect is smaller but may not be zero. UHT milk and high-heat milk powder are free of it. Many bakers still scald or use powder to remove the variable. With raw milk it clearly matters.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Sweet bun dough.** A formula lists: bread flour 1000 g, whole milk 550 g, whole egg 100 g, butter 120 g, sugar 150 g, salt 18 g. (a) Compute the true hydration. (b) Compute the sugar concentration in the water phase. (c) Recommend a yeast type and dose, with a reason. (d) Compare with R1-07 and predict which ferments faster at equal yeast and temperature.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Water = 550 × 0.87 + 100 × 0.75 + 120 × 0.16 = 478.5 + 75 + 19.2 = 572.7 g. True hydration = 572.7 ÷ 1000 = **57.3 %**.

(b) 150 ÷ (150 + 572.7) = 150 ÷ 722.7 = **20.8 %**.

(c) Osmotolerant instant yeast at about **1.5–2 %** (15–20 g). The water-phase sugar is higher than brioche's (~18 %), well into the range where standard instant yeast slows markedly.

(d) R1-07 has about 12 % sugar in its water phase and 10 % butter, so it ferments faster. The bun dough has 21 % and 12 % butter.

</details>

**B2. Adding a tangzhong.** A soft roll formula has bread flour 600 g, whole milk 420 g, egg 60 g, sugar 48 g, salt 11 g, instant yeast 7 g, butter 60 g. Convert it to include an **8 % tangzhong made with milk**, keeping total flour and total liquid identical. Give the tangzhong and main-dough amounts, and verify.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Tangzhong flour = 0.08 × 600 = **48 g**. Tangzhong milk = 5 × 48 = **240 g**.
Main dough: flour 600 − 48 = **552 g**; milk 420 − 240 = **180 g**; egg, sugar, salt, yeast and butter unchanged.
Check: flour 48 + 552 = 600 g; milk 240 + 180 = 420 g. True hydration is unchanged. Cook the tangzhong to 65–70 °C, weigh it after cooling, and top up any evaporated water (it should weigh 288 g).

</details>

**B3. Scaling brioche for an order.** You need **12 brioches à tête at 55 g** and **2 Nanterre loaves at 450 g** of dough each, from R1-09 (total formula 225.5 %). Allow **2 %** dough loss. Compute the flour weight and every ingredient to 0.1 g.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough needed = 12 × 55 + 2 × 450 = 660 + 900 = 1560 g. With 2 % loss: 1560 ÷ 0.98 = **1591.8 g**.
Flour = 1591.8 ÷ 2.255 = **705.9 g** → use 706 g.

| Ingredient | % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100 | 706.0 |
| Whole eggs | 55 | 388.3 |
| Milk | 5 | 35.3 |
| Sugar | 12 | 84.7 |
| Salt | 2 | 14.1 |
| Osmotolerant instant yeast | 1.5 | 10.6 |
| Butter | 50 | 353.0 |
| **Total** | **225.5** | **1592.0** |

At about 1.6 kg this is near the upper limit of a 5–6 L stand mixer; check your mixer's rated capacity, or mix in two batches.

</details>

**B4. Challah variation.** You want R1-08 (500 g flour) richer: replace part of the whole egg with yolks so that yolks are 16 % and whole egg 12 %, keeping true hydration at 52 %. How much water must you add or remove?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Original egg water: whole egg 100 × 0.75 + yolk 40 × 0.50 = 75 + 20 = 95 g.
New: whole egg 60 g × 0.75 = 45 g; yolk 80 g × 0.50 = 40 g; total 85 g.
The eggs now supply 10 g less water, so **add 10 g water** (160 → 170 g, 34 %). True hydration stays (170 + 85 + 4.25) ÷ 500 = 51.9 %.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1. The greasy brioche.** A learner made R1-09 in a 26 °C kitchen with eggs at room temperature. The base dough mixed for 8 min and read 27 °C; the butter, softened on the counter, was added in two additions. The dough looked shiny and slack; the final temperature was 29 °C. After an overnight retard, the brioches proofed in the oven's "proof" setting (35 °C) for 1.5 h. There was fat in the bottom of the moulds. The baked brioches were low, with a close, greasy crumb and a damp band at the base; the tops were dark brown after 12 min at 200 °C, and the internal temperature was 86 °C. List the faults in the order they happened, and give the correction for each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Warm ingredients and kitchen**, so the base was 27 °C before butter. Correction: eggs and milk at 4–5 °C; chill the base to ≤24 °C before the butter.
2. **Base likely under-developed** (8 min, and no windowpane recorded). Correction: 10–15 min on speed 2 to a good windowpane before any butter.
3. **Butter too soft and added too fast** (two additions). Correction: butter beaten plastic at 13–16 °C, added in 4–5 additions.
4. **Final dough at 29 °C**, so the dough broke (butterfat melted out). Correction: stop, chill 15–20 min, remix; keep final ≤24–26 °C.
5. **Proof at 35 °C**, so butter seeped out (fat in the moulds), weakening the dough. Correction: proof at 24–27 °C for 2–3 h.
6. **Oven too hot (200 °C) and pulled at 86 °C**: dark tops, under-baked damp base. Correction: 190 °C for à tête, tent if needed, bake to 88–93 °C.

The root cause is temperature control throughout. See [greasy, broken brioche dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=greasy-broken-brioche-dough) and [dense brioche](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=dense-brioche).

</details>

**C2. The cracked challah.** A six-strand challah was braided tightly "so it would be neat", washed once just before baking, proofed 30 min at 24 °C (it had grown about 35 %), and baked at 200 °C for 30 min. It has deep pale cracks along several joins, a dark top, streaky shine and dull stretched patches between strands. The internal temperature was 91 °C. Diagnose each symptom.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Pale cracks at the joins:** under-proofed (+35 % instead of ~+75 %) and braided too tight, so oven spring ripped the joins open and exposed unwashed, un-browned dough. Proof until the gaps are closing; braid snugly without stretching.
- **Dark top:** 200 °C is too hot for a 900 g egg-and-honey loaf. Bake at 180 °C, and tent when the top reaches deep golden.
- **Streaky shine and dull patches:** one wash only, applied after the surface had stretched during proof; possibly unstrained or too thick. Double wash (after braiding and before baking), strained, thin, with a soft brush.
- The internal temperature was fine, so the loaf is baked through. These are formula-independent process faults.

See [braids splitting or tearing](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=braids-splitting-or-tearing), [egg wash streaks or blotches](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=egg-wash-streaks-or-blotches) and [uneven colour on challah](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=uneven-colour-on-challah).

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis:** "In challah, replacing part of the whole egg with yolks, at the same true hydration, makes the crumb more tender and the crust darker."

Design an experiment to test it. Give the variants and formulas, the controlled variables, what you will measure and how, how you will avoid bias, and what result would reject the hypothesis.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:

- **Variants** (small batches, 250 g flour each, one 3-strand loaf of about 450 g per variant):
  - A (control): R1-08 at ×0.5: whole egg 50 g (20 %), yolk 20 g (8 %), water 80 g.
  - B (yolk-rich): whole egg 30 g (12 %), yolk 40 g (16 %), water 85 g (the extra 5 g replaces the lost egg water, as in B4).
  - Everything else identical: flour, oil 25 g, sugar 12.5 g, honey 12.5 g, salt 4.5 g, yeast 3.5 g.
- **Controlled:** true hydration (52 %), flour and egg batch, mixing method and time, dough temperature 25 °C ± 1, bulk to the same volume, strand weight and length, braid, proof to the same volume, the same egg wash from one bowl, baked together at 185 °C on the same tray (positions swapped in a repeat), pulled at the same internal temperature (90 °C).
- **Measurements:** crumb compression of a 2 cm centre slice under a fixed load (as in X1-16) at day 0 and day 1; crumb colour and crust colour photographed together with a white card, ranked; blind tasting of tenderness and richness by at least three people with coded samples; loaf mass and height.
- **Bias control:** code the loaves; run the experiment twice, with the order of mixing reversed the second time.
- **Rejection:** if B does not compress measurably more than A (beyond your measurement noise, about 5 points with a ruler) and tasters cannot reliably pick B as more tender, the tenderness part of the hypothesis is rejected. Crust colour is evaluated separately; a difference only in crumb colour (yellower) does not support the crust claim.

</details>

## E. Practical assessment

**Product:** [R1-09 Brioche](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md), one full batch: one Nanterre loaf and eight brioches à tête. Submit your formula sheet with process records: ingredient, base, butter and final dough temperatures; mixing times; bulk and retard times; proof temperature and time; oven temperature; internal temperatures. Also submit photographs of the windowpane before and after the butter, the proofed pieces, the baked products and a cut Nanterre crumb against a ruler.

**Specification:** Nanterre about 420 g baked, lobes 3–5 cm above the rim; à tête about 50 g, head centred; crust uniform deep golden-brown; crumb fine, even and feathery, not greasy; internal 88–93 °C.

| Criterion | Pass | Distinction | Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base development | Windowpane photographed before butter | Thin, even windowpane with the time recorded | No windowpane before butter |
| Temperature control | Final dough ≤26 °C; proof ≤27 °C, both recorded | Final dough 23–25 °C, all four temperatures recorded and explained | Final dough >27 °C, or not recorded |
| Dough condition | Smooth, glossy, not greasy | Very thin windowpane after butter; no chilling needed | Broken or greasy dough baked without correction |
| Shaping | Heads attached and upright; loaf lobes even | All 8 heads centred and upright; lobes symmetric | Heads fallen or missing; lobes merged |
| Proof | ~2× volume, no grease in moulds | Proof time predicted within 20 min | Grease in moulds, or under-proofed (tears) |
| Colour | Even golden-brown, no burnt patches | Uniform deep golden across all pieces, no wash marks | Burnt, or pale and blotchy |
| Doneness | Internal 88–93 °C, recorded | Recorded for both products, with bake times | Below 88 °C or not measured |
| Crumb | Fine, even, tender; not greasy | Feathery, pulls into strands, uniform throughout | Dense, cakey, greasy or damp band |
| Records | Complete formula sheet and photographs | Plus a one-paragraph evaluation with one change for next time, justified | Missing temperatures or photographs |

**To pass:** meet "Pass" on every criterion. Any "Fail" means rebake, after reviewing the matching [troubleshooting entry](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md). **Alternative product** (if you have no stand mixer): [R1-08 Challah](recipes/R1-08-challah.md) as a six-strand loaf, assessed on development, braid definition (no split joins), double wash evenness, colour, internal temperature and crumb, with the same record requirements.
