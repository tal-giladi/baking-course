# M05 assessment · Preferments and sourdough

Covers [05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)–[05.4](stage-1/module-05/lesson-04.md), [R1-04](recipes/R1-04-poolish-bread.md)–[R1-06](recipes/R1-06-sourdough-loaf.md), [X1-13](experiments/X1-13-preferments.md) and [X1-14](experiments/X1-14-levain.md). Work on paper or in your lab notebook first, then open the answers. Allow about 90 min for sections A–D. Section E is a two-day bake.

## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Define prefermented flour (PFF). A formula has 1200 g total flour, of which 300 g is in a poolish. What is the PFF?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

PFF is the percentage of the total flour in the formula that is fermented in the preferment. 300 / 1200 = 25 %.

</details>

**A2.** Give the typical hydration and one distinguishing feature of each: poolish, biga, sponge, pâte fermentée.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Poolish: 100 %, a liquid batter with a tiny amount of yeast (~0.1 %) for 12–16 h at ~20–22 °C. Biga: 50–60 %, stiff, fermented cooler; gives strength and a more complex, slightly tangy flavour. Sponge: 55–100 %, short and warm, usually carries all the yeast; used for pan and enriched breads. Pâte fermentée: same hydration as the dough (~65–70 %), contains salt; a piece of old dough.

</details>

**A3.** Explain how alpha- and beta-amylase cooperate, and why an over-ripe preferment can still give a pale crust.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Alpha-amylase cuts starch chains internally, creating dextrins and many new chain ends. Beta-amylase cuts maltose off those ends. Together they supply fermentable sugar. In an over-ripe preferment, yeast and bacteria have consumed sugar faster than the amylases produced it, so little reducing sugar is left for Maillard browning in the crust.

</details>

**A4.** Why does a 40 % poolish dough stretch to baguette length more easily than a straight dough of the same hydration?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The poolish flour spent 12–16 h fully hydrated without mechanical development, while proteases relaxed its gluten. That relaxed gluten makes the whole dough more extensible. The dough also needs less mixing, so less elasticity is built.

</details>

**A5.** Name the two microbial groups in a sourdough, what each mainly produces, and one reason they coexist.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lactic acid bacteria (lactic acid, plus acetic acid, CO₂ and ethanol in heterofermentative species) and wild yeasts (CO₂, ethanol, aroma compounds). They coexist partly because they use different sugars: for example, *F. sanfranciscensis* uses maltose and releases glucose, which *K. humilis* (unable to use maltose) consumes; and the yeasts tolerate the acid the bacteria make.

</details>

**A6.** What is the "false rise" in a new starter, and what should you do about it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A vigorous rise on day 2–3 caused by fast-growing non-sourdough bacteria (enterobacteria, *Leuconostoc* and similar) at near-neutral pH. Acid then suppresses them and activity drops. Keep feeding on schedule at 24–27 °C; the sourdough community establishes over the following days.

</details>

**A7.** List three practitioner levers that tend to make a sourdough loaf more sour, and one that makes it milder.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

More sour: stiff levain, cooler and longer bulk, long retard, more levain, levain used past peak, more wholemeal or rye, cold old starter used after one feed. Milder: liquid levain used at peak from a well-refreshed starter, warmer and shorter bulk, short retard, less levain.

</details>

**A8.** Two starters both read pH 4.0 at peak. Why might one still produce a noticeably more sour bread?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

pH measures free acidity, not the total amount of acid. A wholemeal culture is more buffered and holds more total acid (higher TTA) at the same pH, so it tastes more sour. The levain amount and the process after mixing also change final sourness.

</details>

**A9.** Why is a poke test on a loaf straight from a 4 °C retard misleading?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cold dough is firmer and springs back more slowly because of its temperature, not because it is under-proofed. Judge a retarded loaf by volume growth since shaping, lightness, and your notes from previous bakes.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Straight dough to biga.** Convert R1-01 (500 g flour, 70 % water, 2 % salt) into a biga formula with PFF 35 %, biga hydration 55 %, biga yeast 0.3 % of biga flour, and total instant yeast reduced to 0.5 % of total flour. Give the three-column formula in grams and check it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total formula: flour 500, water 350, salt 10, yeast 2.5 → 862.5 g.
Biga: flour 0.35 × 500 = 175; water 0.55 × 175 = 96.25; yeast 0.003 × 175 = 0.525 g → 271.775 g.
Final dough: flour 500 − 175 = 325; water 350 − 96.25 = 253.75; salt 10; yeast 2.5 − 0.525 = 1.975; biga 271.775.

| Ingredient | Total % | Total g | Biga % | Biga g | Final dough g |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100 | 500 | 100 | 175 | 325 |
| Water | 70 | 350 | 55 | 96.25 | 253.75 |
| Salt | 2 | 10 | — | — | 10 |
| Instant yeast | 0.5 | 2.5 | 0.3 | 0.525 | 1.975 |
| Biga | — | — | — | — | 271.775 |
| **Total** | **172.5** | **862.5** | **155.3** | **271.775** | **862.5** |

Check: 325 + 253.75 + 10 + 1.975 + 271.775 = 862.5 g. In practice: biga 175 g flour + 96 g water (made up with 52.5 g of a 1 % yeast solution + 43.75 g plain water); final yeast 2.0 g.

</details>

**B2. Designing a levain build.** You want a sourdough with 1000 g total flour, 75 % hydration, 2 % salt and 20 % PFF from a 100 % hydration levain, built at a 1:4:4 ratio the night before. How much seed starter, flour and water go into the levain build, and what goes into the final dough?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Levain: 20 % × 1000 = 200 g flour and 200 g water = 400 g in total. A 1:4:4 build has 9 parts, so seed = 400 / 9 = 44.4 g (containing 22.2 g flour and 22.2 g water); feed flour = 4 × 44.4 = 177.8 g; feed water = 177.8 g. Check: flour 22.2 + 177.8 = 200; water 22.2 + 177.8 = 200.
Final dough: flour 1000 − 200 = 800 g; water 750 − 200 = 550 g; salt 20 g; levain 400 g. Total 1770 g = 1000 + 750 + 20.

</details>

**B3. Scaling for production.** You need three R1-06 loaves of 900 g dough each and expect 2 % dough loss in mixing and dividing. Using the R1-06 total formula (bread flour 90 %, wholemeal 10 %, water 74 %, salt 2 %; PFF 15 %, levain at 100 %), give the total flour, the levain, and the final dough in grams (one decimal).

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough to make = 2700 / 0.98 = 2755.1 g. Total formula = 176 %, so total flour = 2755.1 / 1.76 = 1565.4 g.
Total: bread flour 1408.9, wholemeal 156.5, water 1158.4, salt 31.3.
Levain: flour 0.15 × 1565.4 = 234.8 g, water 234.8 g → 469.6 g (for example, 60 g seed + 204.8 g flour + 204.8 g water).
Final dough: bread flour 1408.9 − 234.8 = 1174.1; wholemeal 156.5; water 1158.4 − 234.8 = 923.6; salt 31.3; levain 469.6.
Check: 1174.1 + 156.5 + 923.6 + 31.3 + 469.6 = 2755.1 g. Divide at 900 g × 3.

</details>

**B4. Poolish timing.** Your poolish at 0.1 % yeast is reliably ripe in 14 h at 21 °C. Tonight the kitchen will be 25 °C and you want it ripe in 14 h. Estimate the yeast % to use, using the "rate doubles per ~8–10 °C" rule and assuming ripening time scales roughly inversely with yeast dose over this small range.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A 4 °C rise speeds fermentation by about 2^(4/9) ≈ 1.36 (1.32–1.41 for 8–10 °C). To keep the time the same, reduce the yeast by about that factor: 0.1 / 1.36 ≈ 0.07 %. In practice use 0.05–0.07 %, consistent with the 05.2 table (0.05 % at 24–25 °C). Because dose and time are not strictly proportional, confirm with your notes.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1. The sleepy starter.** Day 12 of a new starter. Wholemeal rye for days 1–3, then white bread flour only. Kept on a kitchen shelf that a thermometer shows at 18–19 °C. After a strong rise on day 2, the baker switched to 1:5:5 twice a day from day 3 "to keep it clean". It never rises more than about +30 % and smells mildly sour. A loaf made from it on day 11 was dense and low. What is wrong, and what should the baker do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The culture is still weak because of three stacked causes: it is too cold (18–19 °C, when establishment wants 24–27 °C), it was diluted heavily (1:5:5 every 12 h) during the quiet phase, before the sourdough population was established, and it lost the nutrient and microbial boost of wholemeal. The dense loaf followed from baking with an immature starter. Correction: move it to 24–27 °C; feed 1:1:1 every 24 h with 20–30 % wholemeal until it rises clearly, then 1:2:2 every 12 h; bake only when it passes the readiness test (≥ +100 %, peak in 4–8 h, three feeds in a row). See [starter not rising](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-not-rising).

</details>

**C2. The stubborn biga dough.** A baker converts a 70 % hydration straight dough (1000 g flour) to a 30 % PFF biga. They make the biga with 300 g flour and 150 g water, then calculate the final-dough water as "70 % of the 700 g final flour" = 490 g. The final dough is mixed 10 min in a stand mixer at speed 2. It is very stiff and elastic, tears when shaped into baguettes, and the loaves are small with a tight crumb and a mild tang. Diagnose it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Two faults. (1) A formula error: total water is 150 + 490 = 640 g on 1000 g flour, so the dough is 64 %, not 70 %. The final-dough water should be total water minus biga water: 700 − 150 = 550 g. (2) Overmixing: 10 min at speed 2 over-develops a dough that already has strength from a stiff biga, making it elastic rather than extensible. Together they make a tight dough that tears at shaping and cannot expand. Correction: compute the final dough by subtraction from the total formula ([05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)) and mix to moderate development (about 2 + 3 min). The mild tang is normal for a biga. See [weak, slack dough](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=weak-slack-dough) for the opposite fault and [dense, tight crumb](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=dense-tight-crumb).

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** *"Retarding a shaped R1-06 loaf for 16 h instead of 8 h makes the bread noticeably more sour without making it flatter."*

Design an experiment to test it. State the independent, dependent and controlled variables, the batch plan, what you will measure and how, how many repeats, and what result would support or refute the hypothesis. Write your design before opening the answer.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:

- **Independent variable:** retard time, 8 h vs 16 h at 3–5 °C.
- **Batch:** one double batch of R1-06 (1000 g flour), divided into two 880 g pieces after bulk. Same levain, bulk, pre-shape and shaping. This removes batch-to-batch variation from the comparison.
- **Scheduling:** shape both at the same time; put both in the fridge together; bake the 8 h loaf first and the 16 h loaf 8 h later, each from cold in the same pot, preheated the same way, with the same bake times. (Alternatively, stagger the mixing so both bake together, but then the dough differs.)
- **Controlled:** fridge temperature measured at the shelf (both loaves on the same shelf), same banneton type and cover, same oven and pot, same cooling time (2 h) before measuring.
- **Dependent variables:** (1) sourness: blind tasting by at least 4–6 tasters with coded samples in random order, ranking sourness, ideally with a triangle test to check whether the difference is detectable at all; (2) crumb pH, measured as a slurry of 10 g crumb in 90 g distilled water with a calibrated meter, the same method for both; (3) flatness: height and width after cooling, and the height/width ratio.
- **Repeats:** at least two independent bake days, because a single pair cannot separate the treatment effect from chance.
- **Interpretation:** the hypothesis is supported if the 16 h loaf is consistently identified as more sour (tasters and lower pH) on both days while the height/width ratio differs by less than your day-to-day variation. It is refuted if tasters cannot distinguish them, or if the 16 h loaf is consistently flatter.

A weaker design uses two separate batches on different days, one per condition. That confounds retard time with everything else that changed between days.

</details>

## E. Practical assessment

**Product:** one [R1-06 Sourdough country loaf](recipes/R1-06-sourdough-loaf.md), made from your own starter ([R1-05](recipes/R1-05-levain.md)), plus the starter log and the bake record.

**Specification:** round or oval, 760–790 g cooled, 9–11 cm tall (boule), deep reddish-brown crust with an ear, open and even crumb with no dense band and no tunnel, ≥96 °C internal, balanced acidity.

**Submit:** the three-column formula with check-sum; the last 3 days of starter log (ratio, temperature, rise %, time to peak); levain rise % at use; DDT calculation and actual dough temperature; bulk log (rise % hourly and end cues); fridge temperature and retard hours; bake log with internal temperature; photos of profile, crust and crumb with a ruler; a short self-evaluation that names one fault and its most likely cause.

| Criterion | Pass | Merit | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starter readiness | Log shows ≥ +100 % and peak within 4–8 h on the last 2–3 feeds | Three consecutive feeds within ±1 h, with pH or smell notes | Starter under +100 % or no log |
| Formula and records | Correct three-column formula; all temperatures and times recorded | Plus a predicted end-of-bulk time within 30 min of actual | Arithmetic error or missing key records |
| Fermentation control | DDT within 24–27 °C; bulk ended at +50–75 % with cues noted | Within ±1 °C of target; cues and rise curve clearly documented | Bulk ended by the clock at <+50 % or >+100 % |
| Volume and shape | Height ≥ 8 cm (boule) or equivalent; loaf holds its shape | ≥ 9 cm with a clear ear | Flat (< 7 cm) or burst sideways |
| Crumb | Open and reasonably even; no gummy layer | Glossy, irregular, even from crust to base | Dense band, tunnel, or gummy crumb |
| Crust | Golden-brown to reddish-brown, crisp when cool | Deep reddish-brown, blistered, dark ear | Pale or burnt |
| Bake | ≥ 96 °C internal, cooled ≥ 2 h | — | Not measured or under 96 °C |
| Diagnosis | Names one fault and a plausible cause from the record | Cause supported by specific numbers from the record, with a one-variable test proposed | No evaluation, or a cause contradicted by the record |

**Pass** requires "Pass" or better on every row. Repeat the bake if any row is "Not yet"; the records usually tell you which lever to change.
