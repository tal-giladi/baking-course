# M09 assessment · Eggs: coagulation, foams and emulsions

Covers [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md), [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md), [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md), recipes [R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md) and [R1-16](recipes/R1-16-sabayon.md), and experiments [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md) and [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md). Write your answers before opening each one.

## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Give the working weights of a whole egg (without shell), a white and a yolk, and the approximate water content of each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Whole egg ~50 g, ~75 % water; white ~30 g, ~88 % water; yolk ~18–20 g, ~50 % water. Weights vary with national size grades, so weigh.

</details>

**A2.** Name the part of the egg mainly responsible for each function: (a) emulsifying, (b) foam volume, (c) yellow colour, (d) tenderizing, (e) structure in a baked custard.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Yolk (lecithin and lipoproteins). (b) White. (c) Yolk (carotenoids). (d) Yolk (fat and lecithin). (e) Both white and yolk proteins, by coagulation; in a custard mostly the yolk, or whole egg.

</details>

**A3.** State the approximate setting ranges of egg white, yolk, beaten whole egg and a milk custard.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

White: starts ~62 °C (ovotransferrin), firm ~80 °C (ovalbumin). Yolk: thickens ~65 °C, sets ~70 °C. Whole egg: ~70–73 °C. Custard (diluted with milk and sugar): ~80–85 °C, curdling above ~85 °C.

</details>

**A4.** Name two ingredients that raise the setting temperature of egg and two that lower it, with the mechanism for one of each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Raise: milk/water (dilution: proteins further apart) and sugar (gets between proteins and holds water). Lower: acid (reduces the proteins' net charge so they repel less) and salt (shields charges). Starch does not change the setting temperature much but protects against curdling.

</details>

**A5.** What is syneresis, and give two bench examples.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The contraction of an over-coagulated protein gel that squeezes out the water it held. Examples: weeping scrambled eggs, a baked custard with liquid and holes, curdled crème anglaise, overwhipped egg whites releasing liquid.

</details>

**A6.** List the five stages of whipped egg white and the stage you would use for folding into a chiffon batter.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Foamy, soft peak, firm peak, stiff peak, overwhipped (dry, grainy). For folding: firm peak (tip curls over), not fully stiff, so the films can still stretch during folding and in the oven.

</details>

**A7.** Why is sugar added to egg whites after soft peak rather than at the start, and what does cream of tartar do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sugar increases viscosity and slows protein unfolding at the bubble surface, so added at the start it slows foaming and reduces volume; added after soft peak, the films already exist and sugar thickens the lamellae and slows drainage, giving a stable, glossy foam. Cream of tartar (~1 % of white weight) lowers pH, moderates protein–protein bonding in the films, and gives a finer, more stable foam that is harder to overwhip.

</details>

**A8.** Name the three ways a foam fails and one counter-measure for each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Drainage (liquid runs down between bubbles): sugar for viscosity, set promptly. Coalescence (films break, bubbles merge): avoid fat, don't overwhip, fold gently. Coarsening / disproportionation (gas diffuses from small to large bubbles): use promptly, fine uniform bubbles, set by heat.

</details>

**A9.** Classify as O/W or W/O: mayonnaise, butter, cream, hollandaise. What property of the droplets lets mayonnaise contain ~80 % oil?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Mayonnaise O/W, butter W/O, cream O/W, hollandaise O/W. The droplets are soft and of mixed sizes, so they pack beyond the ~64 % of random sphere packing and deform into polyhedra separated by thin films of water phase. That packing also gives mayonnaise its stiffness.

</details>

**A10.** According to the FDA, what temperature should dishes containing eggs reach, and what should you use when eggs will not be cooked?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

71 °C (160 °F). Use pasteurized eggs or egg products for raw or lightly cooked dishes, keep them at 4 °C or below and out of the refrigerator no more than 2 hours ([FDA-eggs]).

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling mayonnaise.** Using the R1-15 formula (yolk 20, mustard 6, acid 10, water 6, salt 2, oil 160 g; total 204 g), calculate each ingredient for **600 g** of mayonnaise, the number of yolks at 20 g each, and the oil percentage.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Factor = 600 ÷ 204 = 2.941.
Yolk 58.8 g (3 yolks), mustard 17.6 g, acid 29.4 g, water 17.6 g, salt 5.9 g, oil 470.6 g.
Check: 58.8 + 17.6 + 29.4 + 17.6 + 5.9 + 470.6 = 599.9 g.
Oil % = 470.6 ÷ 600 = 78.4 %, unchanged by scaling.

</details>

**B2. Checking the water phase.** A cook proposes 2 yolks (40 g), 10 g vinegar and 540 g oil for "600 g of mayonnaise". Estimate the water phase as a percentage of oil, compare it with the 15–20 % guideline, and correct the formula so it stays at 600 g.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water phase ≈ yolk water 40 × 0.5 = 20 g + vinegar ~9.5 g ≈ 30 g. 30 ÷ 540 = **5.5 %** of oil: far below 15–20 %. The mayonnaise will go tight and break long before 540 g of oil.
Correction (keeping 600 g): target ~17 % of oil. Reduce oil to 480 g: water phase needed ≈ 0.17 × 480 = 82 g. With the yolks' 20 g water, you need ~62 g more from acid, water and mustard: e.g. vinegar 25 g, water 30 g, mustard 12 g (its water ~9 g), salt 5 g, plus a third yolk if possible for robustness. Total: 40 + 25 + 30 + 12 + 5 + 480 = 592 g; add 8 g more oil (488 g) to reach 600 g. Oil ≈ 81 % of the batch is at the upper limit; many cooks would stop at ~460 g oil and add 20 g more water (oil ~77 %).

</details>

**B3. Meringue batch.** You have **8 whites** weighing 30 g each. You make a French meringue with sugar at **1.5 : 1** on white and cream of tartar at **1 %** of the white. (a) Calculate the sugar and cream of tartar. (b) At stiff, glossy peak the SG is 0.25. What volume of meringue do you get? (c) How many 8 g kisses can you pipe?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) White = 240 g. Sugar = 240 × 1.5 = **360 g**. Cream of tartar = 240 × 0.01 = **2.4 g**.
(b) Mass = 240 + 360 + 2.4 = 602.4 g. Volume = 602.4 ÷ 0.25 = **2,410 ml** (about 2.4 L): use a 5 L bowl.
(c) 602.4 ÷ 8 = **75** kisses (allow a few for losses in the bag).

</details>

**B4. Swapping eggs for yolks.** A cake formula uses **6 eggs** (take 50 g each). You change it to **4 eggs + 4 yolks** (yolks 20 g). Calculate the change in egg water and egg fat, and say how much water you would add to keep the batter's water constant.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Original: 300 g whole egg → water 300 × 0.75 = 225 g; fat 300 × 0.105 ≈ 31.5 g.
New: 200 g whole egg + 80 g yolk → water 150 + 40 = 190 g; fat 21 + 25.6 = 46.6 g.
Change: water **−35 g**, fat **+15 g**. Add 35 g water, or ~40 g milk (≈ 88 % water), to keep water constant. The cake will be richer, more tender and more yellow ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)).

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner makes a chiffon cake. They separate 6 eggs by cracking each over the mixing bowl, pouring the white straight in. The bowl is a plastic mixing bowl also used for cake batters. They add all 90 g of sugar to the whites at the start "so it dissolves", and whip on the highest speed of a stand mixer. After 12 minutes the whites are glossy but will only reach a soft peak; the cake bakes low and dense. Diagnose and correct in priority order.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Three causes, all acting against foaming:
1. **Fat.** Separating directly into the main bowl risks yolk traces in all the whites, and a plastic bowl used for batters holds fat on its surface. Both break protein films and cap volume. Correction: three-bowl separation; glass or stainless bowl, degreased with vinegar.
2. **Sugar at the start.** It slowed foaming and reduced volume. Correction: whip to soft peak, then add sugar gradually.
3. **Technique.** Highest speed from the start makes large, uneven bubbles; add ~1 % cream of tartar (about 2 g for ~180 g white) at the foamy stage, whip on medium, then high, to firm peak.
Also check the white temperature (~20 °C) and verify with SG (target ~0.2–0.3 at firm peak for a sugared foam).

</details>

**C2.** In a restaurant, a cook makes 1 kg of mayonnaise in a food processor: 3 yolks (60 g), 15 g lemon juice, 10 g salt and 900 g sunflower oil straight from the cold store at 8 °C. It thickens nicely, becomes very stiff at about 650 g of oil, then suddenly turns thin and greasy. Explain what happened, rescue it, and rewrite the process.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Two causes. (1) **Water phase far too small:** yolk water ~30 g + juice ~14 g ≈ 44 g for 900 g oil, about 5 % of the oil. As oil accumulated, the films between the packed droplets thinned; the very stiff stage at 650 g was the warning, and then films ruptured and droplets coalesced. (2) **Cold oil** (8 °C) is viscous, forms larger droplets and makes the emulsion more fragile.
Rescue: in a clean processor bowl, put 1 fresh pasteurized yolk and 20 g water at room temperature; with the motor running, add the broken mixture drop by drop, then in a thin stream once thick; add water in 10–20 g amounts whenever it goes tight; then add remaining oil.
Rewrite: bring oil to 18–22 °C; use 4–5 yolks for 900 g oil; water phase ~15–20 % of oil (≈ 135–180 g in total from yolk, acid, mustard and added water); add oil slowly at first, and add water or acid whenever the emulsion becomes tight.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Egg whites from eggs stored 14 days in the refrigerator whip to stiff peak faster than whites from eggs 2 days old, but the foam drains more liquid in 30 minutes."

Design an experiment: independent, dependent and controlled variables, how you obtain the two ages of egg, the measurements, the replication and one confounder.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** egg age at use: 2 days vs 14 days after laying (or after purchase with a known pack date), both stored at 4 °C.
- **Obtaining them:** buy one batch of eggs from one flock with a pack date; use half at day 2 and keep half for day 14 in the same refrigerator. Alternatively buy two cartons of the same brand and grade 12 days apart and use them on the same day, recording both pack dates (this removes day-to-day variation but introduces a second batch: note it).
- **Dependent variables:** time to soft and stiff peak; SG at stiff peak (calibrated 100 ml cup); drainage (g liquid from 20 g foam after 30 min); also white pH with strips if available, and a photo of the thick white spread on a plate (height of thick white).
- **Controlled:** white weight (60 g × 2 replicates per age), white temperature (20 ± 2 °C), bowl and beaters degreased, mixer and speed, no sugar or acid (or the same amounts), same person, same endpoint definition, whites checked for yolk.
- **Replication:** at least 2–3 replicates per age, alternating the order (old, fresh, old, fresh) so that any drift in room temperature or operator fatigue is not attributed to age.
- **Confounder:** white temperature and yolk contamination (older yolks break more easily when separating). Measure temperature and discard any white with yolk. Batch differences between flocks if two cartons are used.
- **Expected:** older, thinner, more alkaline whites typically foam somewhat faster with similar volume and drain somewhat more; the differences are smaller than those from sugar, acid or fat in [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md).

</details>
