## A. Knowledge check

1. Give the approximate share of the kernel's weight for endosperm, bran and germ, and one thing each contributes to (or does to) a dough.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Endosperm ~80–85 %: starch and gluten-forming proteins — the dough's structure. Bran ~13–15 %: fibre that absorbs water and cuts gluten; colour, flavour, minerals. Germ ~2–3 %: oil (flavour, rancidity) and glutathione (weakens gluten).

</details>

2. What does "T65" tell you, and what does it not tell you?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

An ash content of about 0.65 % (band ~0.62–0.75 %) on dry matter: a moderately high-extraction white flour, slightly more mineral-rich, flavourful and absorbent than T55. It does not tell you protein content or gluten strength.

</details>

3. Match each flour to a product: cake flour (~8 %), all-purpose (~10.5 %), bread flour (~12.5 %), high-gluten (~14 %). Products: bagel, génoise, lean hearth loaf, chocolate chip cookie.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Génoise — cake flour; cookie — all-purpose; lean hearth loaf — bread flour; bagel — high-gluten.

</details>

4. Which gluten protein family mainly gives elasticity and which extensibility? Describe the bench test for each property.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Glutenin → elasticity (stretch and release: how strongly it springs back). Gliadin → extensibility (pull slowly: how far before it tears).

</details>

5. List three factors that weaken gluten and name the mechanism of one.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Any three of: fat (coats/lubricates proteins), sugar (competes for water), bran (cuts strands), germ or dead-yeast glutathione (breaks disulfide bonds), proteases (cut chains), excess acid, overmixing, excess water, high dough temperature.

</details>

6. Why does whole-wheat flour need more water and a longer rest than white flour of the same protein?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Bran fibre and pentosans absorb several times their weight of water, and slowly. The dough keeps stiffening for 20–30 minutes; a rest or autolyse lets absorption finish before you judge and develop it.

</details>

7. Give three effects of salt in bread and the normal range.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1.8–2.2 % of flour. Tightens gluten (stronger, less sticky dough); slows fermentation (osmotic stress on yeast); flavour. Secondary: darker crust through more residual sugar.

</details>

8. What is an autolyse, how long, and what is left out?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A rest of flour and water only, mixed until no dry flour remains, for 20–60 minutes before adding salt and yeast. Salt is left out because it tightens gluten and slows hydration; yeast is left out so fermentation does not start.

</details>

9. Name two situations in which a well-made dough will not pass the windowpane test at the end of mixing.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Short or improved mix where strength is built during bulk by folds; whole-grain doughs (bran punctures the film); very wet doughs that tear when stretched by hand.

</details>

10. In rheological terms, why do you rest a dough between pre-shaping and final shaping?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Pre-shaping stores elastic stress. Rest lets the stress relax (viscous flow and rearrangement of weak bonds), so the dough becomes extensible again and can be shaped without tearing or shrinking back.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1.** A dough uses 500 g flour at 65 % hydration. Convert it to 72 %. How much water do you add, and how would you add it to a dough already mixed?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water at 65 % = 0.65 × 500 = 325 g. At 72 % = 360 g. Add 35 g. Add it by bassinage: 3–4 portions of ~9–12 g on low speed or by pinching in by hand, each absorbed before the next, after the dough has reached moderate development.

</details>

**B2.** Formula: bread flour 100 %, water 70 %, salt 2 %, instant yeast 1 %. You need six loaves of 350 g dough each, and you lose 2 % of dough in mixing and dividing. Calculate each ingredient to 0.1 g.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Required dough = 6 × 350 = 2100 g. Dough to mix = 2100 ÷ 0.98 = 2142.9 g. Total % = 100 + 70 + 2 + 1 = 173 %. Flour = 2142.9 ÷ 1.73 = 1238.7 g. Water = 0.70 × 1238.7 = 867.1 g. Salt = 0.02 × 1238.7 = 24.8 g. Yeast = 0.01 × 1238.7 = 12.4 g. Check: 1238.7 + 867.1 + 24.8 + 12.4 = 2143.0 g (rounding) ✓. In practice round flour to 1240 g and recompute: water 868 g, salt 24.8 g, yeast 12.4 g.

</details>

**B3.** You have a bread flour at 13.8 % protein and an all-purpose at 10.0 %. You want 1000 g of a blend at 12.5 %. How much of each?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Let x = fraction of bread flour: 13.8x + 10.0(1 − x) = 12.5 → 3.8x = 2.5 → x = 0.658. Bread flour 658 g, all-purpose 342 g. Check: 658 × 0.138 + 342 × 0.100 = 90.8 + 34.2 = 125.0 g protein = 12.5 % ✓. Remember that blending averages protein, not necessarily gluten quality; test the blend.

</details>

**B4.** Your standard loaf is 1000 g bread flour at 68 % hydration with 2 % salt. You want to replace 40 % of the flour with whole-wheat flour. Write the new flour, water and salt quantities and the process change.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Bread flour 600 g, whole wheat 400 g. Whole wheat absorbs roughly 5–10 points more than white; 40 % substitution → +2 to +4 points → 70–72 %. Start at 70 % (700 g water) and keep 20 g in reserve for bassinage; salt stays 2 % of total flour = 20 g. Process: autolyse 30–45 min (or at least a 20–30 min rest before judging hydration), expect faster fermentation and somewhat lower volume; check the windowpane on a less demanding standard.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner reports: "My usual 68 % dough was slack and sticky from the start, rose unusually fast, spread in proof, and baked pale and flat. It tasted flat too. Same flour, same yeast packet as last week." Diagnose, and say how to confirm.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Most likely **salt omitted**. All symptoms fit: no gluten tightening (slack, sticky), no osmotic brake (fast rise → overproof on schedule → spread), more sugar consumed (pale crust), and flat flavour. Confirm by tasting the crumb against a known loaf and checking the mise en place (unused salt still weighed out). Alternatives to rule out: a change in water weight (check the notebook), dough too warm (check recorded temperature), a new flour lot. Correction: use a checklist and weigh salt into a separate container; if caught during bulk, add the salt with a little water and folds ([02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md)). See [Weak, slack dough](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=weak-slack-dough).

</details>

**C2.** A learner in Israel buys an imported Italian "00" flour for a baguette formula at 72 % hydration. The dough is soupy, will not hold a fold, and the baguettes spread flat. The previous local bread flour worked well. Diagnose and correct.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

"00" describes refinement, not strength. This one is probably a low-protein (~9–10 %) flour meant for pasta or pastry: lower absorption and weaker gluten, so 72 % is far above what it can hold. Confirm by reading protein per 100 g on the label and, ideally, a side-by-side gluten wash ([X1-03](experiments/X1-03-gluten-wash.md)). Correction: choose a flour by protein (≥ 11.5 % for baguettes), or if using this flour, lower hydration to its test-dough value (likely ~60–64 %), add folds, or blend with a strong flour.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

Hypothesis: *"A 30-minute autolyse increases the specific volume of a 70 % hydration lean loaf by at least 5 %."* Design an experiment to test it. Do not open the answer until you have written: variables, batch, procedure outline, measurements, and how you will decide.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:

- **Independent variable:** autolyse (none vs 30 min flour + water, salt and yeast added after).
- **Controlled:** same flour bag, 70 % hydration, 2 % salt, 1 % instant yeast; the **same time from yeast addition to oven** for both (do not let the control dough rest 30 min with yeast — that would add fermentation, a second variable; the extra 30 min of flour–water contact *is* the treatment); same development work after salt/yeast (e.g. 3 min in-bowl pinch-and-fold), same folds, dough temperature 25 ± 1 °C, same shaping, pans, bake.
- **Batch:** 200 g flour per loaf; **three replicates of each** (six loaves), mixed in alternating order with 10-minute offsets so neither treatment is always first.
- **Measurements:** weight and volume by seed displacement → specific volume (mL/g); also windowpane after mixing, extensibility, crumb photo.
- **Decision rule:** compute the mean specific volume of each group and the spread between replicates. Accept the hypothesis if the autolyse mean is ≥ 5 % higher **and** the difference is larger than the replicate-to-replicate spread. Otherwise the result is "no detectable effect at this sample size".
- **Threats noted:** oven position (rotate loaves), dough temperature drift, measurement error of seed displacement (repeat each measurement twice).

</details>
