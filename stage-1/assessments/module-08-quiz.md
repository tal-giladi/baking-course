## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Give the approximate composition of butter, and name one consequence of its water content in a scone and one in a formula conversion.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 80–82 % fat, 15–17 % water, 1–2 % milk solids (plus ~1.5–2 % salt if salted). In a scone, the water turns to steam in the oven and puffs the voids left by melting butter pieces (flakiness and lift). In a conversion, replacing butter with oil at equal weight adds ~19 % more fat and removes ~17 % water; you must add water back to match composition.

</details>

**A2.** State the butter temperature targets for the rubbing-in method, lamination and creaming, and explain in terms of solid fat content why they differ.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Rubbing-in 4–7 °C (about half solid: breaks into pieces that survive mixing); lamination ~13–16 °C (cold-plastic: sheets without cracking or melting); creaming 18–20 °C (soft-plastic: enough crystal network to hold air, soft enough to be cut by sugar). Each job needs a different balance between crystal network and liquid oil.

</details>

**A3.** Why can oil make a very tender muffin but never a creamed cake or a flaky scone?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Oil is liquid: it coats flour proteins thoroughly (tenderizing), but it has no crystal network, so it cannot hold air cells when beaten (no creaming) and cannot exist as discrete solid pieces or sheets (no flakes or layers).

</details>

**A4.** Write the reaction of baking soda with an acid, and the reaction of soda heated without acid. What does the second one leave behind, and how do you detect it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

NaHCO₃ + H⁺ → Na⁺ + H₂O + CO₂. Without acid: 2 NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ (half the CO₂). It leaves sodium carbonate, a strong base: soapy, metallic or bitter taste, yellowish crumb, extra browning, coarse crumb, green-grey blueberries; batter pH ≥ ~7.5.

</details>

**A5.** What is in baking powder, and what distinguishes single-acting from double-acting? Name one fast and one slow leavening acid.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Soda (~25–30 %), one or more leavening acids, and starch as a separator and moisture buffer. Single-acting powder has only a fast acid, so almost all its gas forms on mixing. Double-acting has a fast acid plus a slow, heat-activated acid, so part of the gas forms in the bowl and most in the oven. Fast: monocalcium phosphate, cream of tartar, citric acid. Slow: sodium aluminium sulfate, sodium acid pyrophosphate (SAPP, used in most aluminium-free powders).

</details>

**A6.** A bakery holds muffin batter in the fridge overnight. Which leavening system makes this possible, and what does the rest do to the muffin's shape?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Double-acting (or slow-acid SAPP) baking powder: the heat-activated acid keeps most of the gas in reserve until baking. The rest hydrates the flour and firms the butter, so the batter is thicker and often gives a taller dome.

</details>

**A7.** Why must muffin batter stay lumpy, and what are the three visible consequences of overmixing?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The batter is very wet (~85–95 % true hydration), so every stroke after the flour is wetted develops gluten quickly. Lumps are unhydrated flour pockets that will hydrate on their own without mechanical development. Overmixing gives tunnels (long vertical holes), a peaked top, and a tough, chewy crumb (also a smoother, paler crust).

</details>

**A8.** Describe how to measure batter specific gravity, and what a higher value means.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Weigh a cup filled level with water (W); weigh the same cup filled level with batter (B); SG = B ÷ W. A higher SG means less air in the batter: undercreamed, butter out of its plastic range, curdled egg addition, or gas lost by overmixing. It predicts a denser product.

</details>

**A9.** Give four reasons scones are baked at 210–220 °C.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Butter pieces melt and their water flashes to steam fast, puffing the voids; the dough is still soft enough to expand when that happens; the powder's slow acid releases gas in the same window; the outside sets and browns before melted fat leaks out and the scone spreads. The bake is short, so the scone does not dry out.

</details>

**A10.** Where is baker's ammonia appropriate and why not elsewhere?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

In thin, dry, low-moisture products such as crackers and thin crisp cookies. It decomposes completely into NH₃, CO₂ and water, but the ammonia must escape during baking; in thick or moist products it stays trapped and gives an ammonia smell and taste.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling muffins.** You need **30 muffins** at **85 g** batter each from [R1-12](recipes/R1-12-muffins.md) (total formula 334.3 %: flour 100, sugar 45, baking powder 4, salt 1, zest 1, egg 33.3, milk 70, butter 30, blueberries 50). Allow **2 %** batter loss. Calculate each ingredient.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Batter needed = 30 × 85 = 2,550 g; with 2 % loss: 2,550 ÷ 0.98 = **2,602 g**.
Flour = 2,602 ÷ 3.343 = 778.3 → **778 g**.
Sugar 778 × 0.45 = **350 g**; baking powder × 0.04 = **31.1 g**; salt × 0.01 = **7.8 g**; zest **7.8 g**; egg × 0.333 = **259 g**; milk × 0.70 = **545 g**; butter × 0.30 = **233 g**; blueberries × 0.50 = **389 g**.
Check: 778 + 350 + 31.1 + 7.8 + 7.8 + 259 + 545 + 233 + 389 = 2,600.7 g ≈ 2,602 g.
Mix in two batches of ~390 g flour so you can still combine in 15–20 folds.

</details>

**B2. Balancing soda.** A biscuit formula: flour 500 g, cultured buttermilk 300 g (assume 0.85 % lactic acid), baking powder 15 g, baking soda 3 g. (a) How much soda neutralizes the buttermilk? (b) Is there excess soda, and what will you notice? (c) Rewrite the leavening in grams and baker's % so the soda is balanced and total gas is about the same.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Lactic acid = 300 × 0.0085 = 2.55 g; soda = 2.55 × 0.93 = **2.37 g** (≈ 2.4 g).
(b) Excess ≈ 3 − 2.4 = **0.6 g** soda (baking powder is internally balanced and does not absorb it). Expect a slightly yellow crumb, darker crust and a faint soapy or metallic aftertaste; not severe, but noticeable to a trained taster.
(c) Soda **2.4 g (0.48 %)**. The 0.6 g soda removed was worth ~0.6 × 4 = 2.4 g powder in gas (though as unneutralized soda it gave only half its CO₂). Raise powder to **~16.5–17 g (3.3–3.4 %)** to keep total lift about the same, or leave it at 15 g (3 %) and accept a very slightly lower rise.

</details>

**B3. Butter to oil.** You want an oil-based version of [R1-13](recipes/R1-13-banana-bread.md) (250 g flour, 100 g butter). (a) Calculate the oil and added water that keep fat and water constant. (b) Give their baker's percentages. (c) What must change in the method, and what will change in the loaf?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) 100 g butter ≈ 81 g fat + 17 g water (+2 g milk solids). Use **81 g oil + 17 g water** (or ~20 g milk).
(b) Oil 81 ÷ 250 = **32.4 %**; water 17 ÷ 250 = **6.8 %**.
(c) Oil cannot be creamed: switch to the muffin method (whisk oil, sugar, eggs, banana and yogurt; fold in dry ingredients). Expect a moister, more tender loaf that stays soft longer, with a coarser, less even crumb, possibly slightly lower volume, and less butter flavour.

</details>

**B4. Reading specific gravity.** Your SG cup holds **142 g** of water. Three creamed butter–sugar mixes weigh 133 g, 118 g and 161 g in the cup. Calculate SG and rank the batches by expected crumb lightness. Suggest a cause for the worst one.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

133 ÷ 142 = **0.94**; 118 ÷ 142 = **0.83**; 161 ÷ 142 = **1.13**.
Lightest to densest: 0.83 > 0.94 > 1.13. At 1.13 the mix holds almost no air (unaerated butter–sugar is ~1.2): likely butter too warm (greasy, slack), too cold (lumps), or creamed far too briefly. Probe the butter; 18–20 °C and 3–5 min on medium.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner makes blueberry muffins from a buttermilk recipe (buttermilk, 0.7 % soda, 2 % baking powder) but uses milk instead because the shop was out of buttermilk. To save time they mix the batter with a hand mixer for about 45 s. Results: tall, pointed tops with a crack; long vertical holes inside; crumb yellowish with green-grey streaks around the berries; a soapy aftertaste; chewy texture. Diagnose each symptom and give corrections.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Two independent faults.
**No acid for the soda:** milk has almost no acid, so the 0.7 % soda was not neutralized. Heat decomposed it to sodium carbonate: soapy aftertaste, yellowish crumb (flour pigments at high pH), and green-grey berries (anthocyanins turn blue-green in alkaline conditions). Correction: use buttermilk, or yogurt thinned with milk, or omit the soda and use ~4× its weight of extra baking powder (e.g. soda 0.7 % → powder +2.8 %).
**Overmixing:** 45 s with a mixer developed gluten: tunnels, a pointed cracked peak, and a chewy crumb. Correction: fold by hand 10–15 times to a lumpy batter ([X1-22](experiments/X1-22-muffin-overmixing.md)).

</details>

**C2.** On a 29 °C summer afternoon a learner makes [R1-14](recipes/R1-14-scones.md). They leave the butter out "to make rubbing easier", rub it in until the mixture is completely smooth and sandy, knead the dough "until it came together nicely", roll scraps three times, and bake at 190 °C in a dark tray. Scones: flat (rise ratio ~1.4), spread with rounded edges, greasy bases that are dark brown, pale tops, tough and bready inside. Diagnose and list corrections in priority order.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

**Fat too warm and fully rubbed in:** no solid pieces left to create steam pockets, and the soft fat smeared and leaked: flat, spread, greasy ([X1-23](experiments/X1-23-scone-fat.md)).
**Kneading and repeated rerolling:** gluten developed: tough, bready.
**Oven too cool:** fat melted slowly and soaked in rather than flashing to steam; tops stayed pale.
**Dark tray:** excess bottom heat: dark bases.
Corrections: (1) butter 4–7 °C or frozen and grated; chill flour and bowl; stop rubbing with pea-size pieces; dough ≤ 15 °C, chill if above. (2) Mix liquid 15–30 s, 2–3 folds, reroll once or cut squares. (3) Bake at 220 °C verified by thermometer. (4) Light tray or doubled trays, middle rack.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Resting a muffin batter made with double-acting baking powder for 12 hours at 4 °C before baking produces a taller dome than baking it immediately, without making the muffin denser."

Design an experiment: independent and dependent variables, controlled variables, how you make the two treatments comparable, the measurements, and one confounding factor you must guard against. Do not look at the answer until you have written your design.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** batter rest: 0 h vs 12 h at 4 °C (covered, in the tin or in a bowl).
- **Dependent variables:** dome height above the rim and total height (mean of 6); peak index; specific gravity of the batter at the moment of filling; internal crumb (cell size, tunnels); density of the baked muffin (weight ÷ volume by seed displacement, or simply weight and height); tenderness; bake time to 93–97 °C.
- **Controlled:** one batter batch divided in two by weight, so formula and mixing are identical; same fill weight (85 g); same tin type and liners; same oven profile and rack position; the same person scoops both.
- **Comparability:** bake both on the same day and in the same oven load. Mix the "rested" half the evening before and the "fresh" half from an identical second batch in the morning, *or* mix one batch, bake half at once and half 12 h later, measuring oven temperature both times. Account for the rested batter's lower temperature: either bake it cold (the realistic production case, and record the extra minutes) or add a third treatment of rested batter warmed to room temperature to separate "rest" from "cold".
- **Replication:** at least 6 muffins per treatment, and repeat on a second day.
- **Confounders:** batter temperature at the oven (cold vs room temperature) and oven position; different batches if you mix twice. Measure both batter temperature and oven temperature.
- **Expected:** rested batter is thicker, often SG slightly lower or similar, and gives a taller, rounder dome and equal or slightly finer crumb; the density difference is small. A single-acting or soda-only batter would do the opposite.

</details>
