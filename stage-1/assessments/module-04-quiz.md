## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Name the dominant heat-transfer mode to (a) the base of a loaf on a stone, (b) the top crust in a still (non-fan) home oven, (c) the centre of the loaf.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Conduction from the stone. (b) Mostly radiation from the roof, walls and upper element, with some natural convection. (c) Conduction through the dough (plus some steam movement through the crumb).

</details>

**A2.** Your oven's ready light comes on after 15 minutes. How long should a 15–20 mm baking stone preheat, and how do you verify it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

45–60 minutes at full temperature. Verify with an infrared thermometer on the stone surface: within about 10 °C of set point.

</details>

**A3.** A formula written for a still oven says 220 °C. What is a reasonable starting point in a fan oven, and what extra precaution applies to a lean hearth loaf?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 200–205 °C (15–20 °C lower), or the same temperature with 10–15 % less time, checked by colour and internal temperature. For lean bread, keep the fan off during the steam phase (or use a covered pot), because the fan dries the surface and removes the steam.

</details>

**A4.** Give two reasons why steam increases loaf volume and one reason it gives a glossy crust.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Volume: condensing steam delivers latent heat quickly and evenly to the cold surface, speeding early heating of the dough; and the wet surface stays extensible, so the crust does not set before expansion ends. Gloss: the hot, wet surface starch gelatinizes and later dries into a thin glassy film.

</details>

**A5.** List the four contributors to oven spring.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Thermal expansion of existing gas (Charles's law); dissolved CO₂ coming out of solution; vaporization of ethanol and especially water into the cells; a final burst of yeast CO₂ production until the yeast dies at about 50–60 °C.

</details>

**A6.** Why can internal temperature confirm that crumb has set but not that a loaf is fully baked?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The centre plateaus near 96–99 °C while water remains; further baking drives off water, thickens and colours the crust and dries the crumb without changing the reading. Bake loss and crust colour are needed as well.

</details>

**A7.** State the internal temperature targets for lean and for enriched bread, and the typical bake-loss range for lean hearth bread.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lean 96–99 °C; enriched 88–93 °C. Lean hearth bread loses about 10–20 % of dough weight (higher for small pieces and long bakes).

</details>

**A8.** At roughly what surface temperature does Maillard browning become rapid, and at what temperature does sucrose caramelize? Why does the crumb never brown?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Maillard becomes rapid above about 140 °C; sucrose caramelizes from about 160 °C (fructose from about 110 °C). The crumb stays wet and cannot exceed about 100 °C.

</details>

**A9.** Where does bread stale fastest: freezer, refrigerator or room temperature? Why is reheating a remedy, and why is it temporary?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Refrigerator. Heating above about 50–60 °C melts the retrograded amylopectin crystals so the crumb softens; on cooling the starch re-crystallizes, faster than the first time.

</details>

**A10.** Name the four separate processes that make stored bread worse, and the one condition that slows all of them.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Staling (starch retrogradation), crust softening (moisture migration), drying (water loss) and mould. Freezing, tightly wrapped, slows all four; bread must be reheated after thawing.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling for bake loss.** You must deliver 6 lean boules of **700 g baked weight** each. Your process loses **14 %**. The formula is flour 100 %, water 68 %, salt 2 %, instant yeast 0.8 %. Calculate the dough weight per loaf, the total dough, and each ingredient in grams (ignore mixing losses).

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough per loaf = 700 ÷ (1 − 0.14) = 700 ÷ 0.86 = 813.95 → **814 g**.
Total dough = 6 × 814 = **4,884 g**.
Total formula % = 100 + 68 + 2 + 0.8 = 170.8 %.
Flour = 4,884 ÷ 1.708 = 2,859.5 → **2,860 g**.
Water = 2,860 × 0.68 = **1,944.8 g** (1,945 g).
Salt = 2,860 × 0.02 = **57.2 g**.
Yeast = 2,860 × 0.008 = **22.9 g**.
Check: 2,860 + 1,945 + 57.2 + 22.9 = 4,885.1 g ≈ 4,884 g.

</details>

**B2. Pita for a service.** You need **24 pita** at **104 g** dough each, using R1-03 (flour 100, water 62, salt 2, yeast 1, oil 2). Allow **3 %** for dough lost in mixing and dividing. Calculate each ingredient.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough to divide = 24 × 104 = 2,496 g. With 3 % loss: 2,496 ÷ 0.97 = **2,573.2 g** to mix.
Total formula % = 167 %. Flour = 2,573.2 ÷ 1.67 = 1,540.8 → **1,541 g**.
Water = 1,541 × 0.62 = **955.4 g**. Salt = **30.8 g**. Yeast = **15.4 g**. Oil = **30.8 g**.
Check: 1,541 + 955.4 + 30.8 + 15.4 + 30.8 = 2,573.4 g.

</details>

**B3. Reading bake loss.** Three loaves of the same dough were baked in a new oven. Calculate bake loss for each and say what it suggests, given that your spec is 12–15 %.

| Loaf | Dough (g) | Cooled bread (g) | Internal at removal (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 850 | 775 | 95 |
| 2 | 850 | 737 | 98 |
| 3 | 850 | 690 | 99 |

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Loaf 1: (850 − 775) ÷ 850 = **8.8 %**: underbaked; internal also low. Expect a pale, soft crust and possibly gummy crumb.
Loaf 2: (850 − 737) ÷ 850 = **13.3 %**: in spec.
Loaf 3: (850 − 690) ÷ 850 = **18.8 %**: overbaked for the spec; thick crust, drier crumb, although its internal temperature (99 °C) looks fine. This is exactly why internal temperature alone is not enough.

</details>

**B4. Gas expansion and steam energy.** (a) A dough retarded at 4 °C is loaded into the oven. By what percentage does the gas in its cells expand from heating alone as it reaches 100 °C? (b) 20 g of steam condenses on its surface. How much energy does that deliver (latent heat 2,260 J/g), and how does it compare with 20 g of water at 100 °C cooling to 25 °C (specific heat 4.18 J/g·K)?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) (100 + 273) ÷ (4 + 273) = 373 ÷ 277 = 1.347 → about **35 %**.
(b) Condensation: 20 × 2,260 = **45,200 J** (45.2 kJ), plus the sensible heat as the condensate cools. Water cooling: 20 × 4.18 × 75 = **6,270 J**. Condensing steam delivers about seven times more from the phase change alone.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner bakes a lean boule on a stone in a fan oven at 230 °C with the fan on throughout. They sprayed the loaf with water before loading. Results: loaf small and wide, score barely opened, the side split open near the base, crust dull and matt, 4–5 mm thick on top, colour good. Internal 98 °C. Diagnose and give three corrections in priority order.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The crust set too early: effectively no steam (a spray is a tiny water mass, and the fan removed it and dried the surface from the start). The surface lost extensibility before oven spring ended, so the loaf burst at its weakest point (near the base) instead of the score. Long surface drying thickened the crust and left it dull because the surface starch never gelatinized.
Corrections: (1) turn the fan off for the first 15–20 min, or bake in a covered pot; (2) add real steam (preheated tray + 100–200 g boiling water) and vent after 15–20 min; (3) after the steam phase, the fan can be used at 15–20 °C lower for an even finish. Also check the score depth and angle, and check the stone was preheated 45–60 min.

</details>

**C2.** A household bakes a lean pan loaf on Sunday, slices it when it is about 50 °C "so it's fresh", and keeps it in a plastic bag in the refrigerator. Complaints: the first slices are gummy and dense; by Tuesday the bread is firm and crumbly and "tastes dry", even though the bag is fogged with moisture; by Friday there are green spots. Explain each problem separately and give a storage plan.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Gummy first slices: cut before the crumb set; the hot starch gel smeared under the knife. Cool 1–2 h to below ~35 °C.
Firm and crumbly on Tuesday: starch retrogradation, fastest at refrigerator temperatures. The moisture in the bag shows water was not lost: staling, not drying.
Mould on Friday: sealed humid storage plus a high-water-activity crumb; refrigeration slowed but did not stop it.
Plan: cool fully, keep what will be eaten in 1–2 days at room temperature in a bag (a pan loaf can go in plastic), slice and freeze the rest tightly wrapped, and toast slices straight from frozen. Discard mouldy bread.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "For a lean 750 g boule, baking on a 6 mm steel gives more oven spring than baking on a 20 mm cordierite stone at the same oven setting, but a darker base."

Design an experiment: state the independent and dependent variables, the controlled variables, how you keep proof equal, the measurements, and one confounding factor you must guard against.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** baking surface (steel vs stone), both preheated 60 min at the same setting, at the same rack position, measured by IR thermometer before loading.
- **Dependent variables:** height, width, H:W ratio, score opening; base colour and top colour on a 1–5 scale; base crust thickness; internal temperature at removal; bake loss.
- **Controlled:** one dough batch divided into equal pieces (weighed to 1 g), same shaping, same score, same steam method and amount, same bake schedule, same internal temperature target.
- **Equal proof:** shape both, retard at 3–5 °C for 12–16 h, and bake them one after the other straight from the refrigerator; or, if the oven fits both surfaces on one rack, bake them together.
- **Replication:** at least two rounds, swapping which surface is baked first (or which side of the oven it is on), so order and hot-spot effects are not mistaken for the surface effect.
- **Confounder to guard against:** differences in surface temperature at loading (the steel and stone may not be equally hot, and the second bake follows a door opening) and oven position (hot spots). Measure surface temperature and alternate order/position.
- **Expected:** the steel gives equal or slightly larger spring and a noticeably darker base; if the base burns, the fair comparison is at the setting where the stone gives your target colour.

</details>
