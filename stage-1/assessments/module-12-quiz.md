## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Egg yolk alone sets at around 70 °C. Name three reasons crème anglaise thickens at 80–84 °C instead.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dilution (the yolk proteins are spread through five times their weight of dairy, so they meet less often); sugar (it stabilizes the folded proteins and binds water, raising the temperature needed to unfold them); fat from the cream (it interrupts the network). Heating rate also matters: slow heating thickens slightly lower.

</details>

**A2.** Describe the nappe test and what you see at 78 °C, 83 °C and 87 °C.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dip a spoon, hold it horizontal, draw a finger across the back. At 78 °C the coating is thin and the line blurs and closes. At 83 °C the custard coats evenly and the line stays clean and sharp (nappe). At 87 °C the coating may look thick but grainy, and the custard smells of cooked egg: it is beginning to curdle.

</details>

**A3.** A water bath in a 150 °C oven: at roughly what temperature does the water sit, and why does that protect a baked custard?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 80–90 °C (it cannot exceed 100 °C, and evaporation keeps it lower). The mould walls are held near the custard's target temperature rather than at 150 °C, so the edges cannot overshoot into the curdling zone while the centre is still setting.

</details>

**A4.** Why does crème caramel unmould and crème brûlée not? Give two differences in formula and the effect of each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Crème caramel uses whole eggs (plus some yolks) instead of yolks only: egg-white proteins form a firmer, more elastic gel, and the total protein is higher (~3.2 % vs ~2.3 %). It uses milk instead of cream: less fat interrupts the protein network. Result: a gel that stands on a plate, versus a soft cream that slumps.

</details>

**A5.** Give the two reasons pastry cream must be brought to a full boil and held there for 1–2 minutes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

To complete gelatinization of the cornstarch (sugar raises the gelatinization temperature; full thickening and a firm cold set need 90–95 °C+), and to inactivate egg-yolk α-amylase, which otherwise survives and digests the starch during storage, thinning the cream overnight.

</details>

**A6.** Why can pastry cream boil without curdling when crème anglaise cannot pass 85 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The swollen starch granules and leached amylose hold the egg proteins apart and thicken the liquid, so the proteins coagulate into fine, dispersed particles instead of gathering into large water-expelling curds.

</details>

**A7.** Compare cornstarch, wheat flour and potato starch in a pastry cream: dose relative to cornstarch, texture and appearance.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cornstarch 1×: firm, short, clean-cutting gel, opaque but glossy, neutral taste. Flour about 2×: softer, pastier, duller and cloudier, needs a longer boil to lose raw taste. Potato starch about 0.8–1×: thickens earlier, glossy and clearer but elastic/stringy, softer when cold and less stable to prolonged boiling and stirring.

</details>

**A8.** Explain partial coalescence in two or three sentences, and why cream must be at or below about 5–7 °C to whip.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Whipping damages the membranes of fat globules at the bubble surfaces; fat crystals poking out of cold, partly solid globules pierce neighbouring globules so they stick together without merging fully. Chains of these clusters form a fat network around and between the bubbles that holds the foam. It needs the fat to be partly crystalline: warm cream has too much liquid fat, so globules merge completely and the foam is weak and greasy.

</details>

**A9.** What minimum fat content do you need for whipping, and which stage of whipping do you use for (a) folding into a mousse, (b) piping rosettes?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At least about 30 %; 35–40 % whips best. (a) Soft peak, because folding adds more work. (b) Medium–firm peak: the peak holds with only its tip bending and the surface is still smooth.

</details>

**A10.** State the cooling and storage rules for pastry cream.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Spread about 2 cm thick in a shallow tray, press film onto the surface, refrigerate immediately; below 21 °C within 2 h and at ≤ 5 °C within a few more hours (much faster is better). Store covered at ≤ 5 °C for 2–3 days; do not freeze. Whisk 20–40 s before use.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Pastry cream for éclairs.** You must fill **24 éclairs** with **45 g** pastry cream each. Allow **10 %** for losses in the bag and bowl, and **5 %** evaporation during cooking. Using R1-26 (milk 100, yolks 20, sugar 22, cornstarch 9, butter 7, vanilla 1, salt 0.1), calculate the weight of each ingredient. How many large yolks (18 g) is that?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Finished cream to fill: 24 × 45 = 1,080 g. With 10 % loss: 1,080 ÷ 0.90 = **1,200 g** of finished cream. With 5 % evaporation: 1,200 ÷ 0.95 = **1,263.2 g** of raw ingredients.
Total formula % = 100 + 20 + 22 + 9 + 7 + 1 + 0.1 = 159.1 %.
Milk = 1,263.2 ÷ 1.591 = 793.97 → **794 g**.
Yolks = 794 × 0.20 = **158.8 g** (about 9 large yolks).
Sugar = 794 × 0.22 = **174.7 g**.
Cornstarch = 794 × 0.09 = **71.5 g**.
Butter = 794 × 0.07 = **55.6 g**.
Vanilla = **7.9 g**. Salt = **0.8 g**.
Check: 794 + 158.8 + 174.7 + 71.5 + 55.6 + 7.9 + 0.8 = 1,263.3 g.

</details>

**B2. Crème anglaise for a banquet.** You need **20 portions of 60 g** of sauce, with **4 %** lost in straining and evaporation. Using R1-24 (milk 50, cream 50, yolks 20, sugar 20, vanilla 1, salt 0.1, all on dairy), calculate each ingredient and the number of 18 g yolks.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sauce needed: 20 × 60 = 1,200 g. Raw mix: 1,200 ÷ 0.96 = **1,250 g**.
Total % = 141.1 %. Dairy = 1,250 ÷ 1.411 = 885.9 → **886 g**: **443 g milk + 443 g cream**.
Yolks = 886 × 0.20 = **177.2 g** (about 10 yolks). Sugar = **177.2 g**. Vanilla = **8.9 g**. Salt = **0.9 g**.
Check: 886 + 177.2 + 177.2 + 8.9 + 0.9 = 1,250.2 g.

</details>

**B3. Whipped cream by volume.** A cake needs **1.5 L** of Chantilly at **100 % overrun**. The unwhipped mix (cream + 8 % icing sugar + 1 % vanilla, on the cream weight) weighs **1.02 g per ml**. How much cream, sugar and vanilla do you weigh?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 100 % overrun, the whipped volume is twice the liquid volume, so you need 1,500 ÷ 2 = 750 ml of liquid mix = 750 × 1.02 = **765 g** of mix.
The mix is 109 % of the cream weight, so cream = 765 ÷ 1.09 = 701.8 → **702 g**.
Icing sugar = 702 × 0.08 = **56.2 g**. Vanilla = 702 × 0.01 = **7.0 g**.
Check: 702 + 56.2 + 7.0 = 765.2 g.

</details>

**B4. Starch substitution.** A pastry cream uses 500 g milk and 45 g cornstarch. You run out of cornstarch and have only 20 g of it plus plenty of flour. How much flour do you add to keep the thickening roughly equal, and what changes in the process?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Missing cornstarch = 45 − 20 = 25 g. Flour at about half the thickening power: 25 × 2 = **50 g** flour (plus the 20 g cornstarch). Boil for at least 2 minutes (not 1) to remove the raw-flour taste, and expect a slightly softer, creamier, less translucent cream.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A pastry chef makes pastry cream at 16:00 for tarts the next morning. Notes: "Milk boiled, yolks + sugar + cornstarch whisked, tempered, back on the stove; it thickened beautifully at 86 °C so I took it off straight away to avoid curdling. Butter in, poured into a 10 cm deep plastic tub, lid on, into the fridge." Next morning: a skin on top, the cream is lumpy when whisked, and within a minute of whisking it has become a thick pourable sauce. The tub is still slightly warm in the centre. Identify every problem and give the corrections.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Runny:** not boiled, so starch gelatinization was incomplete and yolk α-amylase survived and digested the starch overnight (the amylase trap). Correction: full boil, 1–2 min, whisking. It cannot be rescued: remake.
2. **Skin and lumps:** no film in contact with the surface; the lid trapped condensation and the surface dried into a skin that was whisked in as lumps. Correction: film pressed onto the surface.
3. **Slow cooling (food safety):** a 10 cm deep tub with a lid cools very slowly; the centre may have spent hours between 20 and 50 °C. Correction: spread 2 cm thick in a shallow tray, refrigerate at once, check < 21 °C within 2 h. This batch should be discarded on safety grounds as well.

</details>

**C2.** Crème brûlée from a new oven: the custards are pale gold on top with a slight crust, the edges are full of small holes, a thin layer of liquid sits on the surface after chilling, and the texture is grainy near the edges but softer in the centre. Bake notes: oven set to 160 °C fan, no water bath "to save time", 40 min, centre 88 °C at the pull.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Overbaked with no heat limit. At 160 °C fan without a bath, the dish walls were far hotter than the custard target; the edges overshot, over-coagulated, expelled water (weeping) and formed steam holes, while the centre lagged. Pulling at 88 °C overcooked the centre too, and the fan dried and coloured the surface. Corrections: water bath at half to two-thirds height with 60–70 °C water in a 150 °C conventional oven (or 100–110 °C fan without a bath); cloth under the moulds; pull at about 80 °C with a sheet-like wobble; lift out of the bath immediately; check the new oven's real temperature with an oven thermometer.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Increasing the sugar in crème anglaise from 15 % to 25 % of the dairy raises the temperature at which it reaches the nappe stage and widens the gap between nappe and graininess."

Design an experiment: independent, dependent and controlled variables; batch size; how you will detect "nappe" and "graininess" objectively; one confounding factor and how you will control it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** sugar at 15 %, 20 % and 25 % of the dairy, all else equal (e.g. 200 g dairy, 40 g yolks per variant; sugar 30, 40, 50 g).
- **Dependent variables:** temperature at which the nappe line first holds clean; temperature at which grain first appears (on the spoon and in a sample strained through a fine sieve); line-spread at 5 °C of samples pulled at fixed temperatures (e.g. 80, 83, 86, 89 °C); residue in the sieve.
- **Objective endpoints:** take a small sample at every 1 °C step from 76 °C onward: spoon a few grams onto a cold plate and photograph it next to a reference; nappe = finger line holds for 10 s; grain = visible particles on a dark plate or residue in a tea strainer. Blind-score smoothness.
- **Controlled:** one yolk batch mixed and divided; same dairy; cold-start method so heating history is equal; same pan, burner setting and stirring; same heating rate (log it; aim for the same °C per minute); same calibrated thermometer.
- **Replication:** run each sugar level twice, in a different order the second time.
- **Confounder:** heating rate differs between runs (a warm pan heats the next batch faster) or sugar changes the volume and heating rate. Control by cooling and drying the pan between runs, starting all batches at 5 °C, and plotting temperature vs time so you can compare only runs with similar rates.
- **Expected:** more sugar shifts nappe and grain upward by a few degrees and widens the gap between them.

</details>
