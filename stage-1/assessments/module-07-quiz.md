## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Name four functions of sugar in a cookie other than sweetness, with a one-line mechanism for each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Any four of: tenderizing (competes for water, delays gluten, starch gelatinization and protein set); moisture retention (hygroscopic); lowering water activity (longer shelf life); browning (Maillard with reducing sugars, caramelization); spread (crystals dissolve as the dough heats and the dough liquefies); aeration in creaming (crystals cut air into fat); crispness (sucrose recrystallizes or forms a glass as the cookie dries).

</details>

**A2.** Rank by relative sweetness (sucrose = 1.0): glucose, fructose, lactose, maltose, sucrose.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fructose (~1.2–1.7) > sucrose (1.0) > glucose (~0.7) > maltose (~0.3–0.5) > lactose (~0.2–0.4).

</details>

**A3.** What does brown sugar contain that white sugar does not, and what does each component do in a cookie?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Molasses (~3–7 %), which brings moisture (~2–4 % in the sugar), invert sugar (reducing and hygroscopic: more browning, more chew, slower hardening), organic acids (react with baking soda: CO₂ lift, lower pH) and minerals and flavour compounds (molasses/caramel flavour, darker colour).

</details>

**A4.** What does DE describe in a glucose syrup, and how does a 63 DE syrup differ from a 38 DE syrup?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dextrose equivalent: how far starch was broken down (glucose = 100). The 63 DE syrup contains more small sugars: it is sweeter, more hygroscopic, browns faster and is less viscous. The 38 DE syrup has more long chains: more body, less sweetness.

</details>

**A5.** Give two differences between Maillard browning and caramelization that you can use on the bench.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Maillard needs a reducing sugar and protein/amino acids; caramelization needs only sugar. Maillard is fast from ~140 °C on a dry surface and speeds up strongly at higher pH (soda, lye); sucrose caramelization needs ~160 °C and above. So: add protein, reducing sugar or soda to brown at a lower temperature; caramel requires very high heat.

</details>

**A6.** Name three things that can seed crystallization in a wet caramel and the control for each.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Undissolved crystals (dissolve completely before boiling); crystals on the pan walls (wash down with a wet brush or cover briefly); stirring after the boil (swirl only); dirty tools or pan (clean equipment). Glucose syrup or acid adds a general safeguard.

</details>

**A7.** State the approximate temperatures for light, medium and dark amber caramel, and the point beyond which it turns bitter.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Light amber ~165–170 °C (330–340 °F); medium ~175–180 °C (345–355 °F); dark ~185–190 °C (365–375 °F); bitter beyond ~190 °C (375 °F).

</details>

**A8.** Describe cookie spread in one sentence using the race model, and name two variables that act on each side.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A cookie spreads until its structure sets, so final width depends on how fast the dough becomes fluid versus how fast the edges set. Flow side: warm or melted butter, more sugar or syrup, baking soda (high pH), low-protein flour, cool oven, greased tray. Set side: cold dough, rest (hydrated flour), more flour or high-protein flour, hot oven, dark tray, egg protein.

</details>

**A9.** Why are chewy cookies chewy and crisp cookies crisp, in terms of sugar state?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Chewy: more moisture, higher aw (~0.5–0.7), sugar amorphous and plasticized by water (a rubbery syrup), helped by invert sugar and molasses that hold water and interfere with crystallization. Crisp: low moisture and aw (~0.2–0.4), sugar crystalline or glassy, so the matrix fractures.

</details>

**A10.** Why must crisp and soft cookies be stored in separate containers?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water moves from higher to lower water activity. The soft cookies lose water and harden; the crisp ones gain it and go soft.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1.** Reduce the sugar in R1-11 by 20 % (keeping the brown : white ratio) and give the new grams, the new total dough weight and the number of 48 g portions (allow 10 g loss). Then list the three changes you expect in the cookies.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Brown: 140 × 0.8 = **112 g** (44.8 %). White: 110 × 0.8 = **88 g** (35.2 %). Sugar removed: 50 g. New total: 1044 − 50 = **994 g**. Portions: (994 − 10) ÷ 48 = 20.5 → **20 portions**.
Expected: less spread and thicker, domed cookies; paler; cakier and drier, staling faster (firm by day 2). Sweetness drops, but less than 20 % as perceived ([X1-18](experiments/X1-18-cookie-sugar-level.md)).

</details>

**B2.** You want to replace 20 % of the granulated sugar in R1-11 with honey (82 % solids) without changing the sugar solids. How many grams of honey, how much granulated sugar remains, and how much extra water enters the dough? What else should you change for the bake?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

20 % of 110 g = 22 g sugar to replace. Honey = 22 ÷ 0.82 = **26.8 g ≈ 27 g**. Granulated sugar remaining: **88 g**. Extra water: 27 − 22 = **~5 g** (about 2 % of flour; ~5 % more than the dough's ~105 g water). Expect faster browning and more spread: lower the oven ~10 °C or shorten the bake 1 min, and rest the dough 24 h.

</details>

**B3.** Scale the R1-10 caramel sauce (200 g sugar : 200 g cream : 40 g butter : 1.5 g salt) to fill 12 jars of 150 g each. Allow 8 % loss to evaporation and 3 % to the pan. Give the grams of each ingredient and one practical constraint.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Target sauce: 12 × 150 = 1800 g. Formula total 441.5 g gives 441.5 × (1 − 0.08) × (1 − 0.03) ≈ 394 g of sauce, so factor = 1800 ÷ 394 ≈ **4.57**. Sugar **914 g**, cream **914 g**, butter **183 g**, salt **6.9 g** (round: 915 / 915 / 185 / 7 g).
Constraint: a home pan cannot safely take this in one batch; the steam burst grows with batch size. Cook it in 2–3 batches of ≤ 400 g sugar in a deep 24 cm pan, or use a professional deep pot.

</details>

**B4.** Estimate the water in the R1-11 dough (butter 16 % water, egg 75 %, brown sugar 3 %) and the ratio of sugar to water. At 20 °C sucrose dissolves at about 2.0 g per g of water. What does this tell you about the dough?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water ≈ 190 × 0.16 + 95 × 0.75 + 140 × 0.03 = 30.4 + 71.3 + 4.2 ≈ **106 g**. Sugar 250 g → sugar : water ≈ **2.4 : 1**, above the ~2.0 : 1 that could dissolve at 20 °C even if the flour took no water. Most of the sugar is undissolved crystals in the dough, and it dissolves as the dough heats in the oven: that is a main driver of spread.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner makes R1-11 on a hot summer day. The butter was left out all morning and was "very soft". They skipped the rest and baked straight away on a greased dark non-stick tray at a dial setting of 180 °C (oven thermometer later showed 165 °C). Result: cookies 13 cm wide, 6 mm thick, with lacy, greasy edges, dark bottoms and a pale top; the centre is thin and chewy. Diagnose, and give corrections in priority order.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Flow dominated the race. The butter was probably above 22 °C (partly liquid), so the dough was warm and fluid with more sugar dissolved; no rest meant unhydrated flour and warm fat; the greased tray lubricated the base; the oven was 15 °C cool, extending the flow time. Greasy edges: fat escaped before the structure set. Dark bottoms with pale tops: dark tray absorbing heat while the oven air was cool.
Corrections: (1) butter at 18–20 °C, measured; (2) rest the portioned dough ≥ 12–24 h and bake from the refrigerator; (3) set the oven by thermometer to a real 180 °C; (4) light aluminium tray with parchment, not grease. Change them together for the next bake, then run X1-20 to see which mattered most.

</details>

**C2.** A wet caramel for sauce (200 g sugar, 60 g water, no glucose) turned cloudy and sandy at about 140 °C. The cook had stirred "to stop it burning" and used the same spatula they had used to scrape the sugar bag. On the second attempt the caramel was fine, but a hard lump formed when they poured in cream straight from the refrigerator. Diagnose both failures.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

First attempt: crystallization of a supersaturated syrup. Stirring after the boil and a spatula carrying sugar crystals both provided seeds; there was no interfering agent. Fix: dissolve fully before boiling, stop stirring (swirl), wash down the walls, clean tools, add 20 g glucose syrup. Rescue: add ~100 g water, redissolve, restart.
Second attempt: cold cream chilled part of the caramel below its setting point, so it solidified into a lump (seized). Fix: warm the cream to 60–80 °C and add it in three parts off the heat. Rescue: return to low heat and stir until the lump dissolves.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Increasing baking soda in the R1-11 dough (without chocolate) from 0.4 % to 0.8 % to 1.2 % of flour increases spread and browning, and above 0.8 % the cookies develop an off-flavour."

Design an experiment: state independent, dependent and controlled variables, batch sizes, how you would lay out trays, what you would measure, and one confounder to guard against.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** baking soda at 0.4, 0.8 and 1.2 % of flour (0.4 / 0.8 / 1.2 g per 100 g flour), weighed to 0.01–0.1 g. Consider a stock mix: soda pre-blended with part of the flour so small amounts are accurate.
- **Dependent:** diameter, thickness, spread ratio; edge, centre and base colour (1–5 scale with photos); surface cracking; interior cell structure (cut one cookie); blind flavour evaluation including "soapy/metallic" yes/no and intensity; optionally the pH of a slurry of crushed cookie in water (10 g in 90 g distilled water) with a pH meter or strips.
- **Controlled:** one master formula; same butter temperature and creaming time; same mixing order; same rest (18–24 h at 3–5 °C); same portion weight (45 g) and shape; same trays, parchment, oven temperature (measured), bake time fixed (11 min).
- **Batch:** 100 g flour per variant (≈ 316 g dough, 6 × 45 g portions).
- **Layout:** 2 trays of 6 with 2 cookies of each variant per tray, positions rotated so every variant is at front and back; or a Latin square with one per variant per tray.
- **Confounder to guard against:** the brown sugar's acid neutralizes part of the soda, so the effective pH depends on the brown sugar batch: use one bag for all variants. Also, flavour judgments are biased if the taster knows the variant: code the samples and have someone else serve them.
- **Expected:** spread and browning rise with soda; at 1.2 % the cookie is darker, coarser and may taste soapy or metallic.

</details>
