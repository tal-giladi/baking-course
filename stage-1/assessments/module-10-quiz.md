## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** State the three traditional balance rules for a regular (low-ratio) creamed cake, and how each changes for a high-ratio cake.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Regular: sugar ≤ flour; eggs ≥ fat; liquids (including eggs) ≈ sugar. High-ratio: sugar > flour (typically 110–140 %); eggs > fat; liquids > sugar; and it needs chlorinated or heat-treated cake flour and emulsified shortening. These are rules of thumb from professional practice, not laws.

</details>

**A2.** Whole egg is both a builder and a tenderizer. Explain, and say which effect dominates in a cake.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The white's proteins coagulate into a firm network (builder); the yolk's fat and lecithin tenderize and emulsify. In a cake, whole egg acts mainly as a structure builder (and a moistener, at ~75 % water).

</details>

**A3.** Why does increasing sugar delay the set of a cake batter? Name the two processes affected.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dissolved sugar competes for water, so starch granules swell and gelatinize at a higher temperature, and it stabilizes proteins so egg proteins coagulate at a higher temperature. Both starch gelatinization and protein coagulation are delayed, so the batter stays fluid longer.

</details>

**A4.** "Baking powder makes the crumb fine." What is wrong with this statement?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Leaveners do not create new cells; their CO₂ diffuses into air cells that already exist from mixing. Fineness is set by the number of cells made during creaming or whipping. Baking powder only enlarges those cells; too much makes the crumb coarser, not finer.

</details>

**A5.** Give the butter temperature and creaming time for a pound cake in a stand mixer, and one consequence each of butter that is too cold and too warm.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

18–20 °C, 4–6 min at medium. Too cold (below ~15 °C): lumps, little air, high SG, dense cake, curdling when eggs are added. Too warm (above ~23–24 °C): greasy, cannot hold air, high SG, heavy cake, curdling or soupy batter.

</details>

**A6.** Why are génoise eggs warmed to 40–45 °C before whipping, and what happens above about 50 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Warming dissolves the sugar, lowers the viscosity and helps proteins form films, so the foam reaches more volume faster. Above about 50 °C the yolk proteins begin to coagulate, forming lumps and reducing foaming.

</details>

**A7.** Give two reasons a chiffon cake is baked in an ungreased tube pan and one reason it is cooled inverted.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Ungreased: the batter grips the walls to climb during baking, and it can hang from them when inverted. The tube conducts heat to the centre of a tall cake. Inverted: while hot, the crumb is weak and full of condensing steam; hanging, gravity stretches it rather than compressing it until it firms.

</details>

**A8.** Give the indicative batter SG ranges for a pound cake and for a génoise, and say how you measure SG.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Pound cake ~0.8–0.9; génoise ~0.4–0.5 (indicative). Weigh a fixed cup filled level with water, then the same cup filled level with batter; SG = batter weight ÷ water weight.

</details>

**A9.** Name four doneness indicators for cakes and the internal temperature range for most cakes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Internal temperature (93–99 °C for most cakes), springback, a clean skewer, and the cake pulling away from the sides; colour and sound are supporting indicators only.

</details>

**A10.** Why should a plain butter cake be stored at room temperature, but a cake filled with whipped cream refrigerated?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Refrigeration speeds starch retrogradation and hardens the butter, so a plain butter cake eats firm and stale sooner; at room temperature, wrapped, it keeps several days. A cream filling is perishable and must be refrigerated for safety, so the texture cost is accepted (or a chiffon-type, oil-based cake is chosen).

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Balance check and rebalance.** A formula for a butter cake reads: flour 1,000 g, sugar 1,100 g, butter 500 g, whole eggs 400 g, milk 500 g, baking powder 40 g, salt 10 g. (a) Express it in baker's %. (b) Check it against the regular and high-ratio rules. (c) Predict the result with unchlorinated flour and butter. (d) Rebalance it as a regular cake, keeping flour and butter unchanged.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Flour 100, sugar 110, butter 50, eggs 40, milk 50, baking powder 4, salt 1.
(b) Sugar 110 > flour: not a regular cake; it is at the bottom of the high-ratio range. Eggs 40 < fat 50: breaks the egg rule under both sets of rules. Liquids = 40 + 50 = 90 < sugar 110: breaks the liquid rule for both (regular needs ≈ sugar; high-ratio needs > sugar).
(c) Too much tenderizer for the builders: the batter sets late, over-expands and sinks; coarse crumb, gummy band under the dip, dark sugary crust. Baking powder at 4 % adds to the over-expansion. With low liquid, the sugar may not fully dissolve (speckled crust).
(d) Sugar 1,000 g (100 %). Eggs at least 500 g (≥ fat); use 550 g for margin. Liquids ≈ sugar: 1,000 − 550 = 450 g milk. Reduce baking powder to about 30 g (3 %). Result: flour 1,000, sugar 1,000, butter 500, eggs 550, milk 450, baking powder 30, salt 10. Check: sugar = flour; eggs 55 ≥ fat 50; liquids 100 = sugar 100.

</details>

**B2. Pan conversion.** You want to bake [R1-18 Génoise](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md) (eggs 200 g, sugar 120 g, flour 120 g, butter 30 g, salt 0.5 g; 20 cm round) in (a) a 24 cm round pan and (b) a 20 × 20 cm square pan, at the same depth. Calculate the factor and each ingredient for both.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Area of 20 cm round = π × 10² = 314.2 cm².
(a) 24 cm round: factor (24 ÷ 20)² = 1.44. Eggs 288 g, sugar 172.8 g, flour 172.8 g, butter 43.2 g, salt 0.72 g. Total 677.5 g.
(b) 20 × 20 square: 400 cm² ÷ 314.2 = 1.27. Eggs 254 g, sugar 152.4 g, flour 152.4 g, butter 38.1 g, salt 0.64 g. Total 597.5 g.
Bake time stays similar (same depth); check from the start of the stated range, and expect corners of the square pan to colour first.

</details>

**B3. Specific gravity.** Your SG cup holds 118 g of water. (a) Pound-cake batter fills it at 106 g; (b) génoise batter at 64 g; (c) a second génoise, folded with 30 strokes per addition, at 71 g. Calculate each SG and comment.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) 106 ÷ 118 = **0.90**: at the top of the indicative 0.8–0.9 range; acceptable but check creaming time and butter temperature, and compare with your own records.
(b) 64 ÷ 118 = **0.54**: slightly above the indicative 0.4–0.5; some foam loss.
(c) 71 ÷ 118 = **0.60**: 11 % denser than (b); over-folding cost air. Expect a lower, tighter cake. The 7 g difference in the cup is the kind of signal you cannot see by eye.

</details>

**B4. Syrup.** A 20 cm génoise weighs 405 g after cooling. You split it into three equal layers and want to apply syrup at 35 % of layer weight, using a heavy syrup of 1.35 : 1 sugar : water by weight. How much syrup in total, and how much sugar and water (make 10 % extra)? What is the sugar concentration?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Syrup needed = 405 × 0.35 = 141.75 g (about 47 g per layer). With 10 % extra: 141.75 × 1.10 = 155.9 g → make 156 g.
Sugar = 156 × 1.35 ÷ 2.35 = 89.6 g; water = 156 ÷ 2.35 = 66.4 g.
Concentration = 1.35 ÷ 2.35 = 57.4 % sugar by weight.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A learner bakes the X1-27 butter cake formula in a 20 cm dark nonstick pan at 190 °C (the dial; not checked). The cake has a tall peak with a crack, long vertical holes from the base to the peak, dark hard sides, and a chewy crumb. The learner mixed "for another minute after the flour to be sure it was smooth". Identify the causes, rank them, and give the corrections.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The cake set too early at the edges and top, with a strong batter: (1) overmixing after flour developed gluten, giving tunnels, the peak and chewiness; (2) an oven that was too hot for a butter cake (190 °C, and not verified); (3) a dark pan that absorbs more radiant heat, setting and browning the sides early. Corrections: mix only until just combined after the flour (or use reverse creaming); bake at 175 °C verified with an oven thermometer, 10–15 °C lower in a dark pan, or use a light aluminium pan; consider baking strips. See [Tunnels](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=tunnels) and [Cracked or domed top](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=cracked-or-domed-top).

</details>

**C2.** A génoise came out 2.8 cm tall in a 20 cm pan (spec 4–5 cm). Observations: foam whipped 4 min on high from eggs straight from the fridge; the foam "was thick but the ribbon disappeared quickly"; batter SG 0.63; butter was melted to bubbling and poured in; cake has a firm, yellow, slightly greasy band at the base. Diagnose, naming each fault and its evidence.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Under-whipped foam: cold eggs not warmed, only 4 min, ribbon not holding (should hold 3–5 s after warming to 40–45 °C and 5–8 min on high plus 1–2 min on medium). (2) Butter too hot and not tempered: fat burst the foam and sank, forming the greasy band. (3) The SG of 0.63 (vs ~0.4–0.5) confirms heavy air loss, from the weak foam and the butter. Corrections: warm eggs and sugar to 40–45 °C, whip to a stable ribbon, fold flour in three additions, butter at 40–50 °C tempered with ~50 g of batter; target SG ≤ 0.5. See [Sponge cake collapsed or deflated](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=sponge-cake-collapsed-or-deflated).

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Wrapping a 20 cm pan with wet baking strips reduces the dome of a butter cake by at least half, without increasing bake time by more than 10 %."

Design the experiment: variables, batch, how you make the comparison fair, measurements, replication, and one confounder.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent variable:** baking strips (wet, wrapped around the pan) vs none.
- **Dependent variables:** dome = centre height − edge height (mm, on the cut face); bake time to 96 °C centre with springback; crust colour of sides; crumb near the edge vs centre; bake loss.
- **Batch:** one batch of the X1-27 CR formula scaled ×3.56 (two 20 cm pans at ×1.78 each, about 650 g batter per pan), divided by weight into two identical light aluminium 20 cm pans. One batch removes batter variation.
- **Controlled:** same pans (material, colour, lining), same batter weight, same oven, same rack, side by side with at least 5 cm between them and from the walls; same doneness criterion; same cooling.
- **Replication and position:** repeat at least twice more, swapping which side of the oven carries the stripped pan, so a hot spot is not mistaken for the strip effect.
- **Confounder:** oven position (hot spots, the fan side), which can dome one pan more than the other regardless of strips; also the strips' water content (soak for a fixed time, squeeze to a fixed weight).
- **Decision rule:** the hypothesis is supported if the mean dome with strips is ≤ 50 % of the mean without, and the mean bake time with strips is ≤ 110 % of the mean without.

</details>
