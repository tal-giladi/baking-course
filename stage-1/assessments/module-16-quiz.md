## A. Knowledge check

**A1.** Name the steps from cocoa bean to chocolate bar in order, and say which step mainly creates flavour precursors, which turns them into flavour, and which sets smoothness.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fermentation, drying, roasting, winnowing, grinding (to liquor), mixing, refining, conching, tempering and moulding. Fermentation creates the precursors; roasting (Maillard reactions) develops flavour; refining sets particle size and so smoothness (conching then coats particles with fat, lowers viscosity and removes harsh volatiles).

</details>

**A2.** What does "64 %" on a dark chocolate measure? What does it not tell you, and where do you find it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The total of cocoa-derived ingredients (cocoa liquor + added cocoa butter + any cocoa powder). It does not tell you the cocoa butter (fat) content or the split between fat and non-fat cocoa solids; read total fat from the nutrition panel (or the manufacturer's data sheet).

</details>

**A3.** Give two differences between couverture and a compound coating, and one reason you would not mix them.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Couverture uses cocoa butter as its fat (high content, typically ≥ 31 %) and must be tempered; compound replaces cocoa butter with other vegetable fats and sets without tempering, with a less clean, waxier melt. Lauric fats in many compounds are incompatible with cocoa butter: mixed, both soften and bloom.

</details>

**A4.** Give the approximate pH of natural and Dutch-processed cocoa, and say which leavening each pairs with.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Natural ~5–6 (acidic): can supply acid for baking soda. Dutch-processed ~7–8 (neutral to alkaline): brings no acid; pair with baking powder, or add another acid if soda is used.

</details>

**A5.** Explain in two sentences why a drop of water seizes chocolate but hot cream makes a ganache.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A little water wets particle surfaces and dissolves some sugar into a sticky syrup that glues the particles into clumps and squeezes out the fat: a stiff paste. Enough water, added hot and at once, dissolves the sugar into a continuous syrup in which the particles and fat droplets are dispersed: a fluid emulsion-suspension.

</details>

**A6.** Give the melting and working temperatures for dark, milk and white chocolate by seeding, and explain why milk and white are lower.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dark: melt 45–50 °C, work 31–32 °C. Milk: melt 40–45 °C, work 29–30 °C. White: melt 40–45 °C, work 28–29 °C. Milk fat dissolves in cocoa butter and disrupts its crystal packing, lowering the melting points of all forms (and milk proteins scorch at lower temperatures). Follow the pack's curve where it differs.

</details>

**A7.** List four properties that form V gives chocolate, and explain why chocolate does not form it when simply cooled.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Gloss, clean snap, contraction (release from moulds), clean melt just below mouth temperature, bloom resistance. Unstable forms nucleate faster than form V, so spontaneous cooling produces forms III/IV first; tempering supplies form V nuclei and holds the chocolate in a window where only form V survives.

</details>

**A8.** How do you tell fat bloom from sugar bloom on the bench, and what causes each?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat bloom: grey, greasy, smears under a warm finger, unaffected by a drop of water; caused by poor temper, warm storage or temperature swings, fat migration. Sugar bloom: white, dry, dusty, dissolves under a drop of water; caused by moisture (condensation after refrigeration, damp storage) dissolving and recrystallizing surface sugar.

</details>

**A9.** Give the dark-chocolate ratios for a glaze, a piping ganache and a truffle ganache, and the ratio shift for milk chocolate.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Glaze 1 : 1; piping 1.5 : 1; truffle 2 : 1 (chocolate : cream, by weight, 35 % cream). Milk chocolate needs more chocolate: about 2 : 1 for glaze, ~2.5 : 1 for piping, 2.5–3 : 1 for truffles; white about 2.5–3 : 1 or more.

</details>

**A10.** What gives a brownie its shiny, crackled top?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A thin layer of whipped egg and dissolved sugar that rises to the surface and dries into a meringue-like film. It needs sugar well dissolved in lightly whipped eggs (helped by adding the warm chocolate mixture) and is lost when sugar stays undissolved or eggs are only stirred.

</details>

## B. Formulation exercises

**B1. Scaling a truffle ganache.** R1-36 formula C is: chocolate 100 %, cream 50 %, invert sugar 5 %, butter 7.5 % (all on chocolate). You need **600 g** of ganache. Calculate each ingredient and the approximate water %, taking cream as 58 % water, invert as 20 % and butter as 16 %.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total % = 162.5. Chocolate = 600 ÷ 1.625 = **369.2 g**. Cream = 184.6 g; invert = 18.5 g; butter = 27.7 g. Check: 369.2 + 184.6 + 18.5 + 27.7 = 600.0 g.
Water = 184.6 × 0.58 + 18.5 × 0.20 + 27.7 × 0.16 = 107.1 + 3.7 + 4.4 = 115.2 g → 115.2 ÷ 600 = **19.2 %**.

</details>

**B2. Converting dark to milk.** A glaze uses 250 g dark chocolate and 250 g cream. You must make it with milk chocolate at the same total weight (500 g) and similar consistency. Calculate the chocolate and cream, and compare the water % of the two glazes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Milk glaze ≈ 2 : 1. Chocolate = 500 × 2/3 = **333 g**; cream = **167 g**.
Water: dark 250 × 0.58 = 145 g → 29 %. Milk 167 × 0.58 = 96.9 g → 19.4 %. The milk version needs much less water for the same consistency because it has fewer cocoa particles and softer fat.

</details>

**B3. Seed calculation.** You have **800 g** of dark couverture melted at 48 °C and want the seed to be **25 % of the total** tempered mass. How much seed do you add, and what is the total? If a colleague instead adds 25 % *of the melted weight*, what seed % of total do they get?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Seed ÷ (800 + seed) = 0.25 → seed = 0.25 × 800 ÷ 0.75 = **266.7 g**; total **1,066.7 g**.
Colleague: 0.25 × 800 = 200 g; 200 ÷ 1,000 = **20 %** of the total, still within the 20–25 % range.

</details>

**B4. Scaling brownies to a new pan.** R1-38 makes 813 g of batter for a 20 × 20 cm pan. You need to fill a **23 × 33 cm** pan to the same depth. Calculate the factor, the batter weight, and the chocolate, butter, sugar, eggs and flour. Will the bake time change much?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Area 759 ÷ 400 = **1.90** (1.8975). Batter 813 × 1.8975 = **1,543 g**. Chocolate 170 × 1.8975 = 322.6 g; butter 265.7 g; sugar 474.4 g; eggs 284.6 g (about 6 eggs; weigh); flour 142.3 g; cocoa 38.0 g; salt 5.7 g; vanilla 9.5 g.
Same depth: bake time stays close (24–30 min), perhaps 2–3 min longer because the larger pan takes longer to heat through; judge by the same cues.

</details>

## C. Troubleshooting cases

**C1.** A café dips candied orange peel in dark chocolate. The pastry cook melts the chocolate in a bowl over boiling water, lets it cool on the bench to 30 °C, and dips the peel, which is stored in the refrigerator. The dipped peel takes 25 minutes to set, looks streaky, and sticks to the parchment. On the display shelf by the window (20 °C in the morning, 29 °C in the afternoon) it turns grey within two days. Diagnose and correct, in priority order.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **No temper:** melted and simply cooled; no seed, no test. Slow set, streaks and sticking are unstable crystal forms. Correct: seed at 20–25 %, work at 31–32 °C, test strip must set in 3–5 min.
2. **Cold centres:** peel from the fridge shock-sets unstable forms and brings condensation (risk of seizing and sugar bloom). Correct: dip dry peel at ~20 °C.
3. **Storage:** 20–29 °C swings on a sunny shelf cause fat bloom even on well-tempered chocolate. Correct: display at a stable 15–18 °C, away from the window.
4. **Bain-marie over boiling water:** steam risk and overheating. Correct: heat off, bowl sealing the pan, or microwave at 50 % power.
The grey is fat bloom (confirm: smears with a warm finger, unaffected by water). The peel is safe; re-melt the chocolate and re-temper.

</details>

**C2.** A cook makes truffles with 400 g of 72 % chocolate and 200 g cream that was boiled for 3 minutes with orange zest, strained and used without re-weighing (it weighed 165 g). They whisk it by hand while it is at 55 °C, add 60 g cold butter cubes straight from the fridge, and put it in the refrigerator to set quickly. The ganache looks oily, fat pools at the edges, and after setting it is hard and grainy. Diagnose and give the rescue and the corrected process.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Causes: (1) **Too little water:** the cream lost 35 g to evaporation; effective ratio 400 : 165 ≈ 2.4 : 1 with a 72 % chocolate, water ≈ (165 × 0.58 + 60 × 0.16) ÷ 625 ≈ 17 %: at or below what the fat needs. (2) **Too much fat:** high-fat 72 % chocolate plus 15 % butter. (3) **Too hot and whisked:** 55 °C and vigorous whisking promote droplet coalescence. (4) **Cold butter** could not disperse. (5) **Fridge setting** gave coarse crystals: hard and grainy.
Rescue: warm to 32–35 °C, add 5–10 % (30–60 g) warm milk or cream, blend with a submerged stick blender until glossy and elastic; set at 16–18 °C for 12 h.
Corrected process: re-weigh infused cream and top up to 200 g (or reduce chocolate to ~340–360 g for a 72 %); add 5–10 % invert sugar; pour cream at ~80 °C, emulsify at 35–45 °C with a stick blender; add soft butter (≤ 10 %, ~40 g) at 35–40 °C; crystallize covered at 16–18 °C for 12 h.

</details>

## D. Experimental design

**Hypothesis to test:** "Adding 10 % glucose syrup (on chocolate weight) to a 1.5 : 1 dark ganache makes it softer at 18 °C and keeps it smooth for longer in refrigerated storage."

Design an experiment: independent, dependent and controlled variables, batch sizes, measurements, replication and one confounder.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A good design:
- **Independent:** glucose syrup at 0 % vs 10 % of chocolate weight. Optionally a third level at 5 %.
- **Batch:** 150 g chocolate + 100 g cream per variant (stick blender works at this size); 10 % variant adds 15 g glucose. Confounder control: glucose adds ~3 g water and 12 g solids; either accept it and note it, or run a third variant with 3 g extra cream so water is matched, isolating the effect of the sugar solids.
- **Controlled:** same chocolate lot, same cream, same heating (cream 80 °C), same emulsifying temperature (35–45 °C) and blender time, same container and fill depth, same crystallization (16–18 °C, 12 h), same storage (4 °C, covered).
- **Dependent:** penetration depth at 18 °C after 12 h (weighted skewer, 3 readings); pipeability (0–3); smoothness and graininess scored blind on day 1, 3, 7 and 14 (brought to 18 °C before scoring); gloss; any visible sugar crystals under a hand lens.
- **Replication:** two independent batches per variant made on different days, or at least two containers per batch; randomize tasting order; blind codes.
- **Confounder:** temperature at testing (cold ganache is firmer): always equilibrate samples to 18 °C for 1 h. Also water difference (above).
- **Expected:** the glucose variant is slightly softer and glossier, and stays smooth longer; the 0 % may develop fine graininess after 1–2 weeks.

</details>
