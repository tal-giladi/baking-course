## Why this lesson exists

The lean loaf in 03.2 ran on a timeline that the formula and your kitchen imposed. A baker works the other way round: first decide when the bread should come out of the oven and how it should taste, then set yeast, temperature and time to get there. This lesson gives you the levers, a quantitative rule for each, and a reliable way to judge the end of bulk, so you can move a bake to fit your day without losing quality.

## You will be able to

1. Name the six levers on fermentation rate and give the direction and rough size of each.
2. Use the "rate doubles per ~8–10 °C" rule and the yeast-dose rule to predict a new bulk time.
3. Plan a retarded bulk or retarded proof at 3–5 °C and explain how chilling changes the dough.
4. Judge the end of bulk from volume (+50–75 %) and four supporting cues, using a marked straight-sided container.
5. Perform the poke test on proofing dough and state three situations where it misleads you.

## The six levers

| Lever | Direction | Rough size of the effect (lean dough, working range) | Side effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Warmer → faster | Rate roughly ×2 per +8–10 °C | Warm: less flavour, slacker dough. Cool: more flavour, more acidity |
| **Yeast %** | More → faster | Halving the yeast makes bulk ~1.5–2× longer (not a clean 2×) | High yeast: yeasty or boozy taste. Low yeast: long timeline, more flavour |
| **Time** | — | The dependent variable. You set the other five so that time fits your schedule | — |
| **Salt %** | More → slower | 2 % vs 0 %: fermentation noticeably slower. Above 2.5 %: strongly slower | Salt also tightens gluten ([X1-06](experiments/X1-06-salt.md)) |
| **Sugar %** | A little (≤ ~5 %) → slightly faster start. A lot → slower | Above ~10 % of flour: osmotic slowdown (M06) | Browning, softness |
| **Hydration** | Wetter → somewhat faster | Modest. A 75 % dough ferments faster than a 60 % one at the same temperature | Wetter doughs also lose shape faster, so they *look* more fermented |

Two levers dominate in practice: **temperature** and **yeast**. Salt stays at ~2 % in almost all lean breads, and sugar belongs to enriched doughs. Keep those two fixed while you learn the other levers.

### Temperature: the rule of thumb

Between roughly 10 and 32 °C, yeast gas production roughly **doubles for every 8–10 °C** increase. This is the working rule for this course, and it is standard professional practice rather than a precise law. The table assumes R1-01 at 0.8 % instant yeast, 2 % salt, 70 % hydration, and a bulk target of +50–75 %:

| Dough temperature | Predicted bulk time | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| 30 °C | ~1–1.25 h | Fast, flatter flavour, dough slackens |
| 27 °C | ~1.25–1.75 h | Still good. Watch the dough closely |
| **24–25 °C** | **~1.5–2.5 h** | Reference |
| 21 °C | ~2.25–3 h | More flavour |
| 18 °C | ~3–4 h | Long bulk. Good flavour |
| 4 °C (fridge) | 12–24 h+ for noticeable rise, and much slower once the dough is fully cold | Retarding (see below) |

You will measure your own numbers in [X1-07](experiments/X1-07-fermentation-temperature.md). A worked prediction: your dough is at 21 °C instead of 24.5 °C, a difference of 3.5 °C. With a ×2 per 9 °C rule, the rate factor is 2^(3.5/9) ≈ 1.31. So a 2 h bulk becomes ~2 h 40 min.

### Yeast: the dose rule

More yeast means more cells making gas from the start. Gas production is not exactly proportional to the dose, because during a long, low-yeast fermentation the yeast population grows, and in a short, high-yeast one it barely does. Working rule: **halving the yeast extends bulk by about 1.5–2×; doubling it shortens bulk to about 0.5–0.65×.** The table assumes 24 °C:

| Instant yeast % | Fresh equivalent % (×3) | Approximate time to +75 % |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 6.0 | ~1 h |
| 1.0 | 3.0 | ~1.5–2 h |
| 0.5 | 1.5 | ~2.5–3.5 h |
| 0.25 | 0.75 | ~4–6 h |

You will verify this in [X1-08](experiments/X1-08-yeast-quantity.md).

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Why doesn't halving the yeast exactly double the time? Two reasons. First, yeast cells multiply during fermentation, so the lower-dose dough partly catches up. Second, the end point is a gas volume, and the dough starts retaining gas only after the water is saturated with CO₂, which adds a fixed "dead time" that doesn't scale. Engineers will recognize a first-order system with a delay. See [SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md).

</div>

### Combining the levers: why bakers ferment slowly

If time is free, why not use lots of yeast and a warm room? Because **flavour and dough maturity build with time, not with gas.** A dough fermented for 1 h at 30 °C and one fermented for 3.5 h at 18 °C can reach the same volume. The slow one has had more time for enzymes to work on starch and protein, for acids to accumulate, and for flavour compounds to form. It also has a more relaxed, extensible gluten network. The fast one tastes flatter and yeastier. Most artisan practice therefore uses **low yeast plus moderate or cool temperature**, and often a period of cold. Preferments ([05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)) take this idea further.

## Retarding: the fridge as a process tool

Retarding means chilling dough to **3–5 °C** to slow fermentation for hours. It does not stop fermentation: yeast still produces some gas, and enzymes and acid-producing reactions continue slowly.

**What retarding does:**

- **Moves the schedule.** Shape at 18:00, bake at 08:00.
- **Builds flavour.** Longer time, more acid and aroma compounds.
- **Changes the crust.** A retarded loaf often bakes with small blisters on the crust and a slightly deeper colour.
- **Makes scoring easier.** Cold dough is firmer and cuts cleanly.

**How dough cools.** Dough is a poor conductor. A ~900 g loaf in a banneton takes roughly 1.5–3 h in a home fridge to get close to fridge temperature, and a 2 kg bulk mass takes considerably longer. Fermentation continues at a declining rate during that time. So:

| Plan | How to do it with R1-01 |
|---|---|
| **Retarded final proof** (most common) | Shape. Leave 15–30 min at room temperature. Refrigerate covered at 3–5 °C for 8–16 h. Bake straight from the fridge; the poke test will read "firmer" than room-temperature proof (see below) |
| **Retarded bulk** | After 2 folds (about 1 h at 24 °C, +20–30 % volume), refrigerate the container 12–24 h. Next day, leave it at room temperature until the dough reaches ~+50–75 % total and ~16–18 °C at the centre, then divide and shape as normal. Proof takes longer from cold dough |
| **Reduced yeast for long retard** | For more than 16 h, drop instant yeast to ~0.4–0.5 % so the dough doesn't over-proof in the fridge |

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** Home fridges vary widely: the door can be 7–8 °C while the back is 2 °C. Put a thermometer next to your dough for a day before you trust a retard. At 7 °C, fermentation runs roughly 1.5–2× faster than at 3–4 °C, and an overnight proof can over-proof.

</div>

## Judging the end of bulk

Volume is the primary measurement. The other cues confirm it.

1. **Volume +50–75 %** for a lean yeasted dough in a straight-sided container with a marked start level. The low end (+50 %) suits weaker flour, warmer dough, or a dough that will get a long retarded proof. The high end (+75 %) suits strong flour and a same-day short proof. Doubling (+100 %), the classic home-recipe instruction, usually over-ferments a lean artisan dough if it is followed by a full proof.
2. **Dome:** the surface is convex and rounded at the edges where it meets the container. A flat or concave top suggests the dough has peaked.
3. **Bubbles:** a few visible at the surface and sides. Many large bubbles plus a sour or boozy smell means over-fermented.
4. **Jiggle:** shake the container, and the dough wobbles as one airy mass, like set jelly, instead of sitting inert.
5. **Aeration and strength:** a wet scraper or hand run down the side and lifted shows a web of cells. The dough releases from the walls easily, and when you tip it out it holds a rounded shape for a few seconds before slowly spreading.

| Under-fermented at the end of bulk | Well fermented | Over-fermented |
|---|---|---|
| +20–40 %, dense and heavy, tight, few bubbles | +50–75 %, domed, airy, jiggles | +100 % or more, flat or sunken top, large bubbles, slack and sticky, sour or boozy smell |
| Shapes easily but springs back. Bakes dense, possibly with blowouts | Shapes with good tension | Tears or won't hold tension. Spreads, pale crust |

## The poke test and its limits

At the end of final proof, press a floured fingertip about 1 cm into the side of the loaf and watch the dent.

| Response | Reading |
|---|---|
| Springs back fully and quickly (1–2 s) | **Under-proofed.** Give it more time |
| Springs back slowly and only partly, leaving a small dent | **Ready.** Bake |
| Stays in, or the dough sighs or deflates around it | **Over-proofed.** Bake immediately, don't score deeply, and expect spreading |

**Where it misleads you:**

- **Cold, retarded dough** is firm and springs back more slowly *because it is cold*, not because it is ready. Many bakers bake retarded dough when it looks "under" by the room-temperature standard. Learn your own fridge by baking a retarded loaf alongside your notes.
- **Very wet doughs** (≥ ~75 %) barely spring back even when under-proofed. **Stiff doughs** (≤ ~60 %) spring back for a long time even when ready.
- **Strength differs.** Strong flour and heavily folded doughs spring back longer. Weak flour gives an early "ready" signal.
- **Where you poke matters.** The surface of a floured banneton loaf behaves differently from a free-standing loaf. Poke in the same place every time.

Combine the poke with **volume** (about +50–75 % from shaping), **softness** (the loaf feels light and marshmallowy rather than dense), and **time relative to your notes**. [X1-09](experiments/X1-09-proof-level.md) calibrates your finger against real outcomes.

3. Use your data to **predict** before you bake: write down "expected end of bulk: HH:MM" and compare it with the actual time. The goal is to be within 20 min.

## What goes wrong

- **Bulk far slower or faster than predicted.** Suspect a yeast dose error, dough temperature, a cold or warm spot, or old yeast. See [bulk too slow or too fast](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=bulk-fermentation-too-slow-or-too-fast).
- **Slack dough, boozy smell, pale crust.** Over-fermented bulk. See [weak, slack dough](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=weak-slack-dough) and [sour or alcoholic off-flavour](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=sour-or-alcoholic-off-flavour).
- **Retarded loaf spreads when turned out.** The fridge was too warm, or the dough stood too long before chilling. See [loaf spreads sideways](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=loaf-spreads-sideways).
- **Dense crumb with a side burst.** Under-fermented or under-proofed. See [dense, tight crumb](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=dense-tight-crumb) and [blowouts on the side](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=blowouts-on-the-side).

## Lab notebook

For each bake, record: yeast %, dough temperature after mixing, the average temperature of the fermentation spot, % rise at every 30 min, and the time you declared bulk done with the cues that decided it. Record your predicted versus actual time. For retards, record the fridge temperature where the dough sat, the time in, the time out, and the dough core temperature at bake. Over several bakes, plot bulk time against dough temperature. This plot is your personal fermentation curve.

## Check yourself

1. Your usual R1-01 bulk is 2 h at 24 °C with 0.8 % instant. You want bulk to take 4 h so it fits your day, without changing temperature. What yeast % do you try?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Halving yeast extends bulk by only ~1.5–2×, so you need somewhat more than one halving: roughly 0.35–0.45× the original dose. Try ~0.3–0.35 % (1.5–1.75 g for 500 g flour) and confirm with the marked container. Alternatively keep 0.8 % and ferment at ~15–16 °C (one "doubling" step of 8–10 °C cooler).

</details>

2. Your kitchen is 29 °C in summer. List two changes that keep R1-01's bulk near 2 h.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Use colder water, so the DDT stays at 24 °C, and ferment somewhere cooler: a cooler with a cold pack, or partly in the fridge. Or reduce the yeast to ~0.5 %, accepting that the dough will still drift warmer. Combining both is best.

</details>

3. A dough is at +100 % with a flat top and a boozy smell. What happened, and what do you do now?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Over-fermented bulk. Shape gently without degassing, give it a short proof (check at 20 min), and bake. Expect a flatter loaf and a paler crust. Next time, shorten bulk or cut yeast or temperature.

</details>

4. A retarded loaf out of the fridge springs back quickly on the poke test. Should you leave it out to proof for another hour?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Not necessarily. Cold dough springs back more because it is firm. Check the volume against shaping (about +50 % or more), how light it feels, and your notes. If those say it is ready, bake from cold.

</details>

5. Why does a 2 kg bulk dough placed in the fridge keep rising for hours?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough conducts heat poorly, so a large mass takes several hours to cool to fridge temperature. Its centre ferments at a declining but significant rate until it gets there. Chill in shallower containers or smaller pieces to retard faster.

</details>
