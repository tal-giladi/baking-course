## Why this lesson exists

Two loaves from the same bulk dough can bake very differently: one tall with an open score, the other flat and burst at the side. The difference is what happened in the last hour. You can learn formula and fermentation from reading. Shaping and scoring are hand skills, so you learn them by repetition. What this lesson provides is the model behind the movements, so you can tell why a shape worked or failed and correct it on the next attempt.

## You will be able to

1. Divide dough by weight to ±2 % with a minimum number of pieces per portion.
2. Pre-shape and bench-rest pieces, and judge when they are relaxed enough to shape.
3. Shape a boule, bâtard, short baguette, pan loaf and roll with a taut skin and a sealed seam.
4. Choose a proofing support (banneton, couche, pan, tray) for each shape and explain what it does.
5. Score at a chosen angle and depth and predict the effect on expansion and ear.

## The model: tension, relaxation and a skin

Gluten dough is **viscoelastic** ([SC-12](stage-1/science/sc-12-rheology.md)). Stretch it quickly and it resists and springs back, which is its elastic side. Give it time and the stress fades as the network rearranges, which is its viscous side. Every step in this lesson uses both:

- **Pre-shaping and shaping** stretch the outer layer quickly, creating tension.
- **Bench rest and proof** give it time, and tension relaxes.
- **The skin** is the outer layer that has been stretched most. Gluten strands there are aligned, so it acts like a membrane under tension around the softer interior. It contains the gas pressure and holds the loaf's height.

If you add too little tension, the loaf spreads during proof and in the oven. If you add too much, especially too fast or on a dough that hasn't relaxed, the skin tears. Once torn, it leaks gas, and the loaf bakes with a rough, lumpy surface and uneven expansion.

<div class="callout key">

**Key point:** Build tension in **stages**, with rests in between: pre-shape (light) → bench rest (relax) → shape (firm) → proof (relax and inflate). Trying to get all the tension in one shaping on an unrested dough is the most common reason dough tears.

</div>

## Dividing

- **Divide by weight**, not by eye. Target ±2 %: ±17 g on an 864 g loaf, and more usefully ±1–2 g on a 65 g roll. Uniform pieces proof and bake in the same time.
- **Cut, don't tear.** Use a bench scraper with one decisive cut. Tearing damages the gluten and the gas structure.
- **Few pieces per portion.** Make one main piece plus at most one or two small top-ups. Fold the small pieces into the centre when you pre-shape so they don't create a seam on the outside.
- **Work quickly and flour lightly.** Put flour only where the scraper cuts. Flour trapped inside the dough makes streaks and seams that don't seal.

## Pre-shaping and bench rest

Pre-shape into the form closest to the final shape:

| Final shape | Pre-shape |
|---|---|
| Boule, roll | Loose round |
| Bâtard, baguette | Loose cylinder (letter-fold, then roll up lightly) |
| Pan loaf | Loose round or loose cylinder |

**How to do it:** place the piece smooth side down. Fold the edges into the centre, flip it seam-down, then drag it towards you on an unfloured part of the bench with a scraper or with cupped hands. The friction against the bench pulls the skin taut.

**Bench rest: 15–30 min**, covered if the room is dry. It is ready when the piece has relaxed and spread by roughly 20–30 % in diameter, and when a gentle stretch meets little resistance. A strong, well-folded dough needs the upper end of the range. A slack dough may need only 10–15 min, or can be shaped without a rest.

## Shaping

### Boule
1. Flip the rested round smooth side down and lightly degas it with your fingertips, leaving the gas in.
2. Fold the edges into the centre all the way round, like wrapping a parcel. Each fold stretches the underside, which will become the skin.
3. Flip it seam-down. With cupped hands, rotate and drag it towards you on unfloured bench. Stop when the surface is smooth and taut and has just started to show strain. Stop *before* you see tears.
4. Place it seam-up in a floured banneton.

### Bâtard
1. Flip the rested round smooth side down. Press it into a rough rectangle.
2. Fold the top third down to the centre and press the edge to seal. Fold the corners in slightly to taper the ends.
3. Roll it towards you, pressing the seam with the heel of your hand at each turn. This builds tension along the length.
4. Seal the final seam by pinching or by pressing with the heel of your hand. Roll it gently to even the shape and taper the ends, to about 22–25 cm long for R1-01.
5. Place it seam-up in an oval banneton or a floured couche pleat.

### Short baguette (baguette-lite)
Real baguettes are 55–65 cm long and weigh about 250–350 g. They don't fit a home oven. Make **35–40 cm "demi" baguettes of 200–250 g** instead.
1. Pre-shape into loose cylinders. Rest 20–30 min.
2. Press into a rectangle. Fold the top third down and seal with the heel of your hand. Fold the top edge down to the bottom and seal firmly, creating a tight cylinder with the seam along its length.
3. Roll to length from the centre outwards with both hands, with light even pressure and your hands moving apart. Taper the ends.
4. Proof seam-up in couche pleats, which support the sides so the baguette stays round. Transfer with a flipping board or a floured piece of card onto parchment.

(Full baguettes, and the poolish that makes them better, come in [05.2](stage-1/module-05/lesson-02.md) with [R1-04](recipes/R1-04-poolish-bread.md).)

### Pan loaf
1. Press the rested piece into a rectangle about the pan length × 1.5.
2. Roll it up tightly from the short side (or letter-fold, then roll), sealing at each turn. The result is a cylinder the length of the pan.
3. Place it seam-down in the greased pan so that the ends touch the pan ends. This makes the dough rise up rather than out.

### Rolls
1. Cup your hand over a 60–70 g piece on unfloured bench.
2. Move your hand in small circles, fingertips and palm edge in contact. The piece rolls against the bench and tucks itself under into a tight ball, with the seam forming at the base.
3. Stop when the surface is smooth and tight: usually 5–10 circles.

## Proofing containers

| Support | Used for | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Banneton** (cane or wood-pulp basket) | Boule, bâtard | Supports the sides so the loaf proofs up rather than out. Breathes, so the skin dries slightly and scores cleanly. Leaves a flour spiral if unlined |
| **Lined bowl/colander** | Boule, bâtard | Home banneton. Use a linen or cotton cloth heavily rubbed with rice flour |
| **Couche** (heavy linen) | Baguette, bâtard, rolls | Pleats form walls between pieces. Absorbs surface moisture |
| **Loaf pan** | Pan loaf | Defines the shape. The dough fills the pan and rises above it |
| **Tray + parchment** | Rolls | Space pieces about 3–4 cm apart for separate rolls, or ~1 cm apart for pull-apart rolls |

**Covering.** Proof under a cover (a shower cap, a large upturned bowl, a plastic bag) so the skin doesn't dry into a crust, which would stop expansion and make scoring drag. Bannetons for retarding go into a bag.

**Proof level** is judged as in [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md): by the poke test, volume and feel. For pan loaves there is a clearer cue: proof until the top crowns about 1–2 cm above the rim of a standard 22 × 11 cm pan (see [R1-02](recipes/R1-02-rolls-pan-loaf.md)).

## Scoring

### What scoring controls
In the first minutes of baking, the loaf expands quickly while the surface starts to dry and set. A score is a **planned weak point**:

- **Where it opens.** Without a score, the loaf splits along the weakest part of the skin, which is often near the base or at the seam: a blowout.
- **How much it springs.** A good score lets the loaf expand fully. A shallow or dragged score limits the expansion.
- **The shape of the opening.** An angled cut creates a flap of dough, the ear, that lifts, dries and browns crisp.
- **Decoration and identity.** Bakeries mark products with their own patterns.

### Angle, depth, tool

| Parameter | For an ear (bâtard, baguette) | For even opening (boule) |
|---|---|---|
| Blade angle to the surface | **30–45°** (shallow, cutting under the skin) | ~80–90° (near vertical) |
| Depth | **~5–8 mm** | ~5–8 mm |
| Speed | One confident stroke. Do not saw | Same |
| Tool | Lame with a curved or straight razor blade. A new blade every 2–3 bakes | Straight blade or very sharp serrated knife as a fallback |

**Patterns:**

- **Bâtard:** one long cut, slightly off-centre and nearly parallel to the long axis. Or 2–3 overlapping diagonal cuts.
- **Boule:** a square (four cuts), a cross, or a hashtag.
- **Short baguette:** 3 cuts, each almost parallel to the long axis. Each cut overlaps the previous one by about a third of its length, and all fall within a central band about 2 cm wide.
- **Pan loaf:** one central lengthwise cut, optional.
- **Rolls:** a single slash or a cross, or none.

**Adjust for proof level.** A well-proofed loaf takes the standard cut. A slightly under-proofed loaf can take a deeper cut to release its extra spring. An over-proofed loaf needs a **shallow** cut, or none, because it has little strength left and a deep cut collapses it.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Razor blades are unforgiving. Mount the blade on a lame or holder, never hold a loose blade in your fingers, cut away from your supporting hand, and store the lame capped. Throw away old blades in a sealed container.

</div>

## On the bench

1. **Shaping drill.** Make one batch of R1-02 dough, or use surplus bulk dough, and shape ten rolls, then reshape them after a 10 min rest. Repeat. This is how you build hand skill.
2. Bake [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md) as a **bâtard** and aim for an ear: 30–45° blade, ~6 mm deep, one stroke.
3. Bake [R1-02 Rolls and pan loaf](recipes/R1-02-rolls-pan-loaf.md): divide by weight, shape rolls, shape a pan loaf.
4. Run [X1-09 Proof level](experiments/X1-09-proof-level.md) to connect the poke test with the scoring bloom.

## What goes wrong

- **Torn surface at shaping.** Too little bench rest, too tight a pre-shape, or dough over-developed or under-hydrated. See [dough tears or feels too tight](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=dough-tears-or-feels-too-tight).
- **Loaf spreads when turned out of the banneton.** Too little tension, over-proof, or dough too slack. See [loaf spreads sideways](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=loaf-spreads-sideways).
- **Burst at the side, not at the score.** Score too shallow, under-proof, or a seam that didn't seal. See [blowouts on the side](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=blowouts-on-the-side).
- **Score seals over, no ear.** Cut too vertical or too shallow, dragged blade, dry skin, or no steam. See [poor oven spring](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=poor-oven-spring).

## Check yourself

1. Why is a bench rest needed after pre-shaping but not after dividing alone?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Pre-shaping stretches the outer layer and adds tension, and the dough needs time for that stress to relax before the final shape. Dividing by a clean cut adds little tension, and the piece is pre-shaped immediately.

</details>

2. You want a pronounced ear on a bâtard. Give the blade angle, depth and one condition the proof must meet.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 30–45° to the surface, ~6–8 mm deep, in one stroke. The loaf must be at or slightly short of full proof, so it still has spring. Steam or a lid is also needed so the flap stays extensible long enough to lift.

</details>

3. Your boule proofed in an unlined colander stuck and deflated when you turned it out. What two things do you change?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Line the colander with a cloth and dust it generously with rice flour (or a rice/wheat mix). Check the proof level too, because an over-proofed loaf sticks more and deflates more easily.

</details>

4. How much should a 22 × 11 cm pan loaf rise before baking?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Until the crown is about 1–2 cm above the rim (for a ~7 cm deep pan), with a slow partial spring-back on the poke test. It will gain a few more centimetres in the oven.

</details>

5. An over-proofed bâtard is ready to bake. How do you score it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Shallowly (2–4 mm), or not at all. The network is already over-stretched, so a deep cut releases the gas and collapses the loaf.

</details>
