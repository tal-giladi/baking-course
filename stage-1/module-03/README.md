# M03 · Yeast and fermentation: lean bread

## Purpose

This module turns the flour–water–salt system from [M02](stage-1/module-02/README.md) into bread. You add one living ingredient, baker's yeast, and learn to run the whole sequence from scaling to cooling as a controlled process. Timings are not the main skill here. The main skill is learning to read the dough: how fast it is fermenting, what moves that rate, and how to tell when bulk fermentation and final proof are done. Every later yeasted product in Stage 1 builds on the lean loaf you make here: poolish bread, sourdough, milk bread, brioche and croissant.

## Objectives

By the end of M03 you can:

1. Explain what yeast consumes and produces in a lean dough, and predict how temperature, salt and sugar change its activity.
2. Convert between fresh, active dry and instant yeast, and know that the conversion depends on the brand.
3. Run the twelve steps of bread for a lean straight dough, hitting a desired dough temperature (DDT) of 24–25 °C and a baked internal temperature of at least 96 °C.
4. Control how fast fermentation runs with yeast %, temperature and time, including retarding in the refrigerator, and plan a bake schedule around those levers.
5. Judge the end of bulk fermentation by volume increase (50–75 %) and dough cues, and judge final proof by the poke test, knowing where that test fails.
6. Divide, pre-shape, shape (boule, bâtard, short baguette, pan loaf, rolls), proof and score dough so that you get predictable oven spring.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* | The species of yeast sold as baker's yeast. It ferments sugars to carbon dioxide and ethanol. |
| Fresh (compressed) yeast | Moist block of live yeast cells, about 70 % water. Perishable; keep it refrigerated and use it within 2–3 weeks. |
| Instant yeast | Dried yeast in fine granules that you can mix straight into flour. About ⅓–0.4 of the fresh-yeast weight gives the same activity. |
| Active dry yeast | Dried yeast in larger granules, traditionally rehydrated in warm water first. About 0.4–0.5 of the fresh-yeast weight. |
| Fermentation | Yeast breaking down sugars without oxygen into CO₂, ethanol and flavour compounds. In a bakery it also means the whole period the dough spends rising. |
| Bulk fermentation | The first rise, with the dough still in one mass. It is where most of the flavour and dough strength develop. |
| Final proof | The last rise, after shaping and before baking. |
| Desired dough temperature (DDT) | The target temperature of the dough when mixing ends, reached by setting the water temperature. 24–25 °C for R1-01. |
| Fold (stretch and fold) | Stretching the dough and folding it over itself during bulk. It builds strength, evens out temperature and redistributes gas. |
| Pre-shape | A loose first shaping of divided pieces, done so they are uniform before the final shape. |
| Bench rest | 15–30 min rest after pre-shaping so the gluten relaxes enough to be shaped without tearing. |
| Surface tension (skin) | The taut outer layer built during shaping. It holds the loaf's shape and steers where it expands. |
| Retarding | Slowing fermentation by chilling the dough to about 3–5 °C, to change the schedule or build flavour. |
| Poke test | Pressing a floured finger into proofing dough and judging how fast the dent springs back. It is an estimate of proof level, not a measurement. |
| Oven spring | The rapid rise in the first 10–15 min of baking, from gas expansion, steam and a last burst of yeast activity. |
| Scoring | Cutting the surface just before baking so the loaf expands where you choose. For an ear, cut at 30–45° and about 5–8 mm deep. |
| Ear | The raised, crisp flap of crust along a shallow-angled score. |
| Osmotic stress | Water pulled out of yeast cells by high concentrations of dissolved sugar or salt around them. It slows or stops fermentation. |
