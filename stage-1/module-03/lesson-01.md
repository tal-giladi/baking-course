## Why this lesson exists

In M02 you built a gluten network that can stretch and hold gas. Now you need something to make that gas. Yeast is the only ingredient in a lean dough that is alive, and it is the only one whose output changes by the hour and by the degree. If you understand what yeast needs, what it produces and what slows it down, you can predict a dough's behaviour and stop following times from a recipe that assumed a different kitchen.

## You will be able to

1. Name the three commercial forms of baker's yeast and convert any quantity between them, stating the uncertainty.
2. Trace the sugar supply in a lean dough from sucrose and damaged starch to the sugars yeast actually takes up.
3. List the products of fermentation and say which one raises the bread and which ones flavour it.
4. Sketch how yeast activity responds to temperature and give the approximate numbers for slow, optimum, rapid and lethal.
5. Explain why sugar and salt slow yeast, and why dry salt should not be left sitting on yeast.

## What baker's yeast is

Baker's yeast is *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, a single-celled fungus. Each cell is roughly 5–10 µm across and reproduces by budding. The same species, in different strains, makes beer and wine. Commercial baker's yeast is grown aerobically on molasses. With oxygen available, the cells respire sugar completely to CO₂ and water and multiply quickly. The yeast is then harvested, washed and sold either moist or dried.

In a dough, the small amount of oxygen mixed in is used up within minutes. From then on the yeast **ferments**: it breaks sugars down only partway, which gives it much less energy per molecule of sugar but produces the two things you want, carbon dioxide and ethanol (see [SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)).

Baking needs the yeast to produce gas, and at the doses you use it does not have to multiply much. In a 2-hour straight dough the yeast population grows only modestly. In long, low-yeast fermentations it grows more, which is one reason halving the yeast does not simply double the time (see [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md)).

## Commercial forms and conversions

| Form | Moisture | How it's used | Storage | Relative quantity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh (compressed, cake) yeast | ~70 % | Crumbled into the flour or dispersed in some of the water | 2–4 °C, use within ~2–3 weeks of production; do not freeze | 1 (reference) |
| Active dry yeast (ADY) | ~7–8 % | Traditionally rehydrated in water at ~38–40 °C for 5–10 min. Many modern ADYs also work mixed straight in | Cool and dry; refrigerate once opened | ~0.4–0.5 × fresh |
| Instant yeast (IDY) | ~4–6 % | Mixed directly into the flour. Rehydration is unnecessary | Sealed: room temp for months. Opened: fridge or freezer, airtight, ~3–6 months | ~0.33–0.4 × fresh |

**The working rule:** fresh : active dry : instant ≈ **1 : 0.5 : 0.33**. For example, 12 g fresh ≈ 6 g active dry ≈ 4 g instant. Some bakers and manufacturers use 0.4 for instant (12 g fresh ≈ 4.8 g instant). The real ratio depends on the brand and strain and on how old the package is, so treat any conversion as a **starting point**. Confirm it by watching the dough's rise against the targets in [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md). When you change brand, re-measure the time to a 75 % rise once (the [X1-08](experiments/X1-08-yeast-quantity.md) setup works well for this).

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Most artisan bakeries in Europe use fresh yeast. Most home bakers and many North American formulas use instant. When you convert a formula from fresh to instant, the yeast grams shrink by about two-thirds. If the dough is stiff or the batch is large, add back the water the fresh yeast would have supplied: fresh yeast is ~70 % water, so 30 g of fresh contributes ~21 g of water. At home-batch sizes that correction is usually under 1 % hydration and can be ignored.

</div>

**Rehydration temperature matters for dried yeast.** Cells coming out of the dry state have leaky membranes for a short time. Rehydrate them in cold water below ~15 °C and they lose cell contents, including glutathione, a compound that weakens gluten. The dough gets slacker and the yeast less active. This is why ADY instructions specify warm water. Instant yeast mixed into flour hydrates more gradually and is less sensitive, but do not pour ice water directly onto it.

## What yeast eats

Yeast ferments simple sugars: glucose, fructose, and (after transporting it into the cell) maltose. A lean dough contains no added sugar, so where does the food come from?

| Source | Amount in flour | How yeast gets it |
|---|---|---|
| Free glucose, fructose | Small (fractions of a %) | Taken up directly. Used first |
| Sucrose (and small oligosaccharides) | ~0.5–1 % of flour | **Invertase**, an enzyme the yeast carries at its cell surface, splits sucrose into glucose + fructose within minutes of mixing |
| Maltose from starch | Continuous supply during fermentation | Flour **amylases** (mostly β-amylase, with some α-amylase) cut **damaged starch** granules into maltose. The yeast takes maltose in with a transporter (maltose permease) and splits it inside the cell with **maltase** |

The first three sources are used up early. After that, a lean dough depends on **maltose from starch**, so its fermentation is limited by how much starch is damaged (from milling, see [02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md)) and how much amylase activity the flour has. Millers adjust the amylase level, often by adding malted flour or fungal amylase. See [SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md).

Some sugar also has to be *left over* at the end. Residual sugars feed crust browning (Maillard reactions and caramelization, [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)). A dough fermented so long that the yeast has used nearly all the available sugar bakes a pale crust even at the right oven temperature. You will see this in [X1-09](experiments/X1-09-proof-level.md).

## What yeast makes

| Product | Why you care |
|---|---|
| **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** | Dissolves in the dough's water until the water is saturated, then diffuses into the gas cells that mixing created. Yeast does not create new bubbles. It inflates the ones mixing made (see [03.4](stage-1/module-03/lesson-04.md) on why the degassing and folding matter) |
| **Ethanol** | Produced in about the same molar amount as CO₂. It evaporates in the oven, so almost none stays in the bread. In the dough it softens gluten slightly and dissolves flavour compounds. In large amounts it smells boozy, a sign of overfermentation |
| **Flavour compounds** | Esters (fruity), higher alcohols, aldehydes and small amounts of sulfur compounds. Together they give a "fermented, wheaty" smell. Slower, cooler fermentation gives more of these per unit of gas, and more flavour |
| **Organic acids** | Small amounts (e.g., succinic, acetic) lower dough pH slightly, from about 6.0 in fresh dough toward 5.3–5.6 by the end of a straight-dough fermentation. Acidity tightens gluten a little and adds some flavour. Real sourness needs lactic acid bacteria (M05) |
| **Heat** | Fermentation releases heat. A large bulk batch warms itself by a degree or two over several hours. At home-batch sizes the effect is small but measurable |

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** The simplified overall equation is C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂. By mass, one gram of glucose gives about 0.49 g CO₂ and 0.51 g ethanol. The actual yield is lower, because the cell also spends some sugar on building itself and on side products such as glycerol. The practical point is that **every gram of gas costs a gram of sugar**. A long fermentation therefore does two things at once: it builds flavour, and it uses up sugar you may want for browning.

</div>

## How yeast responds to temperature

Yeast is an enzyme machine, so its rate depends strongly on temperature. The numbers below are standard professional practice for baker's yeast in a lean dough. They are approximate, and strains differ.

| Dough temperature | Yeast activity | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 °C | Nearly dormant. Enzymes still work slowly | Fridge-retarded dough rises very slowly over 12–72 h |
| 5–10 °C | Slow | Rise measured in many hours |
| 18–22 °C | Moderate | Long, flavourful fermentation. Times around 3–4 h for a 1 % yeast dough |
| **24–27 °C** | **Optimum for bread**: a good balance of rate, flavour and dough strength | Target zone for most lean straight doughs |
| 32–35 °C | Fastest gas production | Quick rise, flatter flavour, and the dough slackens because enzymes and acids work on gluten faster than the dough gains strength |
| ~40–45 °C | Falling off. Cells stressed | |
| **~55–60 °C** | **Cells die** | Happens in the oven. Oven spring continues after this from gas and steam expansion |

In the working range from ~10 to ~32 °C, **gas production roughly doubles with every 8–10 °C rise**. That is the rule of thumb for this course, and you will measure it in [X1-07](experiments/X1-07-fermentation-temperature.md). Its practical meaning is that a 3 °C miss on dough temperature changes bulk time by roughly 25–30 %. This is why [01.3 DDT](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md) comes before bread.

<div class="callout key">

**Key point:** The *dough's* temperature controls fermentation. The room's temperature matters only because it pulls the dough toward itself over time. A 900 g dough mixed at 24 °C in a 20 °C kitchen drifts down slowly, over hours. Measure the dough, not the air.

</div>

## Stress: sugar, salt and the osmotic effect

Yeast cells have a membrane that lets water through freely but controls the passage of dissolved substances. When the water around a cell holds a lot of dissolved sugar or salt, water flows *out* of the cell by osmosis, and the cell slows down to cope. This is **osmotic stress**.

- **Salt.** Salt at 2 % of flour (about 2.9 % of the water at 70 % hydration) slows fermentation noticeably compared with an unsalted dough. You saw the effect on dough strength in [X1-06](experiments/X1-06-salt.md). This is part of why salt is in bread: it moderates the rate. At 3 % the slowdown is strong.
- **Sugar.** A little sugar (up to a few % of flour) can speed up the start of fermentation. Above roughly 5–10 % of flour, osmotic stress takes over and fermentation slows. At 20 % and above, normal instant yeast struggles, which is why **osmotolerant** yeast exists. You will measure this in [X1-15](experiments/X1-15-sugar-in-dough.md) in M06.
- **Direct salt contact.** Dry salt poured onto fresh yeast, or onto a small puddle of yeast dissolved in water, creates a very concentrated solution around the cells and can damage them before the dough is mixed. In practice, mixing salt and instant yeast together into the flour and then mixing promptly is fine. What you should not do is leave salt sitting on yeast in a small amount of liquid for 10–20 min. If you rehydrate ADY or disperse fresh yeast, keep the salt in the flour.

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** Hot water is the most common way beginners kill yeast. Water above ~50 °C poured onto yeast can kill a large share of it before the flour cools the mix. Check the water with a thermometer. The DDT calculation in 01.3 rarely calls for water above 35–38 °C.

</div>

## On the bench

Do this 30-minute demonstration before your first loaf. It is not a formal experiment, but it trains your eye.

1. Weigh three jars, each holding 100 g flour + 70 g water + 1 g instant yeast. Add 2 g salt to jar B. Leave jar C with no yeast.
2. Mix each to a paste, push it level, mark the level on the outside, and note the paste temperature (aim for 24–26 °C).
3. Keep all three at room temperature. At 30, 60 and 90 min, mark the level and smell each jar.

You should see A rise ahead of B (the salt slows it), and C stay flat. At 90 min, A smells distinctly yeasty-fruity. Write the rises down: you will compare them with the rate numbers in [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md).

Then read [R1-01 Lean straight-dough loaf](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md) and check whether you have the yeast, the container and the thermometer it calls for.

## What goes wrong

- **Dough doesn't rise.** Dead yeast (old package, hot water), forgotten yeast, or a very cold dough. Test the yeast: 1 g in 20 g water at ~35 °C with a pinch of sugar should foam within 10 min. See [Collapsed or flat loaf](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=collapsed-or-flat-loaf) and [Bulk too slow or too fast](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=bulk-fermentation-too-slow-or-too-fast).
- **Dough races and smells of alcohol.** The dough is too warm or has too much yeast, or you used fresh-yeast grams for instant yeast (3× too much). See [Sour or alcoholic off-flavour](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=sour-or-alcoholic-off-flavour).
- **Pale crust after a long, warm fermentation.** Sugar depleted. See [Pale crust](troubleshooting/bread-lean.md?id=pale-crust).

## Check yourself

1. A formula calls for 15 g fresh yeast. You have instant. How much do you use, and what range would you accept?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Working rule 0.33 × 15 ≈ 5 g instant. Range 0.33–0.4 → 5–6 g. Start at 5 g and confirm with the rise. The fresh yeast would have supplied ~10 g of water, which is negligible at this scale.

</details>

2. A lean dough contains no added sugar. What does the yeast live on after the first 30 minutes?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Maltose. Flour amylases (mainly β-amylase) produce it from damaged starch, and the yeast takes it up and splits it with maltase. The small amount of sucrose and free sugars in flour is used up early, after invertase has split the sucrose.

</details>

3. Your dough came off the mixer at 21 °C instead of 24 °C. Roughly how much longer will bulk take?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The rate roughly doubles per 8–10 °C, so 3 °C lower means roughly 20–30 % slower. A 2-hour bulk becomes about 2.5 h, and a bit more if the kitchen is also cool. Judge it by volume, not by the clock.

</details>

4. Why does a very long, warm fermentation give a pale crust?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Yeast consumed most of the fermentable sugar, so few reducing sugars are left for Maillard browning and caramelization on the crust.

</details>

5. Why is ethanol hardly present in the finished bread?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It boils at ~78 °C and evaporates during baking as the loaf heats past that temperature. Its main traces are in aroma, and in the dough's behaviour before baking.

</details>
