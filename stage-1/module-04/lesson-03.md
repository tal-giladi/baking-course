## Why this lesson exists

The bake does not end when the loaf leaves the oven. For the next one to three hours the crumb is still setting and losing water, and for the next several days its starch slowly reorganizes into the firm, dry-tasting texture called stale. Most home bakers blame staling on "drying out" and store bread in the refrigerator to prevent it, which is exactly the wrong move. This lesson gives you the correct model so you can choose storage by mechanism, and it is the foundation for every anti-staling technique later in the course.

## You will be able to

- Explain what happens during cooling and why cutting a hot loaf gives a gummy crumb.
- Distinguish staling (starch retrogradation), crust softening (moisture migration), drying and mould as four separate processes with different causes and remedies.
- Explain why bread stales fastest in the refrigerator and why reheating temporarily reverses staling.
- Choose a storage method for a given bread and time horizon, and freeze and reheat bread correctly.

## Cooling: the last stage of baking

A lean loaf leaves the oven with its centre at about 97 °C, its crumb at roughly 40–45 % moisture and its crust with only a few percent. Three things then happen together:

1. **Steam escapes.** Water keeps evaporating from the hot crumb and leaves through the crust. A loaf typically loses another 1–3 % of its weight as it cools, most of it in the first 30–60 minutes. Cool on a wire rack so the base can vent; a loaf cooled on a solid surface sweats and its base goes soft.
2. **The crumb sets.** Hot gelatinized starch is a soft, sticky, weak gel. As it cools, the **amylose** (the linear starch fraction) that leaked from the granules re-associates into a network within hours. This is what firms a warm, fragile crumb into a sliceable one. It is the first, fast step of retrogradation, and it is desirable.
3. **Pressure and temperature equalize.** The crust contracts slightly as it cools; you hear the "singing" crackle of a lean crust as it fractures in fine lines. That is normal and a sign of a thin, well-dried crust.

**Why a hot cut is gummy.** Cut a loaf at 70 °C and the knife drags through an unset starch gel full of steam. The gel smears and compresses into a dense, wet-looking band, and the escaping steam carries off moisture the crumb would otherwise have kept. The texture is permanently damaged in that area.

| Loaf | Minimum cooling before cutting | Centre temperature to aim for |
|---|---|---|
| Rolls, 60–100 g | 20–30 min | below ~40 °C |
| Lean loaf, 500–900 g | 1–2 h | below ~35 °C |
| Large loaf, 1.5–2 kg; high-rye breads | 3 h or more (rye often the next day) | room temperature |

These are standard professional practice values; check with a probe thermometer the first few times.

## Staling is not drying

A slice left in a sealed bag for three days feels firm, crumbly and dry-tasting, yet it has lost almost no water. Staling is a change in the **structure** of the starch, not in the amount of water.

### Starch retrogradation

When starch gelatinized in the oven, its ordered crystalline regions melted and the molecules became disordered and hydrated. On cooling and storage they slowly re-order:

- **Amylose** retrogrades fast, within hours of baking. This is the cooling "set" above.
- **Amylopectin** (the branched fraction, most of wheat starch) retrogrades **slowly, over days**. Its short branches re-associate into crystalline regions, trapping water inside the crystals and stiffening the swollen granules. This is staling.

As amylopectin crystallizes, water moves out of the gluten network and into the starch crystals, and from the crumb toward the crust. The crumb becomes firm and crumbly, and it *tastes* dry because water is now bound in crystals rather than free to moisten your mouth ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md), [SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)).

### Temperature: the counterintuitive part

Retrogradation is a crystallization, and like most crystallizations its rate depends on two opposing effects: colder conditions favour crystal formation, but molecules move more slowly. The result is a maximum rate at low but above-freezing temperatures:

| Storage temperature | Staling rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Freezer, −18 °C | Essentially stopped | Water frozen, molecular mobility very low |
| Refrigerator, 2–5 °C | **Fastest**: several times faster than room temperature | Near the maximum crystallization rate |
| Room, 18–24 °C | Moderate: lean bread noticeably stale in 1–2 days | Slower crystallization |
| Warm, 35 °C+ | Slower, but mould risk rises sharply | Crystals form less readily |

Never refrigerate bread you intend to eat as bread. The refrigerator's only advantage is slowing mould in humid climates, and you pay for it in texture.

### Staling is reversible (for a while)

Amylopectin crystals in bread melt at about 50–60 °C. Heat a stale slice or loaf above that, with enough moisture present, and the crystals melt, the starch re-hydrates, and the crumb softens again. This is why toast, or a stale loaf refreshed in a hot oven, tastes fresh. The reversal is temporary: the melted starch re-crystallizes as it cools, faster than it did the first time, because the loaf has lost some water and there are crystal nuclei left. A refreshed loaf should be eaten within a few hours.

**Refreshing a whole loaf:** run the crust briefly under the tap or brush with water, bake at 180–200 °C (355–390 °F) for 5–10 minutes (small loaf) or 10–15 minutes (large), until the crust is crisp and the centre is warm through (above 60 °C).

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Staling is starch crystallizing, not water leaving. It is fastest in the refrigerator, stopped in the freezer, and reversed by heating above about 60 °C.

</div>

## Crust softening: moisture migration

A crisp lean crust at 1 hour becomes soft or leathery by the next morning, even when the crumb is still pleasant. This is **moisture migration**: water moves from the wet crumb (high water activity) toward the dry crust (low water activity), and in humid air the crust also absorbs water from outside. As the crust gains water it passes from glassy (brittle, crackly) to rubbery (leathery). The drier and thinner the crust, the more noticeable the change.

Moisture migration is physics you can slow but not stop. A thick, well-baked crust takes longer to soften; a paper or linen bag lets water escape to the room instead of accumulating in the crust; a plastic bag traps it and softens the crust within hours. A 5-minute re-crisp in a hot oven restores the crust temporarily.

## Drying and mould: two other problems

**Drying** is real water loss to the air, mostly from cut faces and through the crust when bread is stored unwrapped. It makes crumb hard and crust rock-like. Unlike staling, it is not reversed by reheating alone.

**Mould** is microbial growth, not a texture change. Mould spores land on the loaf from the air and equipment after baking (the bake itself kills them) and grow where water activity is high and the surface stays humid. Lean bread crumb has a high water activity (roughly 0.94–0.97) so it supports mould readily; plastic storage in a warm kitchen shows visible colonies within 3–7 days. Staling and mould are driven by opposite conditions: a sealed bag slows drying but speeds mould; the refrigerator slows mould but speeds staling.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Discard bread with visible mould. Bread is porous and mould hyphae extend well beyond the visible spot, so cutting it off is not reliable. Do not sniff mouldy bread closely. See [00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md) and [SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md).

</div>

| Process | What changes | Speeds it | Slows it | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Staling (retrogradation) | Starch structure | Refrigeration; lean formula | Freezing; fat, sugar, pre-gelatinized starch, enzymes | Yes, temporarily, by heating |
| Crust softening | Water moves into crust | Plastic bag; humid air; thin crust | Paper/linen; well-baked crust | Yes, by re-crisping |
| Drying | Water leaves bread | Unwrapped, cut face exposed | Wrapping; cut face down on a board | No |
| Mould | Microbes grow | Warm, humid, sealed | Dry storage, freezing, acidity (sourdough) | No: discard |

## Storage choices

| Horizon | Lean crusty bread | Soft/enriched or pan bread |
|---|---|---|
| Same day | Cut face down on a board, uncovered | Bag after fully cool |
| 1–2 days | Paper or linen bag, or bread box, room temperature | Plastic bag, room temperature |
| Longer | Freeze | Freeze |
| Never | Refrigerator | Refrigerator (except in hot humid climates where mould wins) |

### Freezing correctly

1. **Cool completely** (warm bread condenses water inside the bag, which becomes ice crystals and freezer burn).
2. **Slice first** if you will use it by the slice; freeze whole if you will serve it whole.
3. **Wrap tightly**, removing air: a freezer bag with the air pressed out, or film then a bag. Air spaces allow sublimation and freezer burn.
4. **Freeze fast.** Put it in the coldest part of the freezer, not stacked against other items, so it passes through the fast-staling 0–10 °C zone quickly. Large loaves pass through slowly; halves freeze better.
5. **Use within 1–3 months** for best quality (safe longer; quality declines).
6. **Reheat, do not just thaw.** Slices go straight into the toaster. A whole or half loaf: thaw in its wrapping at room temperature 1–3 h (so condensation forms on the bag, not the bread), then refresh at 180–200 °C for 5–15 minutes. Or bake from frozen at 180 °C for 20–30 minutes until the centre is above 60 °C. Thawing slowly without reheating leaves the bread in the fast-staling zone for hours.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Bakeries that sell lean bread bake it the same day and discount or repurpose it after 24 h (croutons, bread pudding, breadcrumbs). Longer shelf life is engineered into soft breads with fat, sugar, pre-gelatinized starch (tangzhong, [06.2](stage-1/module-06/lesson-02.md)), emulsifiers and anti-staling enzymes, not by changing storage.

</div>

2. **Run [X1-12 Staling](experiments/X1-12-staling.md).** One loaf, slices stored at room temperature, in the refrigerator and in the freezer, with an unwrapped control; evaluated on days 1, 2 and 4; then a stale slice reheated to show reversibility. It needs about 20–30 minutes a day for five days.
3. Store your next lean loaf half in paper and half in plastic overnight and compare crust and crumb the next morning.

## What goes wrong

- **Gummy band under the crust or through the centre.** Cut too hot, or underbaked. See [Gummy crumb](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=gummy-crumb).
- **Crisp crust goes soft within hours.** Bagged warm, plastic storage, humid air, or thin underbaked crust. See [Crust goes soft after cooling](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=crust-goes-soft-after-cooling).
- **Bread stale by the next day.** Refrigerated, lean formula, underbaked or overbaked. See [Bread stales fast](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=bread-stales-fast).
- **Base soggy.** Cooled on a solid surface or in the pan. Always demould pan loaves immediately and cool on a rack.

## Check yourself

1. A friend keeps bread in the refrigerator "so it stays fresh". Explain what is happening to the bread and what they should do instead.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 2–5 °C amylopectin retrogrades several times faster than at room temperature, so the bread firms and tastes dry sooner even though it is not losing water. The refrigerator only helps against mould. Store lean bread for 1–2 days at room temperature (paper or bread box, cut face down), and freeze anything that will not be eaten in that time; reheat to refresh.

</details>

2. A slice that is firm and crumbly on day 3 becomes soft after 2 minutes in a toaster, then hardens again 20 minutes later. Explain both changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Heating above about 50–60 °C melts the amylopectin crystals formed during staling, so the starch re-hydrates and the crumb softens. On cooling it re-crystallizes, and faster than the first time because nuclei remain and some water has been lost, so it firms again within minutes to hours.

</details>

3. Why is "bread goes stale because it dries out" wrong, and how would you prove it with a scale?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Staling is starch crystallization that binds water inside the starch, not water loss. Weigh a sealed slice on day 0 and day 3: its weight barely changes (a fraction of a percent to perhaps 1–2 %), yet it is clearly firmer. An unwrapped slice loses far more weight: that is drying, a different process.

</details>

4. You bake a lean loaf at 18:00 for breakfast. It must have a crisp crust at 08:00. Plan the evening and morning.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cool fully on a rack (about 2 h). Store overnight in a paper or linen bag or cut face down on a board, not in plastic. In the morning, re-crisp at 200 °C for 5–8 min; let it stand 10 min before slicing. Alternatively, freeze once cool and refresh from thawed in the morning.

</details>

5. Staling and mould need different storage conditions. State the conflict and how freezing resolves it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sealed, humid storage slows drying but favours mould; refrigeration slows mould but maximizes staling. Freezing at −18 °C halts both retrogradation and microbial growth, provided the bread is wrapped tightly to prevent freezer burn and reheated after thawing.

</details>
