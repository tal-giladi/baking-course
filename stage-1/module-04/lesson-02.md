## Why this lesson exists

In the 40 minutes a loaf spends in the oven, a wet dough foam becomes a dry-crusted, set sponge. Everything you controlled in M02 and M03 (gluten strength, gas, sugars left by fermentation) is cashed in here, and most "the bread is dense" or "the crust is pale" problems are decided in these minutes. If you know what happens at each temperature, you can read a finished loaf backwards and tell which stage went wrong.

## You will be able to

- Describe the sequence of events inside a loaf from loading to the end of the bake, with the temperature at which each starts.
- Estimate the gas expansion available from heating alone and explain why real oven spring is larger.
- Explain why the crumb never exceeds about 100 °C while the crust reaches 150 °C or more, and what that means for colour.
- Decide doneness from internal temperature, bake loss, sound and colour, and know which of these can mislead.
- Predict how fermentation length, sugar level and oven temperature change crust colour.

## The loaf as a moving temperature front

Picture a thermometer in the centre and one just under the crust of a 750 g boule baked at 230 °C with steam. The surface passes 60 °C within a few minutes; the centre may take 15–20 minutes to get there. So every event below happens first at the outside and moves inward as a front. At any moment the outer centimetre may be setting while the centre is still expanding. That is why oven spring is limited by how fast the outside sets, and why a loaf that sets too fast on the outside (too hot, no steam) is small.

| Temperature in the dough | Event | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Loading → ~40 °C | Yeast accelerates; gas production at its fastest | Loaf starts to swell within the first minutes |
| ~40–60 °C | Gas expands; CO₂ comes out of solution; yeast slows, dies ~50–60 °C | Main oven spring; score opens |
| ~55–80 °C | Amylases most active on gelatinizing starch, then inactivated | Dextrins and sugars for colour and crumb softness |
| ~60–75 °C | Starch gelatinizes (swells, absorbs water from gluten) | Crumb begins to set; dough goes from stretchy to spongy |
| ~70–90 °C | Gluten proteins firm and cross-link | Structure fixed; oven spring stops |
| ~78 °C+ | Ethanol vaporizes; water vapour pressure climbs | Final increment of expansion |
| Centre 96–99 °C | Crumb fully set; water still present, so temperature plateaus | Doneness range for lean bread |
| Surface >100 °C (once dry) | Crust forms | Crust thickens and hardens |
| Surface ~140 °C+ | Maillard browning becomes rapid | Golden → brown |
| Surface ~160 °C+ | Sucrose caramelization (fructose from ~110 °C) | Deeper brown, bitter-sweet notes |

The temperatures are ranges because they depend on water content, sugar, salt and heating rate; they are standard values from cereal science texts ([Delcour], [Cauvain-TB]) and are good to predict with, not to measure to the degree.

## Phase 1: oven spring

Oven spring is the rapid rise in the first 8–12 minutes, typically adding 20–40 % to the volume of a well-proofed lean loaf (more for underproofed dough, less for overproofed). Four things drive it.

### 1. Gas expansion with temperature (Charles's law)

At constant pressure, gas volume is proportional to absolute temperature. A bubble loaded at 25 °C (298 K) and heated to 100 °C (373 K) grows by 373/298 = **1.25**, a 25 % increase. From a refrigerator-retarded dough at 5 °C (278 K) the factor is 373/278 = 1.34. This is the baseline; the actual spring is larger because gas is also *added*.

### 2. CO₂ coming out of solution

Much of the CO₂ yeast produced during fermentation is dissolved in the dough water, not in bubbles. Gas solubility falls as temperature rises, so the dissolved CO₂ comes out into existing bubbles as the dough heats. No new bubbles form in the oven; every cell you see in the crumb was nucleated during mixing and expanded during fermentation ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)).

### 3. Ethanol and water vaporizing

Fermentation leaves ethanol dissolved in the dough water. Ethanol boils at 78 °C; in dilute solution it vaporizes over a range and adds gas volume. More important is water: the gas in every bubble is saturated with water vapour, and water's vapour pressure roughly doubles for every 15–20 °C in this range. As the crumb approaches 100 °C, water vapour becomes a large share of the gas inside the cells.

### 4. The final burst of yeast activity

Yeast ferments faster as it warms, up to about 35–40 °C, so the first minutes in the oven add fresh CO₂. Activity then falls and the yeast is killed by about 50–60 °C.

### What stops the spring

Expansion continues as long as the cell walls can stretch and the surface can stretch. It stops when the outer layer dries and sets (the crust) and when the crumb sets (starch gelatinization and protein firming). Steam delays the first. Too-hot ovens, dry ovens and fan ovens speed it. A score is a designed weak point so that expansion goes where you choose instead of tearing the side.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Oven spring is a race between gas expansion inside and setting outside. Everything that keeps the surface soft longer (steam, correct loading temperature, a good score) or gives the inside more gas or stronger walls (correct proof, good gluten) makes a bigger loaf.

</div>

## Phase 2: setting the crumb

### Starch gelatinization

Wheat starch granules, heated in water, absorb it, swell, lose their ordered crystalline structure and leak some amylose. In a dilute starch–water slurry wheat starch begins to gelatinize in the 50s °C; in bread dough, where water is limited, it happens mainly between about **60 and 75 °C** and continues as temperature climbs ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)). Gelatinization **needs water**: the swelling starch pulls it from the gluten, which is one reason the crumb goes from stretchy to spongy. A lean dough at 65–70 % hydration has only enough water to partly gelatinize its starch; that is normal, and it is why bread crumb is not a pudding.

While starch gelatinizes, amylase enzymes attack the swollen granules far faster than they could attack intact ones, producing dextrins and sugars. Cereal α-amylase is inactivated around 75–80 °C. A flour with too much amylase activity (sprouted wheat) makes a sticky, gummy crumb; one with too little gives a dry crumb and a pale crust. M05 and the enzymes unit ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) develop this.

### Protein set

Gluten proteins firm and cross-link between roughly 70 and 90 °C, turning the extensible network into a rigid one. Once the starch has gelatinized and the protein has set, the foam is fixed: cells can no longer grow, and any gas pressure beyond that point opens the cell walls into the open, continuous sponge that allows steam to escape. That opening is why a loaf does not collapse as it cools.

### Why the centre stops at about 98 °C

As long as liquid water is present, the crumb cannot exceed the boiling point of water (100 °C at sea level, lower at altitude). In practice a probe in the centre reads 96–99 °C at the end of a lean bake, because the centre is the last point to heat and water is evaporating from it. Extra energy goes into evaporating water rather than raising temperature. The centre climbs, then **plateaus**. Internal temperature therefore tells you the crumb has set, but not how much water has been driven off or how thick the crust is. That is why doneness needs more than one measurement (below, and in [X1-11](experiments/X1-11-bake-time.md)).

## Phase 3: crust and colour

### Crust formation

The surface is the only place that can dry out. Once steam is removed and the surface water evaporates, its temperature can climb past 100 °C toward oven temperature. The dried outer layer (a few millimetres) becomes the crust. Longer baking pushes the drying front inward and thickens the crust; a hotter oven browns the surface faster, before the drying front has moved far, giving a thinner, darker crust.

### Maillard browning

The Maillard reaction is a cascade between reducing sugars (glucose, maltose, fructose) and amino groups of proteins and amino acids, producing brown pigments (melanoidins) and hundreds of aroma compounds: the roasted, malty, nutty crust smell ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)). It runs slowly at lower temperatures and in wet conditions, and becomes rapid on a dry surface above about **140 °C**. That is why the crumb (never above 100 °C, always wet) stays pale while the crust browns.

### Caramelization

Sugars heated alone decompose and brown. Sucrose caramelizes from about **160 °C (320 °F)**; fructose starts much lower, around 110 °C (230 °F). In lean bread there is little free sugar, so caramelization contributes less than Maillard browning; in enriched and sugared doughs (M06, M07) it becomes significant.

### What moves crust colour

| Variable | Direction | Effect on colour | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fermentation length | Longer / overproofed | Paler | Yeast consumed the free sugars |
| Cold retard (8–16 h at 3–5 °C) | Added | Darker, often small blisters | Enzymes keep making sugars while yeast is slow |
| Sugar, milk, egg in formula | More | Darker, faster | More reducing sugars and amino groups (lactose, milk protein) |
| Malt (diastatic or non-diastatic) | Added | Darker | More sugars or enzymes to make them |
| Oven temperature | Higher | Darker, sooner, thinner crust | Surface passes 140 °C earlier |
| Steam | Removed late | Paler, glossier | Surface stays wet and cool longer |
| Surface pH | Higher (alkaline, e.g. pretzel dip) | Much darker | Maillard is faster at higher pH |

<div class="callout warn">

**Warning:** Dark does not mean done. A dough with sugar or milk, or a hot oven, can colour the crust fully while the centre is still at 85 °C. Always check internal temperature on a new formula or a new oven.

</div>

## Doneness: four measurements

| Measure | How | Target | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Internal temperature** | Probe thermometer into the centre through the base or side, reading the lowest point | Lean bread **96–99 °C**; enriched bread (milk, butter, eggs, sugar) **88–93 °C** | Confirms set crumb; says nothing about crust or moisture |
| **Bake loss** | (dough weight − bread weight) ÷ dough weight × 100, weighed after cooling | Lean hearth loaves **~10–20 %**: larger for small pieces and long bakes; pan loaves at the lower end | Needs dough weight recorded before baking |
| **Sound** | Tap the base: hollow, drum-like | Hollow | Crude; a small roll sounds hollow early |
| **Crust colour** | Compare with a colour reference photo or card | Your target shade, even, including the sides and base | Colour depends on sugar and oven, not doneness |

Enriched doughs finish lower because fat, sugar and egg set the structure earlier, and taking them to 98 °C dries them. The next module ([06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md)) uses these targets.

**Worked example.** A boule weighs 850 g as dough. After baking and cooling it weighs 735 g. Bake loss = (850 − 735) / 850 × 100 = 13.5 %. Within range for a well-baked 850 g loaf. The same dough baked as 100 g rolls might lose 16–20 %, because the surface-to-volume ratio is higher.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Bakeries set bake loss as a specification. If you must sell a 750 g loaf and your process loses 13 %, you scale 750 / 0.87 = 862 g of dough. The same number, tracked day to day, is an early warning of oven drift. You met the arithmetic in [01.4](stage-1/module-01/lesson-04.md).

</div>

## On the bench

1. **Run [X1-11 Bake time](experiments/X1-11-bake-time.md).** Five identical pieces pulled at staggered times, each measured for internal temperature, bake loss, crust colour and crumb. This experiment shows the plateau and the gap between "set" and "baked" better than any explanation.
2. **Bake [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md) with a probe.** If you have a leave-in probe thermometer, insert it into the centre through the base after 15 minutes (with the loaf briefly out of the oven) and log temperature every 2 minutes. Plot it. You will see the curve flatten near 97–98 °C.
3. **Watch a pita puff** ([R1-03](recipes/R1-03-pita-flatbreads.md)) through the oven window. It compresses this whole lesson into 90 seconds: surface set, interior water flashes to steam, layers separate, the skin browns in spots where it touched the hot surface.
4. Photograph each crust in daylight next to a white card, and start a colour reference sheet you will use for the rest of the course.

## What goes wrong

- **Pale crust even after a long bake.** Overproofed dough (sugars gone), oven too cool, or steam left in. See [Pale crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=pale-crust).
- **Dark crust, raw centre.** Oven too hot for the loaf size, or a sugared dough judged by colour. See [Raw centre with dark crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=raw-centre-with-dark-crust).
- **Gummy crumb.** Underbaked (centre below 96 °C), cut too hot, or excess amylase. See [Gummy crumb](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=gummy-crumb).
- **Loaf bursts at the side instead of the score.** Crust set before the loaf finished expanding: not enough steam, underproofing, shallow score. See [Loaf cracks in the wrong places](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=loaf-cracks-in-the-wrong-places).

## Check yourself

1. A baguette dough is retarded overnight at 4 °C and loaded straight from the refrigerator. By what factor does its gas expand from heating alone as it reaches 100 °C? Why is the real spring larger?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

373 K / 277 K ≈ 1.35, so about 35 % from Charles's law. Real spring is larger because gas is added: dissolved CO₂ comes out of solution, yeast produces more CO₂ until about 50–60 °C, and ethanol and especially water vapour add to the gas in each cell.

</details>

2. Your probe reads 98 °C after 25 minutes and still 98 °C at 40 minutes. What changed in those 15 minutes?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The centre is on the evaporation plateau; the energy went into driving off water and extending the crust. Between 25 and 40 minutes bake loss increased, the crust thickened and darkened, and the crumb became drier and less gummy. Internal temperature cannot show this; weight and crust inspection can.

</details>

3. Two identical loaves: one proofed 60 minutes, one 150 minutes at the same temperature. Same bake. Which crust is paler, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The 150-minute (overproofed) loaf. Yeast consumed more of the free fermentable sugars, leaving fewer reducing sugars for Maillard browning. It will probably also be flatter and may show a paler, dull crust even with steam.

</details>

4. A dough weighs 920 g before baking and 790 g after cooling. Calculate the bake loss and say whether it is plausible for a lean boule.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(920 − 790) / 920 × 100 = 14.1 %. Plausible: inside the ~10–20 % range for lean hearth bread of that size.

</details>

5. Why is the crust brown but the crumb, which also contains sugars and protein, pale?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The crumb stays wet and cannot rise above about 100 °C, where Maillard browning is slow. Only the dried surface can exceed 140 °C, where browning becomes rapid.

</details>
