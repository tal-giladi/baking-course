## Why this lesson exists

In M03 you treated the oven as a box set to a number. It is not. A home oven is a small, leaky, cycling heat source whose air is a poor conductor, and the number on the dial describes only one of the three ways heat actually reaches your dough. Two loaves from the same dough, baked at the same dial setting, can come out 20 % different in volume and a shade apart in colour depending on what they sat on and whether there was steam. This lesson gives you the model that lets you decide those things on purpose.

## You will be able to

- Name the three heat-transfer modes in a home oven and say which one dominates the bottom, sides and top of a loaf.
- Choose between a baking stone, a steel and a covered cast-iron pot using heat capacity and conductivity, and preheat each for the right time.
- Adjust temperature and time for a fan (convection) oven and explain why the fan is a problem for the first minutes of a bread bake.
- Set up a home steaming method safely and explain, with numbers, why steam increases oven spring and gloss.
- Read your oven's thermostat cycling and plan loading around it.

## Three ways heat moves, and where each one acts

Every bake is a heat-transfer problem: you are moving energy from hot metal and hot air into a cold, wet, gas-filled foam until its centre reaches about 98 °C and its surface dries and browns. [SC-10](stage-1/science/sc-10-heat-transfer.md) covers the physics; here is how it maps onto a loaf.

| Mode | What carries the energy | Where it acts on a loaf | What controls it at home |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | Direct contact, molecule to molecule | The base, through the stone, steel, tray or pot; then from surface to centre through the dough itself | Material and thickness of what the dough sits on; preheat; dough's own low conductivity |
| **Convection** | Moving fluid (air, steam) | Sides and top; the whole surface in a fan oven | Fan on/off, air speed, how crowded the oven is |
| **Radiation** | Infrared emitted by any hot surface | Top (from the roof and upper element) and sides (from walls); inside a covered pot, from the pot walls | Wall and element temperature, distance, colour of the surface receiving it (dark absorbs more) |

Three points matter on the bench:

1. **Air is a bad heat carrier.** Still air at 230 °C transfers heat slowly; you can put your hand in a hot oven for a second but not on the rack. That is why the surface a loaf sits on dominates bottom heat, and why a fan (which thins the insulating boundary layer of air around the loaf) speeds everything up.
2. **Radiation is the big hidden term.** In a domestic oven a large share of the heat reaching the top crust is radiated from the roof and walls, not carried by the air. A loaf near the roof browns faster than the air thermometer predicts; a loaf on a dark tray browns underneath faster than one on a shiny one.
3. **Inside the dough, heat moves only by slow conduction** (and some steam movement through the crumb). Dough is mostly water and gas, a poor conductor. The centre of a 750 g boule lags the surface by tens of minutes. This lag is what sets bake time for a given size, not the oven temperature alone.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The dial controls air temperature. The loaf responds to the sum of conduction from below, radiation from the walls and roof, and convection from the air. Changing what the loaf sits on or whether there is steam changes the bake as much as changing the dial by 20 °C.

</div>

## Thermal mass: stone, steel, cast iron

When you load a cold dough onto a hot surface, the surface gives up heat to the dough and cools locally. What you want at loading is a surface that can deliver a lot of heat quickly without collapsing in temperature. Two properties decide this:

- **Heat capacity** (how much energy is stored per degree): mass × specific heat. A thick, heavy surface stores more.
- **Conductivity** (how fast stored energy reaches the contact face): steel and iron conduct roughly 15–25 times faster than ceramic stone.

| Surface (typical home size) | Approx. density (kg/m³) | Specific heat (kJ/kg·K) | Conductivity (W/m·K) | Behaviour at loading | Preheat at max temperature |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cordierite / ceramic stone, 15–20 mm | ~2,000–2,500 | ~0.8–0.9 | ~2–3 | Moderate, even bottom heat; surface cools under the loaf and recovers slowly | **45–60 min** |
| Steel plate, 6–10 mm | ~7,850 | ~0.5 | ~45–50 | Fast, aggressive bottom heat; best for pizza and pita; can scorch bread bases at high settings | 45–60 min |
| Cast-iron pot (Dutch oven), 3–5 mm walls | ~7,200 | ~0.46 | ~50 | Heat from base and walls plus trapped steam; very forgiving | 30–45 min |
| Thin aluminium or steel tray | — | — | high | Little stored heat; bottom follows air temperature; gentlest | 0–10 min (or none) |

The figures are generic material properties and vary by product; use them for ranking, not prediction. The useful derived quantity is **thermal effusivity** (√(k·ρ·c)), which describes how hard a surface "pushes" heat into something touching it. Steel's is roughly six times that of ceramic stone. That is why a pita puffs in about a minute on a steel and in about two on a stone at the same setting, and why a loaf on a steel at 260 °C can have a black base while the top is still pale.

**Preheat is not optional.** The oven's "ready" light switches off when the *air* reaches set point, typically after 10–20 minutes. A 15 mm stone is still well below air temperature at that point; it needs 45–60 minutes at full temperature to saturate. An infrared thermometer pointed at the stone surface (measured in [X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)) tells you when it is ready: within about 10 °C of the set temperature.

**Placement.** For bread, put the stone or steel on a rack in the lower third, leaving at least 10 cm above the loaf. Too close to the roof and radiation burns the top; directly on the oven floor and the bottom element (in electric ovens) overheats the base.

## The oven itself: thermostat cycling and convection

### Thermostat cycling

A domestic thermostat is an on/off controller. The element runs until the sensor reads slightly above set point, switches off, and switches back on when the air drops below it. Swings of ±5–15 °C around set point are common; some ovens are worse. Your oven map from [X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md) should have recorded this. Consequences:

- The dial number is an average, not the temperature at any given moment. An oven reading 230 °C may spend minutes at 215 °C and minutes at 245 °C.
- **Opening the door dumps heat.** Air temperature can fall 30–50 °C in the 20–30 seconds it takes to load a loaf and add steam. Walls, stone and roof hold their heat, so the recovery is quick, but in a lightly built oven with no stone it can take 5–10 minutes. This is one reason bakers preheat 20–30 °C above the baking temperature and turn it down after loading.
- Some ovens run the top (grill/broil) element during preheat or recovery. A loaf loaded during that phase gets a burst of radiant top heat. If your tops scorch in the first 10 minutes, check what your elements are doing.

### Convection (fan) ovens

A fan moves air across the loaf, thinning the boundary layer and raising the surface heat-transfer coefficient severalfold compared with still air. Standard professional practice when converting a still-oven formula to a fan oven:

| Adjustment | Typical value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lower set temperature | **15–20 °C** | Same heat flux at lower air temperature |
| Or keep temperature, shorten time | 10–15 % less time (check colour and internal temperature) | Faster heat delivery |
| Expect | Faster surface drying, earlier colour, more even browning across trays | Moving air carries water vapour away |

For bread that faster drying is a problem at the start. You want the surface moist and extensible for the first 10–15 minutes (next section), and a fan sweeps away the steam you added and often vents it outside. Options: bake the first phase with the fan off, use a covered pot for the steamed phase, or accept a slightly smaller loaf. After the steam phase, turning the fan on gives an even, well-coloured crust. For enriched breads, cookies and cakes the fan is often an advantage; M06 and M10 return to this.

## Steam: what it does and why it matters

### The mechanism

When you load a proofed loaf, its surface is at dough temperature: about 25 °C straight from proof, or around 5 °C if it was retarded in the refrigerator. In a steam-filled oven, water vapour touching that cold surface **condenses** on it. Condensation releases the latent heat of vaporization, about 2,260 kJ per kg of water. For comparison, heating water by 1 °C takes 4.18 kJ/kg, so every gram of steam that condenses on the loaf delivers as much energy as cooling about 5 g of water from 100 °C to 0 °C. That is a large, fast, perfectly even dose of heat, delivered exactly where the loaf is coldest ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)).

The condensed film does three things:

1. **Keeps the skin wet and extensible.** A dry oven dehydrates the surface within a few minutes; the skin stiffens and caps the loaf before the interior has finished expanding. A wet skin stretches, so the loaf keeps rising and the score opens smoothly. This is the main reason steamed loaves are larger. Oven spring (04.2) happens mostly in the first 8–12 minutes, and that is the window steam has to cover.
2. **Gelatinizes surface starch.** The hot, wet surface starch swells and gelatinizes (it needs water to do so; dry surfaces cannot). When the steam is removed and the surface dries, that gelatinized film dries into a thin, glassy layer: the **gloss** on a well-baked baguette and the thin, crackly crust. A dry-baked loaf has a dull, floury, thicker crust.
3. **Delays crust formation, then allows a better one.** Because the surface stays below 100 °C while wet, browning is postponed. Once steam is vented, the surface dries, passes 100 °C and browns evenly.

Steam has to be **removed** after about 10–20 minutes (vent the door briefly, remove the water tray, or take the lid off the pot). Steam left in for the whole bake gives a pale, soft, leathery crust that never dries properly.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Condensation only happens while the loaf surface is colder than the dew point of the oven atmosphere. A heavily humidified oven has a high dew point, so condensation continues longer; a trickle of steam in a large, vented oven condenses for only seconds. That is why the *amount* and *containment* of steam matter more than the method used to make it.

</div>

### Home steaming methods compared

| Method | How | Effectiveness | Main risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Covered pot (Dutch oven)** | Preheat a lidded cast-iron pot 30–45 min; load dough on a parchment sling; lid on for 20 min, off for the rest | Excellent: the dough's own evaporating water saturates a small closed volume | 250 °C pot and lid; handle only with dry, thick mitts; lower the loaf with the sling, never your hands |
| **Tray of lava rocks, steel chain or heavy cast-iron pan on the lowest rack** | Preheat with the oven; pour 100–200 g **boiling** water in right after loading; close door immediately | Good if the oven holds steam (electric better than gas) | Steam burst scalds forearms and face; stand to the side, use a long-spouted kettle and long mitts |
| **Rolled wet towel in a loaf pan** | Soaked towel in a preheated pan provides steady steam for 15–20 min | Moderate, gentle | Low risk; towel scorches if it dries out |
| **Ice cubes in a preheated pan** | 1–2 handfuls thrown onto the pan | Weak: the ice must first melt and warm to 100 °C, absorbing heat from the oven when you want it hot | Cold ice on hot enamel or glass can cause thermal-shock cracking |
| **Spray bottle** | Mist the loaf and oven walls | Weak: tiny water mass, door open longer | Water on a hot oven light or door glass can shatter it |
| **Inverted pan / roasting pan cover** | Cover the loaf on a stone with a large deep roasting pan for the first 15–20 min | Good, similar to a pot | Handling a hot, awkward cover |

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Steam at 100 °C scalds faster than dry air at 230 °C because it condenses on your skin and delivers its latent heat. When you open a steamed oven or lift a pot lid, open away from you and step back for two seconds. Never pour water onto the oven floor, element or door glass; cold water can crack oven glass and damage electronics in the floor of electric ovens. Do not use ice or cold water on enamelled or glass pans. Keep children and pets away during loading.

</div>

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Deck ovens inject steam from a boiler or cascade system for a set number of seconds after loading, then open a damper to vent it. Home bakers reproduce this with a covered pot (steam from the dough itself) or a preheated steam tray. For multiple loaves or baguettes that do not fit a pot, the tray-plus-boiling-water method with a stone is the standard home substitute.

</div>

### The covered pot as a mini-oven

A lidded cast-iron pot solves three home-oven problems at once. It traps the steam the loaf itself releases (a 750 g loaf loses tens of grams of water in the first 20 minutes, enough to saturate a 4–5 L pot), so no added water is needed. Its walls and lid radiate heat from all sides at nearly the same temperature, so the side and top heating is even. Its heavy base conducts heat into the bottom like a stone. The costs: one loaf per pot, a fixed shape, and hot handling. A typical schedule for a 750–900 g boule, standard professional practice for home baking:

| Phase | Setting | Time | Endpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preheat pot | 250 °C (480 °F) | 30–45 min | Pot at oven temperature |
| Covered | 250 °C, lid on | 20 min | Loaf fully expanded, pale, surface set |
| Uncovered | 230 °C (445 °F), lid off | 20–25 min | Deep golden-brown crust, internal 96–99 °C |

If bases burn in a pot, put a tray or a second rack directly below it to shield it from the bottom element, or put a sheet of parchment plus a sprinkling of semolina under the loaf.

## On the bench

1. **Map before you bake.** Check that you have your oven's cycling and hot spots from [X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md). If not, run it now; everything in this module depends on it.
2. **Run [X1-10 Steam](experiments/X1-10-steam.md).** Three pieces of one lean dough: no steam, tray steam, covered pot. This is the experiment that makes this lesson real.
3. **Bake [R1-03 Pita](recipes/R1-03-pita-flatbreads.md) on a stone or steel.** Pita is heat transfer made visible: the pocket forms only if the surface sets and the interior water flashes to steam fast enough. Bake one on the stone and one in a dry skillet and compare puff time.
4. **Bake [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md) again** using the steaming method you intend to keep, and record loaf height and width against your M03 loaves.

Watch for: how quickly the score opens after loading (with steam it opens in the first 5–8 minutes; without, it often tears on one side), the sheen of the crust, and colour of the base versus the top.

## What goes wrong

- **Base scorched, top pale.** Too much bottom heat relative to top: a steel at maximum, a stone on the lowest rack of an electric oven, or a pot on the floor. See [Burnt crust or burnt bottom](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=burnt-crust-or-burnt-bottom) and [Uneven browning](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=uneven-browning).
- **Small loaf, dull thick crust, score torn at the side.** No or too little steam, or steam lost to a fan or vented oven. See [Thick, hard crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=thick-hard-crust).
- **Pale, soft, leathery crust.** Steam left in too long, or oven too cool after loading. See [Soft, leathery crust](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=soft-leathery-crust).
- **Loaf flat after loading onto an underheated stone.** Preheat time counted from switch-on is not stone temperature. Measure it.

## Check yourself

1. Your oven's ready light comes on after 15 minutes at 250 °C. You load a boule onto the stone immediately. Predict the result and explain it.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The air is at set point but a 15–20 mm ceramic stone needs 45–60 minutes to saturate. The stone surface may be 60–100 °C below set point and cools further under the cold dough. Expect weak bottom heat, a paler base, less oven spring and a longer bake. Measure the stone with an IR thermometer and load when it is within about 10 °C of set point.

</details>

2. Why does a pita puff faster on a 6 mm steel than on a 20 mm stone at the same oven setting, even though the stone is heavier?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

What matters at the instant of contact is how fast the surface can push heat into the dough (effusivity, √(kρc)), not just stored energy. Steel conducts roughly 15–25 times better than ceramic and has higher volumetric heat capacity, so its contact face stays hot and delivers heat several times faster. The pita's base sets and its interior water flashes to steam sooner.

</details>

3. Using latent heat, explain why 1 g of condensing steam heats the dough surface more than 1 g of 100 °C water would.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water at 100 °C cooling to a 25 °C surface gives up about 4.18 × 75 ≈ 314 J. Steam condensing gives up about 2,260 J of latent heat first, then the same 314 J as the condensate cools: roughly eight times more energy per gram, delivered as a thin, even film.

</details>

4. You have a fan oven. The formula says 230 °C still oven, 40 min, steam for 15 min. What do you set, and when?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

For the steamed phase, turn the fan off (or use a covered pot) at about 230 °C so steam is not swept away and the surface stays wet. After venting at 15 min, turn the fan on and drop to about 210–215 °C (15–20 °C lower) for the rest. Judge the end by crust colour and an internal temperature of 96–99 °C rather than the clock, since the fan may shorten the bake.

</details>

5. Name two reasons to remove steam after 15–20 minutes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Oven spring is essentially finished by then, so steam has no more work to do. And a crust cannot dry, exceed 100 °C and brown while it is wet, so leaving steam in produces a pale, soft, leathery crust and delays colour.

</details>
