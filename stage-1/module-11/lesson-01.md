## Why this lesson exists

For ten modules you have mostly been trying to *build* gluten. Short doughs invert the goal: the best tart shell has just enough gluten to be rolled and lined, and not a gram more. Every classic short-dough fault (tough, shrinking, crumbly, greasy) is a failure to control how much gluten forms and where the butter sits. If you understand the three levers (fat coating, water, mixing) you can read any tart dough formula and predict how it will handle and eat before you make it.

## You will be able to

- Explain what "short" means mechanically and why a fat-coated flour particle forms less gluten.
- Read a brisée, sucrée or sablée formula in baker's percentages and predict tenderness, handling and shrinkage from it.
- Choose between the sablage and crémage methods and state what each does to gluten and texture.
- Perform fraisage and explain why it homogenizes a dough without kneading it.
- Account for the hidden water in butter and eggs when judging a dough's real water level.

## What "short" means

"Short" is an old baker's word for crumbly and tender, the opposite of "long" (stretchy, elastic). In a short dough the gluten network exists only as **short, discontinuous strands**, so the baked product breaks cleanly instead of pulling.

Recall from [02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md) that gluten forms when glutenin and gliadin hydrate and are worked, so that the proteins unfold, contact each other and link into a network ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)). Take away either condition, water or work, and the network cannot grow. Short doughs take away both, deliberately.

1. **Fat coats the flour.** Butter is a plastic fat ([08.1](stage-1/module-08/lesson-01.md), [SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)): at 10–18 °C it can be smeared into thin films. Flour particles coated in a film of fat are partly shielded from water. Proteins that cannot hydrate cannot join the network. Where fat also sits *between* hydrated protein strands, it physically interrupts them: the strands stay short.
2. **Water is scarce.** A bread dough contains 60–75 % water on flour. A brisée contains about 20–25 %, a sucrée often has no added water at all beyond the eggs. There is not enough free water to hydrate all the protein even if nothing blocked it.
3. **Work is minimal.** The dough is mixed only until it holds together. Every extra minute of mixing after the water goes in is gluten development you did not want.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** Tenderness in a short dough is decided by the order in which water and fat reach the flour. Fat first means less gluten. Water first, then work, means more.

</div>

## The hidden water: butter and eggs

When you count water in a short dough you must include what the other ingredients bring.

| Ingredient | Water content (approx.) | Other relevant components |
|---|---|---|
| Butter (unsalted, European or US standard) | 15–18 % | 80–82 % fat, ~1–2 % milk solids |
| Whole egg (without shell) | ~75 % | ~12–13 % protein, ~10 % fat |
| Egg yolk | ~50 % | ~16 % protein, ~30 % fat, lecithin |
| Milk | ~87 % | lactose, milk proteins |

**Worked example.** R1-20 brisée uses 250 g flour, 150 g butter, 20 g yolk and 50 g water.
Water from butter: 150 × 0.16 ≈ 24 g. From yolk: 20 × 0.5 = 10 g. Added water: 50 g.
Total ≈ 84 g, or about **34 % of flour**, versus 20 % if you count only the jug.

Most of the butter's water stays trapped in its emulsion while the butter is cold and firm. If the butter warms and softens during mixing, that water is released into the flour and the dough becomes wetter, more elastic and more prone to shrink. This is one mechanism behind the rule "keep it cold": it is about gluten as much as about butter.

## The three French short doughs

The French classification is a family, not three fixed recipes. Houses differ, and the boundary between sucrée and sablée in particular is drawn differently by different schools (some use *sablée* for any sucrée made by the sablage method). The table gives the working ranges used in standard professional practice ([Gisslen], [CIA-BP], [Friberg]).

| | Pâte brisée | Pâte sucrée | Pâte sablée (e.g., sablé breton) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| Butter | 50–60 (to ~75 for rich versions) | 50–60 | 65–85 |
| Sugar | 0–5 (granulated or none) | 35–40 (icing sugar) | 40–70 |
| Egg | 0–10 yolk or egg (optional) | ~20 whole egg (or yolks) | 20–40, usually yolks |
| Added water | 20–25 | 0–5 | 0 |
| Salt | 1.5–2 | 0.5–1 | 1–2 (often salted butter) |
| Other | — | Almond flour 0–15 | Baking powder ~3–4 in sablé breton |
| Texture | Crisp, flaky-tender, neutral | Crisp, fine, snaps cleanly | Very tender, sandy, crumbles |
| Handling | Easy, forgiving | Moderate; softens fast | Hard to roll thin; often baked in a ring as a disc |
| Typical use | Quiche, savoury tarts, rustic fruit tarts | Fruit, lemon, chocolate tarts, tartlets | Biscuit bases, sablé cookies, tart bases baked in rings |

Read the columns as a gradient: moving right, **sugar and fat rise, water falls**. Each step makes the dough more tender and more fragile and makes gluten less important to the structure. By the time you reach a sablé breton, the "structure" is mostly egg-yolk protein, starch and sugar, and the dough is too fragile to line a fluted pan; it is pressed or piped into a ring.

### Pâte brisée

Brisée ("broken") is the lean member: flour, butter, salt, water, sometimes a yolk. Because it depends on water to bind, it has the most gluten of the three and must be handled with that in mind: minimal water, minimal mixing, a proper rest. Its neutral flavour suits quiche and savoury tarts; a spoon of sugar makes it a base for rustic fruit tarts. Brisée can be made so the butter stays in small visible pieces (flakier, closer to pie dough) or rubbed in finely (more even, more tender). Recipe: [R1-20](recipes/R1-20-pate-brisee.md).

### Pâte sucrée

Sucrée is the workhorse of French pâtisserie. The icing sugar (confectioners' sugar) dissolves quickly in the small amount of egg water and gives a fine, even crumb with a clean snap; granulated sugar in the same dough leaves speckles and spreads more. Almond flour adds flavour and tenderness (it is fat and protein with no gluten-forming protein) and helps the shell resist moisture a little. Recipe: [R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md).

### Pâte sablée

*Sablé* means sandy. Higher fat and sugar go further in interrupting gluten; the result melts on the tongue. You will meet sablé-type doughs in Stage 2 as bases for entremets and plated desserts. In this module sablée is mainly a reference point: the far end of the tenderness gradient.

## What each ingredient does

| Ingredient | Main jobs in short dough | Push it up and you get |
|---|---|---|
| **Butter** | Coats flour, interrupts gluten (tenderness); flavour; carries water in its emulsion; melts in the oven to leave a crisp, open texture | More tender and fragile; softens faster at the bench; more spread and slump; greasy if dough warms |
| **Sugar** | Tenderizes: it is hygroscopic and competes with gluten proteins for the little water there is, and it delays protein hydration; browning (caramelization and Maillard); crispness (sugar dries to a glassy state on cooling) | More tender, crisper, faster browning, more spread; shell can darken before the base is baked |
| **Egg** | Water that binds; protein that coagulates on baking and gives structure without gluten (why sucrée holds a straight wall); yolk fat and lecithin add richness and help emulsify the butter | More structure and colour, firmer, slightly harder texture; more water means more gluten if overmixed |
| **Water** | Binds the dough; hydrates gluten; turns to steam in the oven | More gluten: tougher, more elastic, more shrinkage; easier to roll without cracking. Too little: crumbly, cracks, falls apart |
| **Salt** | Flavour; mild gluten tightening | Sharper flavour; at these levels, negligible structural effect |
| **Flour** | Starch (bulk, crispness after baking) and protein (gluten potential) | Stronger flour (12–13 % protein) forms gluten faster: use plain/all-purpose (9.5–11.5 %) or pastry flour |

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Sugar tenderizes by two routes. Physically, dissolved sugar ties up water, so less is available to hydrate gluten proteins. Kinetically, a sugar solution raises the temperature at which proteins denature and starch gelatinizes, so in the oven the structure sets later and the dough can spread a little before it firms. This is the same balance you saw in cookies ([07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md)) and in cake formula balance ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)): sugar and fat are tenderizers, flour and egg are builders ([Figoni]).

</div>

## Two mixing methods: sablage and crémage

The same sucrée formula can be made two ways, and the method changes the product. This is the subject of [X1-30](experiments/X1-30-sablage-cremage.md).

### Sablage (rubbing in)

1. Cold butter (about 4–8 °C), in 1 cm cubes, is rubbed into the flour (with salt, sugar and almond flour) by fingertips, a paddle on low speed, or pulses in a food processor.
2. Stop when there are no visible butter lumps and the mixture looks like fine, damp sand. Mixture temperature should stay at or below about 18 °C.
3. The egg (or water) is added and the mass is brought together with minimal mixing, then finished with fraisage.

Because the flour is coated with fat **before** any water arrives, less gluten can form. The result is more tender and sandier, and usually **shrinks less**. The price is a shorter, more fragile dough that cracks if rolled too cold and tears when lifted.

This is the same rubbing-in you used for scones in [08.4](stage-1/module-08/lesson-04.md), taken further: there you left pea-sized butter for lift; here you rub it in completely for tenderness.

### Crémage (creaming)

1. Butter at about 16–18 °C (soft enough to dent easily, still matt, not shiny or greasy) is mixed with the icing sugar on low speed just until smooth. You are not trying to aerate it as for a cake ([10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md)); air makes the shell rise and bubble.
2. Egg is added gradually, at room temperature, so the emulsion does not break.
3. Almond flour, then flour, are added on low speed and mixed only until no dry flour remains.

Crémage gives a smoother, more homogeneous, more cohesive dough that is easier to roll and line. The water in the egg is dispersed in a butter–sugar emulsion rather than coating the flour, and when the flour goes in, some of it meets that water directly. Mixed briefly, this makes little difference. Overmixed after the flour goes in, a creamed dough develops gluten fast and becomes the tougher, more shrink-prone of the two.

| | Sablage | Crémage |
|---|---|---|
| Butter temperature | Cold, 4–8 °C | Soft, 16–18 °C |
| Order | Fat + dry → then liquid | Fat + sugar → egg → dry |
| Gluten potential | Lower | Higher, especially if overmixed |
| Texture | More tender, sandy, crumbly | Finer, more even, firmer snap |
| Handling | Shorter, cracks, fragile when lifted | Smooth, cohesive, easier to line |
| Shrinkage | Usually less | Usually slightly more |
| Main risk | Butter warming during rubbing; dough too dry to hold | Overmixing after flour; over-aeration |

Neither method is "correct". Many professional kitchens use crémage for sucrée because it scales well in a mixer and lines reliably; others prefer sablage for its texture. Choosing on purpose is the skill.

## Fraisage: homogenize without kneading

After the dough has just come together it is rarely uniform: some patches are buttery, some floury. Kneading would fix that and develop gluten. **Fraisage** fixes it without developing gluten: you push small portions of dough away from you with the heel of your hand (or a scraper) in one firm smear across the bench, flattening butter and dough into thin, uniform layers, then gather the smears together with a scraper. One pass, occasionally two, is enough.

It works because each piece of dough is deformed once, in compression and shear against the bench, rather than stretched and folded repeatedly. Every patch ends up evenly mixed, but the gluten strands are never aligned and worked into a network. Endpoint: the gathered dough is uniform in colour, with no white butter streaks and no dry flour, and it just holds together. Flatten it into a disc about 2 cm thick (faster, more even chilling than a ball), wrap it and chill.

<div class="callout warn">

**Warning:** Fraisage is done fast with cool hands on a cool bench. If the butter softens to shiny or greasy, stop, wrap and chill for 20 minutes before continuing. Warm, smeared butter releases its water and the dough toughens.

</div>

## Predicting a dough from its numbers

With these mechanisms you can read a formula like an engineer reads a specification.

| If the formula has… | Expect |
|---|---|
| Water above ~25 % in a brisée | Easier rolling, tougher bite, more shrinkage; needs a long rest |
| Water below ~18 % (brisée) | Crumbly, cracks at the edges, hard to lift; very tender if it holds |
| Butter above ~65 % (sucrée) | Very tender, softens fast at the bench, slumps in blind baking unless frozen first |
| Granulated instead of icing sugar | Coarser, speckled surface; more spread; slightly crisper |
| Almond flour added (10–15 %) | More tender, richer, slightly more fragile; browns a little faster |
| Yolk instead of whole egg | Richer, more tender, deeper colour; less water, so less gluten |
| Bread flour | Tough, elastic, shrinks; wrong flour for short dough |

4. Keep a small offcut of each raw dough, pull it slowly apart, and compare with a piece of bread dough from memory. That short, clean break is what "short" means.

## What goes wrong

- **Tough, chewy pastry.** Too much water, warm butter, or overmixing after the liquid went in. See [Tough pastry](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=tough-pastry).
- **Crumbly dough that will not hold together.** Too little liquid, or butter rubbed in too warm so it coated everything in grease. See [Crumbly pastry that falls apart](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=crumbly-pastry-that-falls-apart).
- **Greasy dough, butter leaking in the oven.** Butter softened or melted during mixing. See [Greasy pastry, butter leaking](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=greasy-pastry-butter-leaking).
- **Shell shrinks.** Mostly a resting and lining problem, covered in [11.2](stage-1/module-11/lesson-02.md), but it starts with water and mixing here. See [Shrinking tart shell](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=shrinking-tart-shell).

## Check yourself

1. A brisée uses 500 g flour, 300 g butter and 110 g water. Estimate the total water as a percentage of flour, including butter.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Butter water ≈ 300 × 0.16 = 48 g. Total ≈ 110 + 48 = 158 g → 158 ÷ 500 = **about 32 %**, versus 22 % counting added water only. Most of the butter water only matters if the butter softens during mixing.

</details>

2. Two sucrée doughs have the same formula. One was made by sablage, the other by crémage and mixed for 3 minutes on medium after the flour went in. Which will shrink more, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The overmixed creamed dough. In sablage the flour is fat-coated before liquid arrives, limiting gluten. In crémage the flour meets the egg water directly, and 3 minutes of work after that develops a gluten network that stores elastic strain in rolling and recoils in the oven.

</details>

3. Why does a sucrée hold a straight wall in a ring even though it has very little gluten?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Its structure comes mainly from egg protein, which coagulates in the oven, plus starch and sugar that set into a rigid, glassy matrix as the shell dries and cools. It does not need a gluten network to stand up, as long as the butter stays firm until those set (hence chilling before baking).

</details>

4. You replace the icing sugar in a sucrée with the same weight of granulated sugar. Predict two changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Coarser, speckled surface because the crystals do not fully dissolve in the small amount of egg water; slightly more spread and a crisper, more crunchy bite. Colour may be patchier.

</details>

5. What does fraisage achieve that kneading also achieves, and what does it avoid?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Both homogenize the dough (butter, flour and liquid evenly distributed). Fraisage does it with one compressive smear per portion, so it does not repeatedly stretch and fold the dough and does not develop a gluten network.

</details>
