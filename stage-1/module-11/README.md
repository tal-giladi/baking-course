# M11 · Short doughs, pies and tarts

## Purpose

Until now most doughs in the course needed gluten. Short doughs need as little as possible: just enough to roll and line, no more. This module teaches you to control that balance with three levers (fat coating the flour, water, and mixing) and with butter temperature at every step. You will make the three French short doughs, turn them into tart shells that keep their shape through resting, lining, chilling and blind baking, and bake two kinds of filled pastry: a flaky double-crust fruit pie thickened with starch, and a pear and almond tart with crème d'amande. The same ideas carry straight into lamination (M15) and finished tarts (M17).

## Objectives

By the end of M11 you can:

1. Explain what makes a dough "short" and predict tenderness, handling and shrinkage from a brisée, sucrée or sablée formula in baker's percentages, including the water carried by butter and eggs.
2. Make pâte sucrée by sablage and by crémage, and choose between them with evidence from your own experiment.
3. Rest, roll to 2.5–3 mm, line a ring or fluted pan and blind bake a shell to par-baked or fully baked, with wall height within 2 mm of the ring.
4. Make a flaky pie dough with controlled butter piece size and explain how steam and fat produce layers.
5. Calculate the starch for a fruit filling from fruit type and weight, and bake to the correct endpoint (thick bubbling in the centre).
6. Make crème d'amande and bake it with fruit in a sucrée shell to a measured centre temperature.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Short dough | A dough with little, discontinuous gluten, so it is tender and breaks cleanly; achieved with fat, little water and minimal mixing. |
| Pâte brisée | Lean French short dough: flour 100, butter 50–60, water 20–25, salt ~2, optional yolk; for quiche and savoury or rustic tarts. |
| Pâte sucrée | Sweet French short dough: flour 100, butter 50–60, icing sugar 35–40, egg ~20, optional almond flour; for fine tarts. |
| Pâte sablée | Very rich, sandy short dough with higher butter and sugar (e.g., sablé breton); too fragile to line pans easily. |
| Sablage | Rubbing cold butter into the dry ingredients before adding liquid; coats flour with fat, giving a more tender, less shrink-prone dough. |
| Crémage | Mixing soft butter with sugar, then egg, then flour; gives a smooth, cohesive dough that is tougher if overmixed. |
| Fraisage | Smearing dough once across the bench with the heel of the hand to homogenize it without developing gluten. |
| Fonçage | Lining a ring or pan with rolled dough, easing it into the corner without stretching. |
| Docking | Pricking the base of a shell so trapped steam and air escape and it does not dome. |
| Blind baking | Baking a shell empty, usually with parchment and weights, before or instead of baking it with a filling. |
| Par-bake / full bake | Par-baked: set and dry but pale, for shells baked again with a filling. Fully baked: evenly golden, for unbaked fillings. |
| Shrinkage | Loss of wall height or diameter in the oven from elastic recovery of stretched gluten while the butter is melted and the structure not yet set. |
| Flaky dough | Pie dough with butter left as flattened pieces that become steam-separated layers. |
| Mealy dough | Pie dough with butter rubbed in fine; tender, few layers, resists soaking; used under wet fillings. |
| Starch gelatinization (in fillings) | Swelling of starch granules in hot fruit juice that thickens it; needs near-boiling temperatures in a sugary filling. |
| Crème d'amande | Almond cream of equal weights butter, sugar, almond flour and eggs, baked in tarts. |
| Frangipane | Crème d'amande combined with crème pâtissière, usually 2 : 1. |
| Egg-wash seal | A thin coat of egg baked onto a shell to slow moisture moving from a filling into the pastry. |

## Time estimate

About **1 week** (week 16 of the suggested schedule): 2.5 h reading; one session for doughs and X1-30 (about 3 h plus an overnight rest); one for shells and X1-32 (about 3 h); one for X1-31 and the pie (dough the day before, then about 3 h plus 4 h cooling); one for the pear and almond tart (about 2.5 h). Total roughly **14–16 hours**.
