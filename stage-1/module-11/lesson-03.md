## Why this lesson exists

The French short doughs of [11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md) aim for an even, fine, tender crumb. A flaky pie crust aims for something else: visible layers that shatter. The difference is entirely where the butter sits. This lesson also introduces the first two *fillings* of the course: a starch-thickened fruit filling (your first practical encounter with starch gelatinization as a thickener, which dominates M12) and crème d'amande, a baked butter–egg–almond cream. Both have failure modes that are decided by numbers you can control.

## You will be able to

- Explain how butter pieces become flaky layers and how piece size controls flakiness versus tenderness.
- Choose flaky or mealy dough for a given use and make each.
- Calculate the starch for a fruit filling from the fruit weight and type, and state the endpoint that proves it has thickened.
- Make crème d'amande, fill and bake an almond tart with fruit, and judge doneness.
- Explain why frangipane is a later variant that depends on pastry cream (M12).

## Flaky pie dough: where the butter sits

A pie dough has roughly the same ingredients as brisée: flour 100, butter 65–75, ice water 25–30, salt about 2, sugar 0–5 %. What changes is the **state of the butter** at the end of mixing.

| | Mealy | Flaky | Very flaky (rough puff style) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Butter piece size | Rubbed fine, like breadcrumbs (≤ 2 mm) | Pea-sized to hazelnut (5–10 mm), flattened into flakes | Walnut-sized (15–25 mm), flattened, plus 1–2 folds |
| Water needed | Less (fat-coated flour absorbs less): ~20–25 % | ~25–30 % | ~28–32 % |
| Texture | Tender, crumbly, fine; few layers | Tender with distinct layers | Pronounced layers, lighter, more shattering; slightly tougher between layers |
| Resists soaking | Best | Moderate | Poorest |
| Use | Bottom crust under wet fillings (custard, pumpkin, juicy fruit) | Top and bottom crusts for fruit pies, hand pies | Top crusts, galettes, decorative lattices |

Many bakers use a **hybrid**: rub half the butter in fine for tenderness and a sealed base, leave the other half in pea-sized flakes for layers.

### How flakes become layers

1. **Mixing.** Cold butter pieces (4–7 °C) are tossed in flour and pressed flat between fingers, or cut in with a pastry blender or a few pulses of a food processor. Each flattened piece is surrounded by floury dough.
2. **Hydration and folding.** Minimal ice water binds the floury parts into dough. Rolling flattens the butter pieces further into thin, discontinuous sheets. One or two letter folds (fold in thirds like a letter, roll out again) stack those sheets into layers, the same principle as puff pastry but crude and irregular.
3. **Oven.** The dough layers between butter sheets begin to set; the butter melts and its water (about 16 %) plus water from the adjacent dough turn to steam. The steam pushes the layers apart while they set, and the melted fat soaks into and fries the dough around it, leaving thin crisp leaves separated by gaps ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)).

For this to work, the butter must **still be a solid piece when the dough enters the oven**. If it softened and smeared into the flour during mixing or rolling, there is no fat barrier to separate layers: the dough becomes mealy (if the fat coated the flour) or tough and greasy (if the fat melted out). That is why every step of pie dough runs cold: ice water at 1–4 °C, dough at or below 15 °C, rests between steps.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Piece size is a trade-off. Large pieces give fewer, thicker fat barriers and more steam in each pocket: high lift, obvious layers. But large pieces coat less flour, so more flour meets water and forms gluten, and the dough needs more water: the crust is flakier but less tender. Fine pieces coat more flour: very tender, but no barriers, so no layers. [X1-31](experiments/X1-31-pie-fat-size.md) measures this directly.

</div>

### Water is the balance point

The ice water in pie dough is added a little at a time, tossing with a fork, until a handful **just holds together when squeezed** and a few dry crumbs remain in the bowl. At 25–30 % of flour this is usually reached; flours vary by a few percent. Too little water: the dough cracks at the edges, falls apart when lifted and may be dry and sandy after baking. Too much: easy to roll but tough, elastic, shrinks, and bakes hard. Add the last 10 % of the water only if needed, and write down what you used.

## Fruit pie fillings: starch as a thickener

Fruit releases a lot of juice as it heats: its cells rupture and sugar draws water out osmotically. A starch thickens that juice by **gelatinization** ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)): granules absorb water, swell many times, leak amylose and turn a thin syrup into a viscous paste that sets further on cooling.

### How much starch?

Fruit juiciness varies widely: by type, ripeness, variety, and whether it was frozen (freezing ruptures cells, so frozen fruit releases more juice). So the amount of thickener is a range, expressed as a percentage of **prepared fruit weight**.

| Fruit | Cornstarch | Tapioca (instant/quick-cooking, ground) | Plain flour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apples, firm pears | 1.5–3 % | 2–4 % | 3–6 % |
| Peaches, apricots, plums | 3–4 % | 4–5 % | 6–8 % |
| Blueberries, cherries, blackberries | 4–5 % | 5–6 % | 8–10 % |
| Frozen berries and cherries | 5–6 % | 6–7 % | 10–12 % |
| Rhubarb, raspberries (very juicy) | 5–6 % | 6–7 % | 10–12 % |

These are standard practical ranges, not laws. Start in the middle and adjust by one step next time based on how the cut pie behaves.

| Thickener | Appearance | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Cornstarch** | Glossy, slightly cloudy | Strong, sets to a clean gel on cooling; must reach a boil to thicken fully; weakened by long boiling in acid; weeps after freezing |
| **Tapioca** | Clear, very glossy | Thickens at a slightly lower temperature, freeze-stable; pearl or coarse tapioca must soak 15 min in the fruit juice or it stays as gelatinous beads |
| **Flour** | Cloudy, matt | About half as effective as cornstarch (only ~70–75 % of flour is starch, and its proteins and lipids interfere); softer set; can taste floury if underbaked |

### The endpoint: bubbling in the centre

Starch in a sugary fruit juice needs a higher temperature to gelatinize than in plain water, because sugar competes for water and raises the gelatinization temperature. For cornstarch in a fruit filling, full thickening needs the filling to reach close to boiling (above about 90–95 °C). The edges of a pie get there quickly; **the centre is last**.

The practical endpoint, used universally: **thick, slow bubbles rising in the centre vent**, not just at the edges. Thin, fast, watery bubbling at the edge means the centre is still below temperature. A pie pulled at "golden crust" before the centre bubbles will be runny when cut, however much starch it contains. If the crust is dark before the centre bubbles, tent it with foil and continue.

The gel sets further as it cools (amylose chains re-associate, [SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)). A fruit pie cut hot will run even if perfectly baked. Cool at least **3–4 hours** to near room temperature.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Acid weakens cornstarch somewhat: at the low pH of fruit (about 3–3.5 for berries and cherries), prolonged heating partly hydrolyses the starch chains and reduces thickening. At pie levels (a tablespoon of lemon juice for a 900 g filling) the effect is modest, which is why the ranges above already include it; for very acidic fruit (rhubarb, sour cherries) use the top of the range. A more reliable alternative for very juicy fruit is to **pre-cook**: macerate the fruit with the sugar for 30–60 minutes, drain the juice, boil it with the starch until thick and clear, then fold it back into the fruit. The filling is thickened before it enters the pie, and the bake only has to heat it.

</div>

### Baking the pie

1. **Vents.** Cut 4–6 slits (2–3 cm) in the top crust, or use a lattice. Steam must escape, and the vents are where you read the endpoint.
2. **Egg wash** (egg with a pinch of salt, or yolk and cream) for colour and gloss; coarse sugar for crunch.
3. **Chill the assembled pie 20–30 minutes** so the butter is firm when it enters the oven.
4. **Hot, then lower.** Start at **220 °C (430 °F)** on the lowest rack, on a preheated tray (bottom heat for the base; the tray also catches drips), for **20 minutes** to set the crust and start the steam lift. Then **190 °C (375 °F)** for **40–50 minutes** until the crust is deep golden and the filling bubbles thickly in the centre.
5. **Cool** on a rack 3–4 hours before cutting.

A glass or thin metal pie plate conducts to the base faster than ceramic; a glass plate also lets you see whether the base is browned.

## Crème d'amande: a baked almond cream

Crème d'amande is one of the simplest and most useful French components: **equal weights of butter, sugar, almond flour and eggs** (1:1:1:1), sometimes with a little flour for stability (about 10 g for every 100 g of almond flour), and optional flavouring (rum, vanilla, citrus zest). It is spread into a tart shell, topped with fruit if you like, and baked until puffed and set.

| Ingredient | Role |
|---|---|
| Butter | Continuous fat phase; richness; carries the creamed air |
| Sugar | Sweetness; tenderness; browning |
| Almond flour | Body, flavour, fat; absorbs moisture from fruit |
| Eggs | Emulsifier (yolk lecithin), water, and protein that coagulates in the oven to set the cream |
| Flour (optional, ~10 % of the almond flour weight) | Starch that gelatinizes and binds free water; reduces rising and sinking, holds fruit juices |

### Method and control points

Butter at **18–20 °C** (soft, not greasy) is mixed with sugar until smooth and pale, then almond flour; eggs at room temperature go in gradually so the emulsion holds, then the flour. Mix **only until smooth**, on low speed or by hand. This is creaming with a restraint: you want a smooth emulsion, not a cake batter full of air. Overcreamed crème d'amande rises dramatically in the oven, then sinks, cracks and can overflow the shell.

Fill the shell **no more than half to two-thirds full**; it rises by about a third as it bakes. Bake at **165–175 °C (330–350 °F)** until the surface is evenly golden, the centre springs back when pressed, and a skewer comes out clean. The centre should reach **at least 85 °C** (eggs fully set; also the safety point for raw egg).

### Frangipane is a later variant

In classical French terminology, **frangipane** is crème d'amande lightened with **crème pâtissière**, typically **about 2 parts crème d'amande to 1 part pastry cream** by weight. It is softer, moister and more custardy, and it is the filling of the galette des rois. You will make pastry cream in [12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md) ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)); frangipane is listed as a variant in [R1-23](recipes/R1-23-creme-damande.md) for use after that. Many recipes use the two names interchangeably; in this course the distinction is kept because the two behave differently.

### The module practical: pear and almond tart

The M11 practical assembles everything: a par-baked sucrée shell (11.1, 11.2), crème d'amande (this lesson), and fruit. Poached or canned pear halves, sliced crosswise and fanned, are pressed into the cream, in the style of the classic *tarte bourdaloue*. Apricots (fresh halves or canned, well drained) work the same way. The fruit must be drained and patted dry: every gram of juice ends up in the cream and the base.

## What goes wrong

- **Runny fruit pie.** Not enough starch for that fruit, or pulled before the centre bubbled, or cut warm. See [Runny fruit pie filling](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=runny-fruit-pie-filling).
- **Soggy bottom crust.** Wet filling, no bottom heat, base not sealed or pre-baked. See [Soggy bottom](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=soggy-bottom).
- **Pie crust not flaky, or greasy.** Butter softened or smeared during mixing and rolling. See [Greasy pastry, butter leaking](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=greasy-pastry-butter-leaking) and [Tough pastry](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=tough-pastry).
- **Crème d'amande rises then sinks, or overflows.** Overcreamed, overfilled or underbaked. See [Frangipane sinks or overflows](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=frangipane-sinks-or-overflows).

## Check yourself

1. You have 1,100 g of prepared fresh cherries. How much cornstarch do you use, and how much plain flour if you have no cornstarch?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cherries: cornstarch 4–5 % → **44–55 g** (start at about 50 g). Flour 8–10 % → **88–110 g**. If the cherries are sour, use the top of the range.

</details>

2. A pie crust made with pea-sized butter came out tender but with no visible layers and a slightly greasy base. What happened to the butter?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It softened during mixing, rolling or while the pie waited to be baked, and was smeared into the dough instead of remaining as discrete flakes. With no solid fat barriers, steam could not separate layers; the melted fat pooled at the base. Keep butter at 4–7 °C, chill the dough between steps and chill the assembled pie before baking.

</details>

3. Why is a mealy dough preferred for the bottom crust of a pumpkin or custard pie?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The fat is rubbed in finely, coating the flour, so it absorbs less water and forms a denser, more uniform layer with fewer channels and gaps. It resists soaking from a wet filling better than a flaky crust, whose layers and gaps fill with liquid.

</details>

4. A crème d'amande batch has 150 g each of butter, sugar, almond flour and egg. How much flour do you add at the R1-23 level (10 % of the almond flour weight), and what does it do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

150 × 0.10 = **15 g** (the batch becomes 615 g). Its starch gelatinizes during baking and binds free water from the eggs and the fruit, so the cream rises less, sinks less and holds juices without becoming wet.

</details>

5. Why is thick bubbling in the centre vent a better doneness test for a fruit pie than crust colour?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The filling thickens only once its starch reaches near-boiling temperature, and the centre is the last place to get there. Crust colour depends on oven temperature, sugar and egg wash, and can be complete while the centre is still too cool for the starch to thicken.

</details>
