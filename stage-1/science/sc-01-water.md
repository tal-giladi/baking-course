## The one-paragraph model

Water is a small, bent, polar molecule that sticks to itself and to anything else that is polar (sugars, starch, proteins, salt) through hydrogen bonds. That stickiness explains almost everything it does in baking: it dissolves sugar and salt, it hydrates gluten and swells starch, it softens (plasticizes) solid structures so they bend instead of snap, and it takes a lot of energy to heat and even more to evaporate, which is why a wet dough surface cannot rise above ~100 °C until it dries. Not all the water in a food is equally available: some is held tightly by sugars, salt, starch and protein. The **water activity** (aw) of a food measures how "free" its water is, on a scale of 0 (none available) to 1 (pure water). Water activity, not moisture percentage, decides which microbes can grow, whether a biscuit is crisp or soft, how fast browning goes, and which way water moves when two components touch.

## The mechanism

### A polar molecule that makes hydrogen bonds

The oxygen atom in H2O pulls electrons away from the two hydrogens, so the oxygen end is slightly negative and the hydrogen ends slightly positive. Neighbouring molecules line up so that a hydrogen of one points at the oxygen of the next: a **hydrogen bond**. Each hydrogen bond is weak (roughly a tenth to a twentieth of a covalent bond), but liquid water is a constantly reforming network of them, and that network is what gives water its unusual properties.

Anything with –OH, –NH or charged groups can join the network. Sugars are covered in –OH groups; starch is a chain of sugars; proteins have many polar and charged side chains; salt splits into charged ions. All of them pull water toward themselves. Fats have almost no polar groups, which is why fat and water separate (see [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)).

### What water does in a dough or batter

| Role | What happens | Bench consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent | Dissolves sugar, salt, acids, soluble proteins, yeast food | Sugar must dissolve before it can tenderize or feed yeast; undissolved salt shows as specks |
| Hydrating agent | Binds to gluten proteins and starch, lets them move and interact | No gluten network forms in dry flour; hydration sets dough consistency |
| Plasticizer | Water molecules between polymer chains let them slide | More water = softer crumb, bendable cookie; less water = crisp, brittle |
| Reaction medium | Enzymes, yeast and chemical leaveners only work in solution | Dry-mix baking powder is stable; wet batter starts reacting |
| Heat carrier and leavening gas | Water turns to steam and expands ~1,600× in volume | Oven spring, puff pastry lift, choux cavities |
| Temperature cap | Wet surfaces stay at or below ~100 °C | Crust cannot brown until the surface dries |

### Heat numbers worth remembering

| Property | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Specific heat of liquid water | ~4.18 J/g·°C | Water-rich doughs heat slowly; a wet dough needs more energy per degree than flour (~1.8 J/g·°C) |
| Latent heat of fusion (ice → water) | ~334 J/g | Ice in a mixer removes a lot of heat; this is why ice is used to hit a low desired dough temperature |
| Latent heat of vaporization (water → steam at 100 °C) | ~2,260 J/g | Evaporating 1 g of water costs as much energy as heating ~5 g of water from 0 to 100 °C; evaporation cools crust and keeps the interior ≤100 °C |
| Boiling point at sea level | 100 °C (212 °F) | Drops roughly 1 °C per ~300 m of altitude; dissolved sugar raises it (the basis of syrup stages, [13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md)) |
| Volume expansion water → steam at 100 °C | ~1,600× | The main "leavening" in puff pastry and choux |

Heat transfer and evaporation are developed in [SC-10](stage-1/science/sc-10-heat-transfer.md) and [SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md). The headline here: water is a thermal buffer. As long as there is liquid water to evaporate, the temperature at that spot is pinned near its boiling point.

### Where water hides in your ingredients (true hydration)

Baker's percentage "hydration" usually counts only added water. **True hydration** counts every source.

| Ingredient | Approximate water content |
|---|---|
| Flour (as bought) | 12–14 % |
| Whole milk | ~87–88 % |
| Whole egg (out of shell) | ~75 % |
| Egg white | ~88 % |
| Egg yolk | ~50 % |
| Butter | ~15–17 % (typically ~16 %) |
| Honey | ~17–18 % |
| Fresh yeast | ~70 % |
| Levain at 100 % hydration | 50 % of its weight is water (plus flour's own moisture) |

These are standard compositional values; individual products vary by a few percent. The flour's own 12–14 % is normally left out of hydration calculations, but it explains why the same formula behaves differently with an old, dry bag of flour.

### Free water, bound water and water activity

Water in food exists on a spectrum:

- **Tightly bound** — a molecular layer stuck directly to polar groups on starch, protein and sugars. It does not freeze at normal freezer temperatures, does not act as a solvent and is not available to microbes.
- **Loosely held** — water trapped in the gluten network, inside swollen starch granules, or structured around dissolved sugar and salt. Partly available.
- **Free** — water that behaves essentially like pure water: it dissolves things, evaporates easily and feeds microbes.

**Water activity** captures this as one number. Formally, aw is the ratio of the water vapour pressure above the food to that above pure water at the same temperature: aw = p / p0. Practically: seal a food in a small jar and let the air come to equilibrium; the relative humidity of that air, divided by 100, is the aw. A cake crumb at aw 0.90 would hold the air in a sealed box at 90 % relative humidity.

Dissolved molecules lower aw because they tie up water. Small molecules are the most effective per gram, because what counts is the *number* of dissolved particles: salt (which splits into two ions) beats sugar, and glucose or fructose beat sucrose per gram. A saturated salt solution has aw ~0.75; a saturated sucrose solution ~0.85.

### Typical bakery water activities

| Product | Typical aw | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Bread dough, fresh bread crumb | ~0.95–0.97 | Moulds within days at room temperature; soft, flexible crumb |
| Bread crust (fresh from oven) | much lower, ~0.3–0.6 | Crisp until it absorbs water from crumb and air |
| Cakes | ~0.85–0.95 (higher for sponges, lower for rich, high-sugar cakes) | Shelf life of days to a couple of weeks |
| Pastry cream, custards | ~0.95+ | High risk; refrigerate, short life ([SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)) |
| Jams and fruit fillings (set) | ~0.8 | Bacteria stopped; moulds and yeasts still possible |
| Honey | ~0.5–0.6 | Microbially stable |
| Flour | ~0.6–0.7 | Stable if kept dry; not sterile ([FDA-flour]) |
| Cookies, crackers, crisp tart shells | < 0.6 (often 0.2–0.5) | Crisp; microbially stable; loses crispness if it picks up moisture |
| Dry meringue | ~0.3–0.4 | Crisp, very stable if kept sealed |

These are typical ranges from bakery-science practice; your product can sit outside them depending on formula and bake.

### Water activity and microbes

Microbes need free water to grow. Lowering aw does not kill them; it stops or slows growth.

| Group | Approximate minimum aw for growth |
|---|---|
| Most bacteria (including most pathogens) | > 0.90 |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* (notable exception) | ~0.86 |
| Most yeasts | ~0.88 |
| Most moulds | ~0.80 |
| Osmophilic yeasts and xerophilic moulds | down to ~0.60–0.65 |
| No microbial growth | below ~0.60 |

Read this table as: fresh bread and cream fillings support bacteria; a jam or a rich fruit cake mainly worries you about mould; a cookie or meringue is microbially stable and fails by going soft, not by spoiling. Temperature, pH ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)) and preservatives interact with aw; food safety is covered in [SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md).

### Water as a plasticizer: crisp versus soft

Starch, proteins and sugar in a dry cookie form a rigid, glassy solid. Add a little water and it slips between the chains, the solid turns rubbery, and the cookie bends instead of snapping. Most crisp products lose their crispness somewhere around aw 0.4–0.5 (the exact point depends on formula). That is a small amount of water by weight: often a gain of only 1–3 % moisture turns a crisp biscuit soft.

### Moisture migration: water moves toward equal aw, not equal moisture

When two components with different aw touch or share a closed space, water moves from the higher-aw component to the lower-aw one until their water activities are equal. Their moisture *percentages* can remain very different at equilibrium, because each holds water differently.

- Pastry cream (aw ~0.97) in a tart shell (aw ~0.3) → the shell goes soggy.
- Bread crumb (aw ~0.96) under crust (aw ~0.4) → the crust softens and turns leathery within hours.
- A soft cookie stored with crisp cookies → the soft one dries, the crisp ones soften.
- A cake (aw ~0.9) with a fondant or sugar decoration → the decoration weeps or sticks.

Barriers (cocoa butter or chocolate painted on a tart shell, an egg-wash seal baked on, a fat-based layer), matching water activities (for example, adding sugar to lower a filling's aw) and assembling close to service are the three ways to fight it. You test this directly in [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md).

## What it predicts on the bench

| If you change... | Prediction | Why | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydration from 65 % to 75 % | Softer, stickier dough; more open crumb; faster fermentation | More free water plasticizes gluten and speeds enzyme and yeast activity | [X1-04](experiments/X1-04-hydration-series.md), [02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md) |
| Replace water with milk at equal weight | Stiffer dough than expected | Milk is only ~88 % water; you lowered true hydration | [06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md) |
| Add 10–20 % sugar to a yeast dough | Slower fermentation; dough feels wetter | Sugar dissolves and lowers aw, stressing yeast; dissolved sugar adds to the liquid phase | [X1-15](experiments/X1-15-sugar-in-dough.md) |
| Swap part of the sucrose in cookies for honey or invert | Softer, chewier cookie that stays soft longer | Invert sugars hold water more strongly and lower aw more per gram | [X1-19](experiments/X1-19-sugar-type.md), [07.1](stage-1/module-07/lesson-01.md) |
| Store bread in a sealed bag | Soft, leathery crust, crumb stays softer | Water migrates crumb → crust and cannot escape | [X1-12](experiments/X1-12-staling.md), [04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md) |
| Fill a tart shell 12 h before service | Soggy base | Water migrates filling → shell toward equal aw | [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md), [17.1](stage-1/module-17/lesson-01.md) |
| Leave crisp meringues or cookies uncovered on a humid day | They go sticky or soft | They absorb water vapour from air with a higher relative humidity than their aw | [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md) |
| Use ice water on a hot day | Lower dough temperature | Melting ice absorbs 334 J/g on top of the warming of cold water | [01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md) |

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Moisture migration in a jar (10 minutes to set up, 24 h to read).** Weigh a plain cracker and a slice of fresh bread separately. Put both in a sealed jar or zip bag without touching. After 24 h, weigh them again and snap the cracker. The cracker gains weight and bends; the bread loses weight. Water moved through the air from high aw to low aw.
2. **Sugar and salt tie up water.** Slice a cucumber into three equal groups of rounds. Sprinkle one with 2 % salt, one with 10 % sugar, leave one plain. After 20 minutes, compare the liquid drawn out. Salt pulls more water than sugar at a far lower dose: particles count, not grams.
3. **The 100 °C cap.** Put a probe thermometer in a pot of water on high heat and read it every minute. The temperature climbs, then stops at ~100 °C (a little lower at altitude) no matter how hard it boils. All further energy goes into evaporation. That is your dough surface in the first part of the bake.

## Misconceptions

- **"A moist cake has a lot of water; a dry cake has little."** Perceived moistness depends on free water and fat, not just moisture percentage. A high-sugar cake can hold more water but have a lower aw and taste moist for longer.
- **"Low moisture percentage means safe."** Safety tracks water activity. A filling with 30 % moisture but little sugar can have a high aw; jam with 30–35 % moisture is stable because sugar binds its water.
- **"Water moves from the wetter to the drier component until they have the same moisture."** It moves until the *water activities* are equal. At equilibrium a tart shell and its filling still have very different moisture percentages.
- **"Adding sugar makes dough drier."** Sugar dissolves into the water and increases the volume of the liquid phase; sweet doughs feel softer and slacker at the same hydration, even though the water is less available to yeast.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| How much water is in this dough or product (moisture %)? | Scale plus oven: weigh, dry at low heat to constant weight, reweigh | Stage 1 (kitchen method, approximate) |
| How much water did the loaf lose in baking (bake loss %)? | Scale: weigh dough before, loaf after cooling | Stage 1 |
| What is the water temperature for my DDT? | Probe thermometer | Stage 1 |
| How concentrated is my syrup or filling? | Refractometer (°Brix, % dissolved solids by weight) | Stage 2 |
| What is the water activity? | Water activity meter (dew-point or capacitance sensor) | Stage 3 (costly; used for shelf-life and safety work) |
| How much moisture fast, for QC? | Infrared moisture analyzer (heats and weighs) | Stage 3 |

Without an aw meter you can still reason about aw: a formula with a lot of dissolved sugar or salt relative to its water has a lower aw.

## Check yourself

1. A brioche formula uses 60 % whole egg and no added water. What is the approximate true hydration from the egg alone?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Whole egg is ~75 % water, so 60 % × 0.75 ≈ 45 % hydration from egg, before counting any milk or the water in butter.

</details>

2. You pipe crème pâtissière into baked choux at 9 a.m. for a 6 p.m. event. Predict what happens to the shells and why.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The shells soften. Pastry cream has an aw near 0.97; a dry choux shell is much lower, so water migrates into the shell until their water activities are closer to equal. Fill closer to service, or seal the inside of the shell (for example with a thin layer of cocoa butter or chocolate).

</details>

3. Two jams have identical moisture percentages, but one uses more fructose-rich syrup in place of sucrose. Which has the lower aw?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The fructose-rich one. Fructose is a smaller molecule than sucrose, so the same weight contributes about twice as many dissolved particles and lowers aw more.

</details>

4. Why does a loaf's crust not brown during the first 10 minutes of a steamed bake, even at 240 °C oven temperature?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

While the surface is wet, evaporation pins it near 100 °C. Browning needs the surface to dry and rise well above 100 °C ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)).

</details>

5. A cake has aw 0.88. Which microbial group is your main shelf-life concern at room temperature?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Moulds (and some yeasts). At 0.88 most bacteria are inhibited, but most moulds grow down to ~0.80.

</details>
