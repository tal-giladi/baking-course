## The one-paragraph model

A protein is a long chain of amino acids that folds into a specific shape, held together by many weak bonds and, in some proteins, a few strong sulfur–sulfur bridges. In its **native** (folded) state it floats in water on its own. Heat, acid, salt, air bubbles and mechanical work can **denature** it: the weak bonds break and the chain unfolds, exposing sticky parts that were hidden inside. Unfolded chains then bond to each other — **coagulation** or aggregation — and form a network that traps water and gas. That network is the structure of a set custard, a baked cake, a meringue and a bread crumb. Wheat's gluten proteins are a special case: they build a stretchy, elastic network at room temperature simply by being hydrated and worked, and heat then sets that network permanently.

## The mechanism

### From amino acids to a folded protein

There are 20 common amino acids. They share a backbone and differ in their side chain, which can be:

| Side-chain type | Behaviour | Why it matters in baking |
|---|---|---|
| Water-loving (polar, charged) | Faces the water outside the folded protein | Keeps native proteins dissolved; charges depend on pH ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)) |
| Water-avoiding (hydrophobic) | Buried inside the fold | Exposed on unfolding; drives proteins to air–water and oil–water interfaces and to each other |
| Sulfur-containing (cysteine) | Can form a **disulfide bond** (S–S) with another cysteine | Strong cross-links; the backbone of gluten elasticity and of egg-white gel strength |

A protein's shape is held by many weak interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, hydrophobic clustering) plus, sometimes, disulfide bonds. Weak bonds break easily; that is why proteins are so sensitive to temperature, pH and salt.

### Native, denatured, coagulated

| State | What it looks like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Native | Folded, dissolved, liquid | Raw egg white: clear, runny |
| Denatured | Unfolded, but not yet joined up | Egg white just starting to turn opalescent at ~60 °C |
| Coagulated / aggregated | Unfolded chains bonded into a network trapping water | Opaque, set egg white; set custard; baked cake crumb |
| Over-coagulated | Network contracts, squeezes out water | Rubbery egg white, curdled custard, weeping scrambled eggs |

Four levers denature proteins:

| Lever | How it works | Bench example |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | Molecular motion breaks weak bonds | Custards, eggs, cake and bread setting |
| Acid (low pH) | Changes charges on side chains; proteins lose their mutual repulsion near their isoelectric point | Cream of tartar in meringue; lemon in ricotta-style curd; buttermilk tenderness |
| Salt | Shields charges; at moderate levels strengthens gluten and egg gels | Salt tightens dough; salted eggs set more firmly |
| Mechanical / interface | Whipping stretches proteins across air bubbles; they unfold at the surface | Egg-white foam ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)) |

**Controlling coagulation** is the art. The same proteins can give a silky custard or a curdled one; the difference is how many bonds form and how fast. Dilution (milk in a custard), sugar and fat all *raise* the temperature at which a set appears and make it gentler; acid and salt usually *lower* it and make it firmer.

### Egg proteins in numbers

| Component | Approximate composition | Key proteins |
|---|---|---|
| Whole egg (no shell) | ~75 % water, ~12–13 % protein, ~10–11 % fat | — |
| White (~⅔ of egg weight) | ~88 % water, ~10–11 % protein, almost no fat | Ovalbumin (~54 % of white protein), ovotransferrin (~12 %), ovomucoid, lysozyme, ovomucin |
| Yolk (~⅓) | ~50 % water, ~16 % protein, ~30–33 % fat | Lipoproteins; phospholipids including lecithin (emulsifier) |

| Temperature | Egg white | Egg yolk |
|---|---|---|
| ~60–62 °C | Ovotransferrin starts to set; white turns milky | Still liquid |
| ~62–65 °C | Soft, tender, just-set gel | Begins to thicken |
| ~65–70 °C | Firmer | Thickens, then sets (~70 °C) to a tender paste |
| ~80 °C and above | Ovalbumin fully set: firm, then rubbery | Firm, crumbly |

Diluted with milk and sugar, eggs set much higher. Crème anglaise is cooked to ~82–84 °C; much above ~85 °C the yolk proteins over-coagulate and the sauce curdles. Baked custards are set when the centre reaches roughly 75–80 °C and still wobbles. You map this yourself in [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md) and [X1-33](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md).

### Gluten: wheat's network-forming proteins

Wheat flour contains ~8–14 % protein, of which roughly 80 % is the two gluten-forming families:

| | Gliadins | Glutenins |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Single, compact chains | Very large polymers of subunits linked end-to-end and side-to-side by **disulfide bonds** |
| Contribution | Viscosity and **extensibility** (the dough stretches and flows) | **Elasticity** and strength (the dough springs back) |
| Analogy | Lubricant or glue between the chains | Coiled springs linked into a mesh |

When flour is hydrated and mixed, these proteins absorb roughly twice their own weight of water, unfold, and align into sheets and strands. Mixing stretches glutenin polymers and helps disulfide bonds rearrange into a continuous network; rest lets that network relax. The network holds fermentation gas like a balloon skin. Dough behaviour (strength, extensibility, elasticity) is covered as rheology in [SC-12](stage-1/science/sc-12-rheology.md); flour choice in [02.1](stage-1/module-02/lesson-01.md); network building in [02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md).

| Factor | Effect on gluten | Why |
|---|---|---|
| More water (up to a point) | More development, more extensible | Proteins need water to unfold and move |
| Mixing / kneading / folds | Stronger, more aligned network | Mechanical work unfolds and orients glutenin |
| Salt (~1.8–2.2 % of flour) | Tighter, stronger, less sticky | Shields charges so proteins pack closer |
| Sugar | Slower development, weaker | Competes for water |
| Fat | Shorter, more tender | Coats proteins, blocks bonding ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)) |
| Mild acid | Tighter at first; excessive acid weakens | Charge effects; low pH also boosts protease activity |
| Proteases, glutathione (from dead yeast cells) | Slack, extensible dough | Cut protein chains or break disulfide bonds ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) |
| Oxidation (air during mixing, ascorbic acid improver) | Stronger | Encourages disulfide cross-links |
| Heat in the oven (~70–80 °C onward) | Network sets permanently | Gluten proteins coagulate as starch gelatinizes around them |

In short pastry ([11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md)) you want the opposite of bread: little water, fat coating the flour and minimal mixing so gluten barely forms.

### Milk proteins

Milk is ~3.3 % protein, in two classes:

| | Casein (~80 %) | Whey proteins (~20 %) |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Micelles: clusters held together partly by calcium phosphate | Dissolved globular proteins (mainly β-lactoglobulin) |
| Heat | Very heat-stable; milk can be boiled without curdling | Denature from ~70–80 °C; form the skin on hot milk |
| Acid | Coagulates near pH 4.6 (yogurt, curdled buttermilk batters) | Stable in acid unless heated |
| Baking role | Structure and water binding; browning with lactose | Denatured whey interferes with gluten, which is why bakers traditionally scald milk (or use heat-treated milk powder) for bread |

### Gelatin, briefly

Gelatin is collagen that has been unwound and partly broken by processing. Dissolved in warm water and cooled, its chains wrap back into partial triple-helix junctions and form a gel. That gel is **thermoreversible**: it sets on cooling (typically below ~15–20 °C, strengthening over hours) and melts at ~27–35 °C, close to mouth temperature, which is why gelatin desserts melt cleanly on the tongue. Gel strength is graded by **Bloom** (sheet gelatin comes in bronze, silver, gold grades); swapping grades changes the dose. Raw pineapple, kiwi, papaya, mango and fig contain proteases that digest gelatin; cook the fruit first. Gelatin appears in Stage 1 in stabilized creams ([12.3](stage-1/module-12/lesson-03.md)) and entremets ([17.3](stage-1/module-17/lesson-03.md)).

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Egg-white temperature ladder.** Heat a small pan of water to 62 °C and hold it (check with a probe; adjust the heat). Drop in a spoonful of egg white in a small heatproof cup for 10 minutes. Repeat at 70 °C and 80 °C. Compare: milky-soft, tender, firm-rubbery. Keep raw egg handling hygienic ([FDA-eggs]).
2. **Gluten ball.** Mix 50 g bread flour with ~30 g water, knead 5 minutes, rest 10 minutes, then rinse under a trickle of water, squeezing, until the water runs nearly clear. What's left is mostly gluten: stretch it and feel both elasticity and extensibility. Repeat with cake flour and compare the size.
3. **Acid curdles milk.** Warm 200 g milk to ~80 °C and stir in 15 g lemon juice. Curds form as casein coagulates near its isoelectric point; the greenish liquid is whey.

## Misconceptions

- **"Protein content equals bread quality."** Protein *quality* (the gliadin–glutenin balance and how the network forms) matters as much as quantity. Two 12 % flours can behave very differently.
- **"Kneading creates gluten."** Gluten proteins are already in the flour; water lets them form a network and mixing organizes it. Given time, hydration alone develops a lot of gluten (autolyse, no-knead methods).
- **"Eggs cook at 100 °C."** Egg proteins set between ~62 and ~80 °C. Anything hotter only risks overcooking; custards fail by overshooting, not by undershooting boiling.
- **"Denatured means destroyed."** Denaturation is unfolding. It is often exactly what you want: foams, set custards, baked structure all depend on it.

| Has my custard reached its endpoint? | Probe thermometer (instant-read, ±0.5 °C, calibrated) plus nappe test | Stage 1 |

## Check yourself

1. You add 50 % more sugar to a custard base without changing anything else. Does it set at a higher or lower temperature, and does it become firmer or softer?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Higher setting temperature and softer set: sugar separates the proteins and slows their bonding.

</details>

2. Why does a brioche with 50 % butter need more mixing to reach a windowpane than a lean dough, and why is butter often added late?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat coats gluten proteins and hinders bonding. Developing the gluten first with the water and eggs, then working in butter, builds the network before fat can block it.

</details>

3. Scrambled eggs left on the heat weep liquid on the plate. Explain in protein terms.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Over-coagulation: the protein network keeps forming bonds, tightens and squeezes out the water it was holding (syneresis).

</details>

4. A pineapple mousse set with gelatin never sets when made with fresh fruit but does with canned. Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fresh pineapple contains bromelain, a protease that cuts gelatin chains. Canning heat inactivates the enzyme.

</details>
