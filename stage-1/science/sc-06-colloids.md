## The one-paragraph model

A **colloid** is one substance finely dispersed through another without dissolving: gas bubbles in liquid (a **foam**), droplets of one liquid in another (an **emulsion**), or a liquid trapped in a continuous solid network (a **gel**). Most of pastry is colloids: whipped cream, meringue, mayonnaise, ganache, buttercream, custard, cake batter and, after baking, bread and cake crumb (a solid foam). Every dispersion is thermodynamically unstable — given time, bubbles merge and escape, droplets merge and separate. Your job is to slow that down: make the bubbles or droplets small, coat them with something that sits at the interface (proteins, emulsifiers, fat crystals or particles), and make the continuous phase thick or set. Most colloid failures on the bench are one of five processes: drainage, creaming, flocculation, coalescence or Ostwald ripening.

## The mechanism

### The families

| Type | Dispersed phase | Continuous phase | Bakery examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquid foam | Gas | Liquid | Whipped egg white, whipped cream, sabayon, mousse |
| Solid foam | Gas | Solid | Baked meringue, bread and cake crumb, sponge |
| Oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion | Oil/fat | Water | Milk, cream, mayonnaise, crème anglaise, ganache, cake batter (partly) |
| Water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion | Water | Fat | Butter, margarine; buttercreams behave largely fat-continuous |
| Gel | Liquid | Solid network | Starch gel (pastry cream), gelatin, pectin (jam), egg (custard) |
| Suspension | Solid particles | Liquid | Cocoa particles in ganache, sugar crystals in fondant |

Real products are combinations. A cake batter is an emulsion with a foam in it and suspended flour; whipped cream is a foam stabilized by partly merged fat droplets; a mousse is a foam set by a gel.

### Interfaces and the molecules that sit on them

Creating bubbles or droplets creates a huge amount of new surface. Surfaces cost energy (surface tension), and the system will shrink them whenever it can. Stabilizers lower that cost and form a protective film.

| Stabilizer | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Small-molecule emulsifiers (surfactants) | One water-loving end, one fat-loving end; pack quickly at the interface | Lecithin (egg yolk phospholipids), mono- and diglycerides (commercial shortenings, bread improvers) |
| Proteins | Unfold at the interface ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)); hydrophobic parts into air/oil, hydrophilic into water; bond to each other into an elastic skin | Egg-white proteins in meringue; milk proteins in cream; yolk lipoproteins in mayonnaise |
| Particles (Pickering stabilization) | Solid particles wetted by both phases lodge at the interface | Fat crystals in whipped cream and creamed butter; cocoa particles in ganache |
| Viscous or gelled continuous phase | Slows movement of bubbles and droplets | Sugar syrup in Italian meringue; starch in pastry cream; gelatin in mousse |

### How colloids fail

| Process | What happens | Reversible? | Bench example | How to slow it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Drainage** | Liquid runs down between bubbles under gravity | Partly | Liquid pooling under a French meringue or whipped cream | Thicken the liquid (sugar), set the proteins (heat) |
| **Creaming / sedimentation** | Droplets float up (or particles sink) | Yes (stir) | Cream rising on unhomogenized milk; nuts sinking in thin batter | Smaller droplets, more viscous continuous phase |
| **Flocculation** | Droplets clump but keep their films | Often | Grainy-looking sauce that smooths with whisking | Charge (pH), emulsifier |
| **Coalescence** | Films break; droplets or bubbles merge into bigger ones | **No** | Split mayonnaise; overwhipped cream turning to butter; broken ganache | Strong interfacial film, correct temperature, don't add dispersed phase too fast |
| **Ostwald ripening / disproportionation** | Gas diffuses from small bubbles (higher internal pressure) to large ones; small shrink, large grow | No | Coarsening of a meringue or mousse over hours | Small uniform bubbles, stiff films, setting the foam |
| **Phase inversion** | Continuous and dispersed phases swap | Sometimes | Cream churned into butter (O/W → W/O); ganache splitting when fat overwhelms water | Keep the phase ratio and temperature in range |

**Stokes' law** sums up why size and viscosity matter for creaming and sedimentation: the speed at which a droplet rises or sinks grows with the *square* of its radius and falls with the viscosity of the liquid. Halve the droplet size, and it rises four times slower.

### Foams in pastry

| Foam | Stabilizer | Key numbers and conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Egg-white foam (meringue) | Egg-white proteins; sugar for viscosity; cream of tartar for pH ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)) | Whites whip to ~6–8× volume; even ~0.1 % yolk fat measurably reduces volume; sugar added too early delays foaming, added gradually after soft peaks gives a stable, glossy foam |
| Whipped cream | Partial coalescence of milk-fat globules around bubbles; fat crystals | Needs ≥ ~30 % fat (35–40 % whips best) and ≤ ~7 °C; doubles in volume at most; overwhip → butter and buttermilk |
| Whole-egg foam (génoise) | Yolk and white proteins, sugar; warming to ~40–45 °C improves volume | Beaten to ribbon stage, roughly 3× volume |
| Sabayon | Egg-yolk proteins set gently by heat | Cooked to ~70–80 °C while whisking: foam plus coagulation ([R1-16](recipes/R1-16-sabayon.md)) |
| Creamed butter and sugar | Plastic butter with β′ fat crystals lining air cells ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)); sugar crystals cut air in | Butter at ~18–21 °C; volume rises and colour pales; specific gravity falls |
| Bread and cake crumb | Gluten or egg/starch network set by heat | Liquid foam → solid foam when proteins coagulate and starch gelatinizes; bubbles "open" into a sponge |

**Creaming is a foam, not just mixing.** The air cells you trap in butter become the nuclei that baking powder CO₂ and steam expand in the oven. Chemical leaveners cannot create new bubbles; they only inflate existing ones. Poor creaming therefore gives a dense cake no matter how much baking powder you add ([10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md), [X1-26](experiments/X1-26-creaming-time.md)).

### Emulsions in pastry

| Emulsion | Type | Emulsifier | Critical conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milk / cream | O/W | Milk-fat globule membrane, casein | Cream 35–40 % fat |
| Mayonnaise | O/W with ~75–80 % oil | Yolk lecithin and lipoproteins | Add oil slowly at first; droplets packed so tightly they deform, which gives the thick, spoonable texture ([R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md)) |
| Butter | W/O, ~16 % water | Fat crystal network | Stable while solid; separates when melted |
| Ganache | O/W: cocoa butter and milk-fat droplets in a sugar-water phase, with suspended cocoa particles | Milk proteins, lecithin in chocolate, cocoa particles | Enough water phase for the fat; emulsify at ~35–45 °C; split when fat is too high relative to water or when too hot |
| Swiss/Italian meringue buttercream | Butter-continuous structure holding meringue foam and water | Butter fat crystals, egg proteins | Curdles when too cold (butter too hard to take in the water phase); goes soupy when too warm (above ~24–26 °C) |
| Cake batter | O/W with air | Egg yolk, proteins, emulsifiers in shortening | Curdled creamed batter when eggs added cold or too fast |

**Ganache as an engineering problem.** A ganache fails when its water phase cannot hold its fat. High-cocoa dark chocolates bring more fat and solids and less of their own water-compatible sugar, so a 70 % chocolate at a 1:1 ratio with cream is more split-prone than a 55 % chocolate at the same ratio. Rescue: warm to ~35 °C and emulsify with a little warm cream or milk using an immersion blender from the centre outward ([16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md), [X1-42](experiments/X1-42-ganache-ratio.md)).

**Buttercream rescue logic.** Curdled and cold: warm a portion of the bowl (torch the outside or melt 10 % of it) and beat; the fat softens and takes in the water phase. Soupy and warm: chill 10–15 minutes and beat again. Diagnose by temperature first.

### Gels

| Gel | Network | Sets | Melts / breaks | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starch (pastry cream, pie filling) | Swollen granules and leached amylose | On cooling after gelatinization | Does not remelt; weeps with retrogradation | [SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md) |
| Egg protein (custard, flan) | Coagulated protein | On heating to ~75–85 °C (diluted) | Irreversible; over-heating squeezes out water | [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md) |
| Gelatin | Partial triple helices | On cooling below ~15–20 °C | Remelts ~27–35 °C | Thermoreversible |
| Pectin (high-methoxyl) | Pectin chains linked by sugar and acid | Needs ~60–65 % sugar and pH ~2.8–3.5 | Heat-stable once set | Jams, pâte de fruits (Stage 2) |
| Chocolate / ganache set | Fat crystal network | On cooling | Melts near body temperature | [SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md) |

## What it predicts on the bench

| If you change... | Prediction | Why | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A speck of yolk in the whites | Lower volume, weeping foam | Fat competes at the air interface and disrupts the protein film | [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md) |
| Add all the sugar to whites at the start | Slower foaming, smaller volume but very stable | Sugar raises viscosity and delays protein unfolding | [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md) |
| French vs Swiss vs Italian meringue | Italian most stable, French least | Hot syrup or warming partly sets proteins and makes a thick continuous phase | [X1-35](experiments/X1-35-meringue-stability.md), [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md) |
| Whip cream at 12 °C | Slow, low volume, greasy | Too little fat crystallized to support partial coalescence | [12.3](stage-1/module-12/lesson-03.md) |
| Pour oil into yolk fast at the start of mayonnaise | Split, thin sauce | Droplets added faster than emulsifier can coat them; coalescence | [R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md), [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md) |
| Increase chocolate ratio in a ganache from 1:1 to 2:1 (dark) | Firmer, then oily and grainy if water phase runs short | Fat exceeds what the water phase can hold | [X1-42](experiments/X1-42-ganache-ratio.md) |
| Cream butter and sugar 2 vs 8 minutes | Longer: lower batter density, taller, finer cake (up to a point) | More and smaller air cells | [X1-26](experiments/X1-26-creaming-time.md) |
| Fold flour roughly into génoise foam | Dense, flat cake | Bubbles burst; foam collapses | [10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md) |

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Vinaigrette vs mayonnaise.** Shake 30 g oil with 10 g vinegar in a jar: it separates within minutes (creaming, then coalescence). Repeat adding 5 g egg yolk or 3 g mustard: it stays mixed far longer. Emulsifiers at work.
2. **Overwhip on purpose.** Whip 150 g cold cream (35 %+) past stiff peaks and keep going. Watch it go grainy, then suddenly separate into yellow butter and thin buttermilk: coalescence and phase inversion. Weigh both.
3. **Foam coarsening.** Whip one egg white to stiff peaks without sugar and another with 60 g sugar added gradually. Leave both 30 minutes in glasses. The plain foam drains and coarsens; the sugared one stays glossy and fine.

## Misconceptions

- **"A split emulsion is ruined."** Most can be rescued: re-emulsify by starting with a fresh little continuous phase (yolk, warm cream, water) and adding the split mixture slowly.
- **"More whipping always means more volume."** Past optimum, protein films over-coagulate (dry, grainy whites) and fat globules coalesce (butter). Every foam has a peak.
- **"Buttercream curdled because the butter was bad."** Almost always temperature: too cold, and the butter cannot take in the water phase.
- **"Baking powder makes the bubbles in a cake."** It inflates bubbles already created by creaming or whisking; it cannot nucleate new ones.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| How much air did I beat in? | Specific gravity: weigh a fixed-volume cup of batter and divide by the weight of the same cup of water | Stage 1 |
| Is my foam stable? | Drainage: measure liquid collected under a foam after 30–60 min in a funnel or sieve over a cup | Stage 1 |
| Is my emulsion at the right temperature? | Probe thermometer (ganache ~35–45 °C when emulsifying; buttercream ~20–22 °C working) | Stage 1 |
| What is my droplet or bubble size? | Microscope (a cheap USB microscope reveals a lot) | Stage 2 |
| Viscosity / firmness | Viscometer, texture analyser | Stage 3 |

## Check yourself

1. Your Swiss meringue buttercream looks like cottage cheese. The kitchen is 17 °C. What happened and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Too cold: the butter is too firm to take in the water phase of the meringue, so the emulsion looks curdled. Warm part of the bowl gently (a torch or a few seconds over a bain-marie) and beat again until smooth.

</details>

2. Why does Italian meringue survive being folded into a mousse better than French meringue?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Hot syrup partly sets the egg proteins and creates a viscous sugar continuous phase, so bubbles resist drainage, coalescence and coarsening.

</details>

3. You want a more stable whipped cream for piping without gelatin. Name two colloid levers.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Higher fat cream (~38–40 %), whipping very cold (≤ 5 °C) with a chilled bowl, adding mascarpone (more fat and solids) or a little sugar for viscosity; stop at firm, not stiff, peaks.

</details>

4. A dark 70 % chocolate ganache at 1.5:1 chocolate:cream turns oily. Name the cause and a fix.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat relative to the water phase is too high for a stable O/W emulsion. Add warm liquid (cream or milk, a few % at a time) and re-emulsify with an immersion blender, or reformulate with more cream or a lower-cocoa chocolate.

</details>
