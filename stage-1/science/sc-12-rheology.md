## The one-paragraph model

Rheology is the science of how materials deform and flow. Every dough, batter and cream you handle sits somewhere between an ideal **solid** (a spring: it stretches under force and snaps back) and an ideal **liquid** (honey: it flows and stays where it flowed). Bread dough is both at once, **viscoelastic**: pull it quickly and it springs back; leave it alone and it slowly flows and relaxes. Two practical numbers describe a dough: how far it stretches before tearing (**extensibility**) and how hard it pushes back when you stretch it (**resistance**, or elasticity). Everything you do, salt, water, mixing, resting, acid, fat, enzymes, temperature, moves one or both. Good bread dough also **strain-hardens**: the thinner a gas-cell wall is stretched, the stiffer it gets, so bubbles grow evenly instead of bursting. Batters and creams are different systems: there, **viscosity** and **yield stress** decide whether bubbles stay suspended and whether a cream holds its piped shape. With these few ideas you can read a dough through your hands and predict how it will shape, proof and bake.

## The mechanism

### Elastic, viscous, viscoelastic

| Behaviour | Model | Response to a stretch | Kitchen example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic | Spring | Stretches in proportion to force, returns fully when released | A rubber band; very strong, freshly mixed bagel dough |
| Viscous | Dashpot (a piston in oil) | Flows at a speed proportional to force; does not return | Honey, melted butter, thin batter |
| Viscoelastic | Spring and dashpot together | Partly springs back, partly flows; depends on **how fast** and **how long** | Bread dough, brioche, pâte sucrée |

Time scale is the key idea. Dough reacts elastically to fast deformation (a quick pull springs back) and viscously to slow deformation (a boule slowly slumps on the bench). This is why you can shape dough firmly and then it relaxes, and why a slow, gentle stretch goes further than a fast yank.

In gluten, the elastic part comes mainly from long **glutenin** polymers linked by disulfide bonds; the viscous, flowing part mainly from **gliadins**, which act as a plasticizer between them ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md), [Wieser 2007]). Water plasticizes both.

### Relaxation: why bench rest works

When you shape or roll a dough you store elastic stress in the stretched gluten. Left alone, the network slowly rearranges and that stress decays: **stress relaxation**. Practical rules from bakery practice:

| Situation | Typical rest | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-shaped bread dough | 15–30 min covered | Can be shaped tightly without tearing |
| Pizza or pita dough that springs back | 10–20 min | Rolls to size and stays there |
| Rolled tart or pie dough | 30 min to overnight, chilled | Less shrinkage in the oven ([X1-32](experiments/X1-32-tart-shrinkage.md)) |
| Laminated dough between folds | 20–30 min, chilled | Rolls without tearing layers; butter re-firms |

A dough that shrinks back when rolled is not "too strong" as a flour; it has unrelaxed stress. Rest it.

### Extensibility versus resistance

Bakers describe dough strength with two linked properties. **Extensibility** is how far the dough stretches before breaking. **Resistance** is the force it pushes back with. A good bread dough balances them: enough resistance to hold shape and gas, enough extensibility to expand without tearing.

| Change | Resistance | Extensibility | What you feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add salt (0 → 2 %) | ↑ | ↓ slightly | Tighter, less sticky, more "bouncy" ([X1-06](experiments/X1-06-salt.md)) |
| More water (65 → 75 %) | ↓ | ↑ | Softer, slacker, stickier ([X1-04](experiments/X1-04-hydration-series.md)) |
| Mix longer (to full development) | ↑ then ↓ past optimum | ↑ | Smooth, thin windowpane; overmixed dough goes sticky and slack ([X1-05](experiments/X1-05-mixing-time.md)) |
| Rest (bench rest, autolyse) | ↓ | ↑ | Relaxed, easier to shape |
| Stretch and fold during bulk | ↑ | ↓ (temporarily) | Stronger, rounder dough |
| Mild acid (sourdough, preferment) | ↑ slightly | ↓ slightly | Tighter at first |
| Excess acid, very long fermentation | ↓ | ↑ then tears | Slack, sticky, weak ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) |
| Add fat (butter, oil) | ↓ | ↑ | Silky, extensible; shorter crumb |
| Proteases, glutathione (dead yeast) | ↓↓ | ↑ | Slack, sticky dough |
| Oxidizers (ascorbic acid in flour) | ↑ | ↓ | Stronger, more tolerant dough |
| Colder dough | ↑ (stiffer) | ↓ | Firm, hard to shape; butter in brioche sets |
| Higher-protein flour | ↑ | ↑ (often) | More water absorption, more strength |

### Strain hardening: the property that makes bread rise

In a proofing loaf, each gas cell is surrounded by a thin gluten film. As gas pushes, some walls are thinner than others. If a material got *weaker* as it thinned, the thin spot would stretch fastest and burst, and bubbles would merge into big holes and escape. Good bread dough does the opposite: its resistance rises faster than the stretch at large deformations (**strain hardening**), so a thinning spot stiffens and the stretch moves to thicker parts of the wall. Walls thin evenly, cells stay separate longer, and the loaf holds gas into the oven. Flours with better gluten quality strain-harden more, and strain hardening is closely associated with good loaf volume [Delcour 2012].

Strain hardening falls as temperature rises in the oven, which is one reason cells finally coalesce and open during baking as the crumb sets ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)).

### Batters: viscosity holds bubbles

A cake or muffin batter is not a gluten network; it is a thick liquid holding air bubbles and fat. Bubbles try to rise and escape. Their rise speed grows with **the square of the bubble radius** and falls with **viscosity** (Stokes' law, as intuition). Two predictions follow:

- **Small bubbles stay, big bubbles leave.** Creaming and whisking make many small bubbles that stay suspended; large bubbles float out and burst.
- **Thicker batter holds air longer.** A thin, runny batter (warm, too much liquid, melted fat) loses air before it sets; a batter that is too thick does not flow to fill the pan evenly.

Batter viscosity changes during baking: it first drops as the batter warms and fat melts, then rises sharply as starch gelatinizes and egg proteins set. If the drop is too large or lasts too long, bubbles escape and the cake is dense or tunnelled. Over-mixing a muffin batter does the reverse: it develops gluten, making the batter elastic, which traps large gas channels (tunnels) ([X1-22](experiments/X1-22-muffin-overmixing.md)).

### Shear-thinning, yield stress and creams

Many pastry fluids are **non-Newtonian**: their viscosity depends on how hard you stir them.

| Behaviour | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Shear-thinning | Thinner while stirred or piped, thicker at rest | Whipped cream, pastry cream, ganache, fruit purées, ketchup |
| Yield stress | Behaves like a solid until a threshold force, then flows | Chantilly, buttercream and meringue holding piped ridges |
| Thixotropy | Thins with stirring over *time* and recovers slowly at rest | Some starch-thickened creams and gels |
| Shear-thickening | Thicker under fast stirring | Cornstarch-in-water slurry (oobleck) |

Consequences: a piped rosette holds its ridges because the stress from gravity is below the cream's yield stress. Pastry cream set in the fridge is a stiff gel; beating it smooth makes it pipeable, but vigorous beating also breaks some starch gel structure permanently, so it never fully recovers. Over-whipped cream has a very high yield stress and turns grainy as fat clumps into butter ([12.3](stage-1/module-12/lesson-03.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)).

### Plastic fats

Butter and shortening at the right temperature are **plastic**: solid below a yield stress, spreadable above it. Lamination and creaming depend on it. Butter is plastic over a narrow window (roughly 13–18 °C for lamination; standard professional practice): too cold and it shatters, too warm and it smears into the dough ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md), [15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)).

### Laboratory instruments (Stage 2/3 equipment)

Industrial and research bakers measure all of this. You will not own these in Stage 1, but you should know what the numbers on a flour specification sheet mean.

| Instrument | What it does | What it reports | Tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Farinograph** (Brabender) | Records mixing torque as flour and water are mixed | Water absorption (to reach a set consistency, 500 BU), development time, stability, mixing tolerance | How much water a flour needs, how long to mix, how tolerant it is to overmixing |
| **Mixolab** (Chopin) | Mixing torque while heating and cooling the dough | Protein behaviour plus starch gelatinization and enzyme activity | Mixing and baking behaviour in one test |
| **Alveograph** (Chopin) | Blows a bubble from a sheet of dough until it bursts | P (tenacity), L (extensibility), W (energy), P/L ratio | Mimics gas-cell expansion; common in French and European flour specs |
| **Extensograph** (Brabender) | Stretches a rested dough cylinder after 45, 90, 135 min | Resistance to extension, extensibility, area (energy) | How dough relaxes over time; response to oxidizers |
| **Rheometer** (oscillatory) | Applies tiny oscillating deformation | G′ (storage, elastic), G″ (loss, viscous), tan δ = G″/G′ | Fundamental viscoelastic properties; research and formulation |
| **Texture analyser** | Pushes, pulls or cuts with a controlled probe | Force vs distance curves | Firmness, stickiness, extensibility (Kieffer rig), crumb softness |

### Home proxies

| Test | What it approximates | How |
|---|---|---|
| Windowpane | Gluten development and extensibility | Stretch a small piece thin enough to see light through without tearing |
| Poke test | Proof level, remaining elasticity | Flour a finger, press 1 cm; note how fast and how fully the dent springs back ([X1-09](experiments/X1-09-proof-level.md)) |
| Stretch test | Resistance vs extensibility | Pull a 50 g strip slowly; note length at tear and how hard it pulls back |
| Slump test | Viscous flow | Round a 200 g ball, trace its outline, re-trace after 30 min; measure diameter increase |
| Line-spread test | Batter or cream viscosity | Fill a ring on a plate marked with concentric circles, lift, read spread after 60 s |

## What it predicts on the bench

| If you change... | Prediction | Why | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skip the bench rest before final shaping | Tearing, uneven shape, tight spots | Unrelaxed elastic stress | [03.4](stage-1/module-03/lesson-04.md) |
| Add salt late (after autolyse) | Dough visibly tightens within a minute | Salt increases gluten resistance | [02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md), [X1-06](experiments/X1-06-salt.md) |
| Raise hydration by 10 points | Slacker, stickier dough, spreads more, more open crumb | Water plasticizes gluten; lower viscosity | [X1-04](experiments/X1-04-hydration-series.md) |
| Roll a tart shell and bake without resting | Shrinks down the ring sides | Elastic recovery in the oven | [X1-32](experiments/X1-32-tart-shrinkage.md), [11.2](stage-1/module-11/lesson-02.md) |
| Stir muffin batter until smooth | Tough, tunnelled muffins | Gluten development makes the batter elastic | [X1-22](experiments/X1-22-muffin-overmixing.md), [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md) |
| Use a weak (low-protein) flour for a long-proofed loaf | Spreads, flat loaf, coarse holes | Less strain hardening; cells coalesce | [X1-03](experiments/X1-03-gluten-wash.md), [02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md) |

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Relaxation clock (30 minutes).** Roll two 150 g pieces of the same dough to 20 cm; cut one immediately into a 10 cm square, the other after 20 minutes' rest. Measure each square 10 minutes after cutting. The unrested square shrinks noticeably more.
2. **Salt snap.** Mix two 100 g flour doughs at 65 % hydration, one with 2 g salt and one without. After 10 minutes, stretch each slowly: the salted dough pulls back harder and feels less sticky.
3. **Oobleck and cream.** Mix cornstarch and water about 1.5:1; punch it (solid) then let your finger sink slowly (liquid): shear-thickening. Compare with whipped cream, which thins when you stir it: shear-thinning.

## Misconceptions

- **"Strong dough means a lot of elasticity."** Strength in practice means *balanced* resistance and extensibility plus strain hardening. A very elastic, inextensible dough makes a small, tight loaf.
- **"A dough that shrinks back needs a different flour."** Usually it needs rest. Elastic stress relaxes in 10–30 minutes.
- **"Thicker batter always makes a better cake."** Viscosity must be high enough to hold small bubbles but low enough to flow and let the batter rise before setting.
- **"Rheology is only for industry."** Every hand test you do (windowpane, poke, slump, piped ridge) is a crude rheological measurement.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Is gluten developed? | Windowpane test | Stage 1 |
| How much does the dough flow? | Slump test with a ruler | Stage 1 |
| Is the batter or cream at the target consistency? | Line-spread test on a marked plate; timed flow from a funnel | Stage 1 |
| How much water does this flour need; how tolerant is it? | Farinograph or Mixolab data on the supplier's spec sheet | Stage 2 (read spec sheets) |
| How extensible vs strong is this flour? | Alveograph W and P/L; extensograph | Stage 2 (read) / Stage 3 (run) |
| What are G′, G″ of my filling or dough? | Oscillatory rheometer | Stage 3 |
| How firm is my crumb day 1 vs day 5? | Texture analyser | Stage 3 |

## Check yourself

1. Your pita dough shrinks back every time you roll it. What is happening and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The gluten holds unrelaxed elastic stress from dividing and rounding. Cover the pieces and rest 10–20 minutes; the stress relaxes and the dough rolls out and stays.

</details>

2. Predict how adding 10 % butter to a lean dough changes its resistance and extensibility.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Resistance falls and extensibility rises: fat coats and lubricates gluten strands, giving a silkier, softer dough and a more tender, finer crumb.

</details>

3. Why do small bubbles survive in a creamed cake batter while large ones escape?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Rise speed grows with the square of bubble radius and falls with viscosity. Small bubbles rise so slowly in a viscous batter that the batter sets before they reach the surface.

</details>

4. A supplier offers two flours: alveograph W 180, P/L 0.5 and W 320, P/L 1.2. Which would you choose for a long-fermented pan loaf, and which for a crisp cracker or a tart dough?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The W 320 flour (stronger, more tenacious) for the long-fermented loaf, which needs gas retention and tolerance. The W 180, extensible flour (low P/L) for crackers and tart dough, where you want easy sheeting and low shrinkage.

</details>

5. Why does a Chantilly rosette hold its shape at 4 °C but slump at 25 °C?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Its structure (partly coalesced fat around air bubbles) gives a yield stress that depends on solid milk fat. Warming melts fat crystals, the yield stress falls below the stress from the rosette's own weight, and it flows.

</details>
