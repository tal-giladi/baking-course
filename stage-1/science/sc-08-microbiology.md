## The one-paragraph model

A bakery is full of microbes, and you want exactly two kinds to grow: baker's yeast and, in sourdough, lactic acid bacteria. Yeast is a single-celled fungus that eats simple sugars and, in the oxygen-poor interior of a dough, turns them into carbon dioxide (the leavening gas) and ethanol plus a range of flavour compounds. Its speed depends steeply on temperature: roughly doubling for every 8–10 °C between 10 and 35 °C, stalling in the refrigerator and dying at about 55–60 °C in the oven. Lactic acid bacteria make acid (and, depending on the species, CO2, ethanol and acetic acid); in a sourdough they outnumber yeast about a hundred to one and set the flavour and pH. Everything else is an unwanted guest: moulds and rope bacteria spoil product, and pathogens (Salmonella, pathogenic E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria) make people ill. You control all of them with the same four levers: **temperature, time, water activity and acidity**, plus hygiene that keeps them out in the first place.

## The mechanism

### The yeast cell

Baker's yeast is *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, an oval cell about 5–10 µm long. A gram of fresh (compressed) yeast holds on the order of ten billion cells, ~70 % water. It reproduces by budding: a daughter cell grows from the mother and pinches off. In a normal bread fermentation there is some growth, but most of the gas comes from the cells you added, which is why yeast quantity sets fermentation speed so directly ([X1-08](experiments/X1-08-yeast-quantity.md)).

| Form | Moisture | Relative dose (same gas production) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh (compressed) | ~70 % | 1 | Refrigerate, use within ~2 weeks of opening; crumbles, smells clean |
| Active dry | ~7–8 % | ~0.4–0.5 × fresh | Larger granules; traditionally rehydrated in warm water |
| Instant dry | ~4–6 % | ~0.33–0.4 × fresh | Mix straight into flour; keeps for months sealed |
| Osmotolerant instant | ~4–6 % | as instant | For doughs above ~10 % sugar on flour |

These conversions are standard professional practice; suppliers state their own.

### What yeast eats

Yeast uses glucose and fructose directly. It splits sucrose outside the cell with the enzyme **invertase** (within minutes of mixing) and takes up **maltose**, which is released from starch by flour amylases ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)). It cannot use lactose, so milk sugar stays in the dough and browns the crust. In a lean dough the few percent of free sugars in flour are used first; long fermentations depend on the steady maltose supply from damaged starch.

### Respiration versus fermentation

Both routes start with **glycolysis**: one glucose (6 carbons) is split into two pyruvate (3 carbons each), releasing a little energy.

| Route | Needs oxygen? | Overall | Energy yield per glucose | What the baker sees |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiration | Yes | glucose + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O | High (~15× fermentation) | Yeast grows fast (industrial yeast production is aerated) |
| Alcoholic fermentation | No | glucose → 2 ethanol + 2 CO2 | Low (2 ATP) | Gas, ethanol and flavour in dough |

A dough becomes anaerobic within minutes of mixing: yeast and flour enzymes use up the trapped oxygen quickly. Baker's yeast also ferments preferentially whenever sugar is plentiful even if oxygen is present (the Crabtree effect), so in practice **all bread leavening is fermentation**. By mass, 180 g of glucose becomes ~92 g ethanol and ~88 g CO2. Ethanol evaporates in the oven (boiling point ~78 °C, [SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)); some of it, with esters, higher alcohols and acids produced alongside, stays as flavour.

### Temperature response of yeast

| Dough temperature | What yeast does | Bench use |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 °C | Nearly dormant; gas production very slow but not zero | Retarding shaped loaves and croissants; the dough still moves over 12–48 h |
| 10–20 °C | Slow; flavour builds relative to gas | Overnight bulk, cool preferments |
| 24–27 °C | Controlled, predictable | Standard desired dough temperature (DDT) for lean bread |
| 32–38 °C | Maximum gas production rate | Fast proofs; flavour is thinner, timing window narrow |
| > 40 °C | Stress; rate falls | Avoid proofing hotter than ~35 °C with butter-laminated doughs anyway (butter melts ~32 °C) |
| 50–60 °C | Cells die in the oven | End of oven spring's yeast contribution |

Rule of thumb from practice: each ~8–10 °C drop roughly halves fermentation speed in the 10–30 °C range, which is why a dough at 20 °C takes about twice as long as one at 27 °C. You measure this in [X1-07](experiments/X1-07-fermentation-temperature.md).

Other stresses: **salt** slows yeast (at 2 % on flour, noticeably; above 2.5–3 %, severely); **sugar** above ~5 % on flour slows it through osmotic pressure, and above ~10 % you switch to osmotolerant yeast or more yeast; **cinnamon** and some spices inhibit it; very **acidic** doughs (pH below ~4) slow commercial yeast more than sourdough wild yeasts.

### Lactic acid bacteria and sourdough ecology

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are small rod- or sphere-shaped bacteria that turn sugars into lactic acid. They are tolerant of acid, so as they lower the pH they suppress most competitors.

| Type | Products from glucose | Examples in sourdough | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homofermentative | ~2 lactic acid | *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* | Fast acidification, mild clean sourness |
| Heterofermentative | lactic acid + CO2 + ethanol or acetic acid | *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, *Limosilactobacillus* species | Some gas, sharper acetic notes, more complex flavour |

In a mature levain, LAB typically number around 10^8–10^9 per gram and yeasts around 10^6–10^7 per gram; the ratio of roughly 100:1 is stable for as long as the feeding routine is stable [Arora 2021]. The yeasts are often not *S. cerevisiae* but acid-tolerant species (for example *Kazachstania humilis*) that cannot use maltose, which leaves maltose to *F. sanfranciscensis*: they avoid competing. A ripe levain sits around pH 3.5–4.2 ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)).

The community is shaped by what you do, not by the city it lives in. Levers, as rules of thumb from bakery practice:

| Lever | Direction | Tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Warmer (~28–32 °C) | Faster, more lactic, milder |
| Temperature | Cooler (~18–22 °C) | Slower, relatively more acetic, sharper |
| Hydration | Stiff levain (50–60 %) | More acetic, slower |
| Feeding ratio | Larger (1:5:5 or more) | Less acid carried over, longer time to peak |
| Flour | Wholegrain, rye | More food and minerals, faster, more acid |

You track rise and pH across feeding regimes in [X1-14](experiments/X1-14-levain.md).

### Spoilage: moulds and rope

**Moulds** (*Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*, *Rhizopus* and others) do not survive baking. Mould on bread is almost always post-bake contamination from air, slicers, boards and hands during cooling and packing. It grows at aw above ~0.80 ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)), so fresh bread (aw ~0.95) moulds in days; faster in a sealed bag in a warm kitchen, where condensation adds free water at the surface.

**Rope** is a spoilage of the *inside* of the loaf by *Bacillus* species (mainly *B. subtilis* and *B. licheniformis*). Their spores come in on flour and survive baking, because the crumb only reaches ~96–99 °C. In a warm (above ~25 °C), moist loaf they germinate and secrete enzymes that break down starch and protein: after 1–3 days the crumb smells of overripe melon or rotting fruit, turns sticky and pulls into fine threads when broken. Rope is favoured by slow cooling, warm storage, high pH and wholemeal or bran-rich doughs. Acidity prevents it: sourdough bread rarely ropes, and industrial bakeries add vinegar or propionate [Smith 2004].

### Pathogens that matter in a bakery

| Pathogen | Where it enters | What controls it | Key fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Salmonella* | Raw and undercooked eggs; raw flour; cross-contamination | Cooking egg dishes to 71 °C (160 °F); pasteurized eggs for uncooked uses; refrigeration of eggs at ≤ 5 °C [FDA-eggs] | Mousse, tiramisu, raw-yolk sabayon and uncooked buttercreams need pasteurized eggs or a cooked method (Italian meringue, pâte à bombe) |
| Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*, *Salmonella* | Raw flour, including in raw dough and batter [FDA-flour] | Do not eat raw dough or batter; heat-treat flour for no-bake uses; wash hands and surfaces after handling flour | Flour is a raw agricultural product, not a sterile one: its low aw keeps pathogens alive without letting them grow |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | Hands, noses, skin cuts, into cream fillings, pastry cream, custards | Hygiene, gloves over cuts, fast cooling, cold holding | Its toxin is **heat-stable**: reheating a filling that was abused does not make it safe. Grows down to aw ~0.86 |
| *Listeria monocytogenes* | Unpasteurized dairy, soft cheeses, contaminated fridges and drains | Pasteurized dairy, clean fridges, short cold-storage life | Grows slowly **at refrigerator temperature** — the fridge is not a pause button for it |
| *Bacillus cereus* | Spores in flour, starches, rice | Fast cooling of cooked starchy fillings | Like rope, spores survive cooking |

### The danger zone and cooling

Most pathogens grow between about **5 °C and 60 °C**, fastest at 25–45 °C, where the fastest can double every 20–30 minutes. Standard food-safety practice for cooked, moist fillings such as pastry cream and custard is a two-stage cool: from 60 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, then to 5 °C or below within the next 4 hours. In a bakery this means shallow trays, cling film pressed onto the surface, and an ice bath or blast chiller rather than a deep bowl on the bench. Hold at ≤ 5 °C and use pastry cream within about 2–3 days (standard professional practice).

### Why some products keep

A cookie, a meringue, a jam or a rich fruit cake keeps because microbes need water they cannot get: below aw ~0.90 most bacteria stop, below ~0.80 most moulds stop, below ~0.60 nothing grows. Sugar and salt lower aw ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)); acid lowers pH ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)); baking kills vegetative cells; packaging stops recontamination. Shelf life is the sum of these hurdles [Smith 2004].

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Balloon fermenter (15 minutes plus 1 hour).** Three bottles, each with 100 g water, 10 g sugar and 3 g instant yeast, in ~15 °C, ~30 °C and ~45 °C water baths; stretch a balloon over each neck and measure circumference every 15 minutes. The 30 °C bottle wins; 45 °C starts fast and fades. Repeat at 30 °C with 2 g salt or 40 g extra sugar added: both slow the gas.
2. **Mould is post-bake.** Split one loaf: seal half in a bag straight from the oven while warm, the other half after full cooling, handled with clean hands. Keep both at room temperature and date the first visible mould.

## Misconceptions

- **"Yeast needs sugar added to work."** Flour enzymes release maltose from starch continuously; a lean dough with no added sugar ferments fine. Added sugar mainly speeds the start and, above ~5 %, slows the yeast.
- **"Refrigeration kills bacteria and freezing sterilizes."** Cold slows most microbes and freezing stops growth; neither kills reliably. *Listeria* grows in the fridge.
- **"Baking makes everything safe."** Baking kills vegetative cells, but spores (rope, *B. cereus*) survive, *S. aureus* toxin survives, and anything added after baking (cream, fruit, glaze) is unprotected.
- **"Sourdough gets its character from the local air."** The starter's population is mostly selected by the flour, temperature, hydration and feeding routine [Arora 2021].

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Is fermentation on schedule? | Dough temperature (probe thermometer), volume in a straight-sided container | Stage 1 |
| How active is my levain? | Rise to peak in a marked jar; time to peak | Stage 1 |
| How acidic is the levain or dough? | pH strips (approximate) → pH meter with a spear probe | Stage 1 strips; meter Stage 1–2 |
| Is my fridge cold enough? | Fridge thermometer, checked daily | Stage 1 |
| Did the filling cool fast enough? | Probe thermometer readings logged at 0, 2 and 6 h | Stage 1 |
| How much gas does this yeast/flour make? | Risograph or fermentometer | Stage 3 |
| Which microbes are in my starter? How many? | Plate counts, microscope; sequencing via a lab | Stage 3 |
| Is this product safe for its shelf life? | aw meter, pH meter, challenge tests by an accredited lab | Stage 3 |

## Check yourself

1. Your kitchen is 20 °C instead of the usual 26 °C. The formula says bulk 2 h. What do you expect, and name two ways to recover the schedule.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Six degrees cooler cuts the fermentation rate to roughly 60–65 % of normal, so the same rise takes ~1.5–1.6× as long: expect ~3–3.5 h. Raise the dough temperature with warmer water (recompute DDT), increase the yeast by ~50 %, or ferment in a warmer spot such as an oven with only the light on (monitor the dough temperature).

</details>

2. You flush a container of freshly mixed dough with nitrogen to remove all oxygen. Predict the effect on gas production.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Almost none. Dough is anaerobic within minutes of mixing anyway; yeast ferments sugars to CO2 and ethanol without oxygen. (Oxygen does matter for gluten chemistry during mixing, not for leavening.)

</details>

3. A pastry cream was left on the counter overnight. A colleague proposes to boil it again to "make it safe". Why is that not acceptable?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

*S. aureus* can have grown and produced toxin during the hours in the danger zone. The toxin is heat-stable and survives boiling. Discard it.

</details>

4. A wholemeal sandwich loaf develops a fruity smell and stringy, sticky crumb after two days in a warm kitchen. What happened, and what one formula change prevents it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Rope: *Bacillus* spores from flour survived baking and grew in the warm, moist, near-neutral crumb. Acidify the dough, for example with a portion of sourdough or a small addition of vinegar, and cool and store the bread cooler.

</details>

5. Why is raw cookie dough a food-safety issue even when made with pasteurized eggs?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Raw flour can carry *E. coli* and *Salmonella* [FDA-flour]. Only heat-treated flour makes a no-bake dough safer.

</details>
