## The one-paragraph model

Almost all the brown colour and roasted flavour in baking comes from two families of reactions. The **Maillard reaction** is a reaction between a **reducing sugar** and an **amino group** (from proteins or free amino acids); it produces hundreds of flavour compounds and brown pigments, and it becomes fast in the bakery once a surface is dry and above roughly 140 °C. **Caramelization** is sugar alone breaking down under heat; it needs higher temperatures for sucrose (~160–180 °C) but starts much lower for fructose (~110 °C). Both go faster with more heat and more time; Maillard also goes faster at higher pH and needs some, but not much, water. So you control colour with four levers: what sugars and proteins are on the surface, how dry the surface is, how hot it gets, and its pH. Very dark browning of starchy foods also forms **acrylamide**, a food-safety reason to stop at golden brown rather than dark brown.

## The mechanism

### The Maillard reaction

The start is simple; the rest is a cascade:

1. The carbonyl group of a reducing sugar (glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose — not sucrose, see [SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)) reacts with a free amino group, especially from the amino acid lysine and from free amino acids released by fermentation and enzymes.
2. The product rearranges into intermediates (Amadori or Heyns products) — still colourless.
3. These break down along many pathways into reactive fragments. One important side route, **Strecker degradation**, converts amino acids into aldehydes that carry malty, nutty, chocolatey and baked-bread aromas.
4. Fragments recombine into flavour compounds (pyrazines for roasted and nutty, furans for caramel-like, and many more) and eventually into large brown polymers called **melanoidins**.

Which sugars and amino acids you start with shapes the flavour: milk (lactose + milk protein) gives a different crust note from egg or from a long-fermented dough rich in free amino acids.

### What controls Maillard speed

| Factor | Effect | Bakery consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Rate rises steeply with temperature (often roughly doubling per 10 °C). Slow below 100 °C, visible from ~120 °C, fast from ~140–150 °C | Surface must get well above 100 °C to brown in a normal bake time |
| Water | Needs a little water; slowed at very low aw and diluted at high aw. Fastest at intermediate aw (~0.6–0.8) | A wet surface is capped at ~100 °C and too dilute; the crust must dry first ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)) |
| pH | Faster at higher (alkaline) pH; slower in acid | Soda-leavened cookies brown more; pretzel lye dip; sourdough crusts need more heat to colour ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)) |
| Reducing sugar available | More reducing sugar → faster | Honey, invert, glucose, milk and malt darken crusts; overfermented dough has less residual sugar → pale crust |
| Amino groups available | More protein / free amino acids → faster | Egg wash, milk, milk powder; long fermentation releases amino acids |
| Time | Colour keeps building | Longer bake at lower temperature can match colour but dries more |

### Caramelization

Caramelization is sugar decomposing when heated alone (or in a concentrated syrup): molecules lose water, split into fragments and repolymerize into brown caramel pigments and flavours (buttery diacetyl, sweet-roasted furans, bitter compounds as it darkens). Sugar melts, then colours.

| Sugar | Approximate onset of caramelization |
|---|---|
| Fructose | ~110 °C |
| Glucose | ~160 °C |
| Sucrose | ~160–180 °C (decomposes around its melting point; begins inverting first) |
| Maltose | ~180 °C |

Typical sucrose caramel stages (standard pastry practice; judge by colour as well as thermometer because readings drift in small batches):

| Temperature | Colour | Flavour | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~160 °C (320 °F) | Pale straw | Sweet, faintly caramel | Spun sugar, light glazes |
| ~165–175 °C (330–345 °F) | Light to medium amber | Rounded caramel | Caramel sauce, crème caramel |
| ~175–185 °C (345–365 °F) | Deep amber | Complex, slightly bitter | Dark caramel for mousses and sauces |
| > ~190 °C (375 °F) | Very dark brown | Bitter, burnt | Usually too far |

Both acid and alkali speed caramelization; acid also inverts sucrose, which is why a squeeze of lemon changes both colour speed and crystallization in a caramel ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md), [R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Molten sugar at 160–190 °C sticks to skin and keeps burning. Keep a bowl of ice water nearby, use a deep pan, add liquids (cream, butter) off the heat and slowly, and stand back from the steam burst.

</div>

### Maillard vs caramelization side by side

| | Maillard | Caramelization |
|---|---|---|
| Reactants | Reducing sugar + amino group | Sugar only |
| Typical bakery temperature | Noticeable above ~140 °C at the surface; slowly lower | ~160 °C+ for sucrose; ~110 °C for fructose |
| pH | Faster when alkaline | Faster at both extremes |
| Water | Needs a little; fastest at intermediate aw | Happens in melt or concentrated syrup |
| Flavour | Savoury-roasted, bready, nutty, malty, toasty | Sweet, buttery, then bitter |
| Where | Bread crust, cookies, cake surface, brown butter, toasted nuts, dulce de leche (slow, at lower temperature) | Caramel, sugar crust on crème brûlée, sugar-topped pastries |

In a cookie or a sugar-rich crust both happen at once; you rarely need to separate them on the bench, but separating them helps you choose the lever.

### Where browning happens in a bake

A dough surface in a 230 °C oven goes through three phases ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)):

| Phase | Surface temperature | What's happening |
|---|---|---|
| Wet surface (steam phase) | ≤ ~100 °C | Evaporation holds the surface at or below boiling; gelatinized starch on the surface (from steam) forms a thin glossy skin; no browning |
| Drying | 100 → 150 °C | A dry layer forms; Maillard begins |
| Browning | ~150–190 °C+ | Colour and flavour develop quickly; can overshoot to bitter |

The interior of a loaf stays near 96–99 °C at the end of the bake and never browns. That is why crust and crumb taste so different.

### Acrylamide, a food-safety note

Acrylamide forms in the Maillard reaction when the amino acid **asparagine** reacts with reducing sugars at high temperature (above ~120 °C) and low moisture. It concentrates in dark crusts, crackers, biscuits, toast and fried potatoes. It is classified as a probable human carcinogen, and food regulators, including in the EU, expect producers to keep levels low. In Stage 1 the practical rules are:

- Bake and toast to **golden, not dark brown**, especially thin, dry products.
- Very high reducing sugar (honey, fructose, invert) on a dry high-temperature product raises acrylamide; so does ammonium bicarbonate as a leavener in biscuits.
- Industry uses the enzyme asparaginase and lower pH to cut formation; that is a Stage 3 topic.

Colour target and food safety point the same way: a well-developed crust does not need to be black to taste good.

## Controlling colour

| Lever | Darker / faster | Lighter / slower |
|---|---|---|
| Egg wash | Whole egg + yolk, or yolk + cream (protein + fat + some sugar); two coats | Egg white only (shine, little colour); water |
| Milk | Milk or milk-powder wash; milk in the dough (lactose is not fermented by baker's yeast, so it stays for browning) | Water-based dough, no wash |
| Sugar | More sugar, or reducing sugars (honey, invert, glucose, malt) | Less sugar; sucrose only |
| pH | Pinch of soda, alkaline dip (pretzels) | Acidic dough (sourdough), cream of tartar |
| Oven temperature | Higher; convection fan on; top element | Lower; tent with foil; pan on a lower rack |
| Fermentation | Underfermented dough has more residual sugar → darker crust | Overfermented dough is pale (yeast ate the sugar) |
| Steam | Venting after the steam phase lets the crust dry and colour | Prolonged steam delays colour |
| Pan | Dark metal, thin pan | Light, shiny metal; insulated or doubled pans |
| Product thickness | Thin edges brown first | Thick, wet centres |

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Four toasts.** Brush four slices of the same white bread with: water, milk, a 1 % baking-soda solution (1 g in 100 g water), and beaten egg. Toast them together under the grill or at 220 °C for the same time. Rank the colour. Protein, lactose and pH each move the result.
2. **Dry-pan sugar ladder.** In a small heavy pan melt 100 g sugar over medium heat with a probe thermometer. Drip a little onto baking paper at 160, 170, 180 and 190 °C. Label, cool and taste: sweet → caramel → bitter. (Safety callout above.)
3. **Slow Maillard.** Pour 200 g sweetened condensed milk into a small glass dish, cover it with foil, and set it in a larger pan of hot water in a ~200 °C oven for 1.5–2 hours, topping up the water and stirring every 30 minutes. The milk sits near 100 °C yet turns into caramel-brown dulce de leche: Maillard browning, just slow. (Do not heat an unopened can; it can burst.)

## Misconceptions

- **"Browning is caramelization."** In bread, cookies and cakes most of the colour and flavour is Maillard, which needs protein and reducing sugars and happens well below sucrose's caramelization range.
- **"A hotter oven just browns faster."** It also changes where the heat goes: the crust colours before the interior sets, giving a dark outside and underbaked inside ([SC-10](stage-1/science/sc-10-heat-transfer.md)).
- **"Sucrose browns crusts."** Pure sucrose is not a reducing sugar. It contributes after yeast invertase or acid splits it, or when it caramelizes at high surface temperature.
- **"Darker is always more flavourful."** Past deep golden-brown, bitter and burnt compounds and acrylamide rise; flavour peaks before the colour does.

## Check yourself

1. A lean baguette comes out pale after the full bake time. Name three possible causes linked to browning chemistry.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Overproofed dough (little residual sugar), oven too cool or steam left in too long (surface never dried and got hot enough), dough very acidic (long, sour preferment), or an oven with weak top heat.

</details>

2. You add baking soda to a cookie formula that previously had only baking powder. Predict colour and flavour changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Darker and faster browning with more roasted, toffee-like notes, because higher pH accelerates Maillard; also more spread. Excess soda would add a soapy note.

</details>

3. Why can dulce de leche brown at ~100 °C while bread crumb, also around 100 °C for a long time, stays white?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Condensed milk is rich in reducing sugar (lactose, and sucrose that partly inverts), milk protein, and relatively little free water, and it is held there for hours. Bread crumb is wet (aw ~0.96, diluting reactants), low in reducing sugar and only spends minutes near 100 °C.

</details>

4. Your caramel reads 170 °C on the thermometer but looks nearly black at the edges. What do you trust, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Trust the colour. Small batches heat unevenly and a probe measures only one spot; edges and the thin layer on the pan run hotter. Swirl the pan to even out heat and check the thermometer's calibration.

</details>
