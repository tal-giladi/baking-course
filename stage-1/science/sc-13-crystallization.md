## The one-paragraph model

A crystal is molecules stacked in a regular, repeating pattern. Crystals form in two steps: **nucleation** (the first tiny ordered cluster appears) and **growth** (more molecules join existing crystals). Both need **supersaturation**: more dissolved (or melted) material than the liquid can hold at that temperature. The confectioner's whole art is controlling these two steps. **Many nuclei at once** give many tiny crystals: smooth fondant, snappy tempered chocolate. **Few nuclei and time to grow** give few large crystals: gritty fudge, grainy caramel. **No nuclei at all**, helped by "interfering agents" that get in the way, gives no crystals: clear hard candy and smooth caramel. Cocoa butter adds a twist: it can crystallize in six different forms, and only one (form V) gives glossy, snappy chocolate that shrinks from the mould and melts cleanly in the mouth. Tempering is the procedure that makes form V and only form V.

## The mechanism

### Solubility and supersaturation

Sucrose is very soluble, and more so when hot.

| Temperature | Sucrose that dissolves in 100 g water (approx.) | Concentration (sugar % of solution) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 °C | ~200 g | ~67 % |
| 50 °C | ~260 g | ~72 % |
| 100 °C | ~480 g | ~83 % |

(Standard solubility values; see [McGee].) A syrup boiled to soft ball (~85 % sugar, [SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)) and cooled to 40 °C holds far more sugar than it can at that temperature: it is strongly **supersaturated**. It "wants" to crystallize, but nucleation has an energy barrier, so a clean, undisturbed supersaturated syrup can stay clear for a surprisingly long time. Then one trigger sets it off.

### Nucleation and growth

| Trigger | Effect | Kitchen situation |
|---|---|---|
| **Seed crystals** | Existing crystals skip nucleation; growth starts immediately | Undissolved sugar on the pot wall; a sugary spoon; a crumb of fondant |
| **Agitation** | Stirring or beating creates many nuclei | Stirring caramel; beating fondant |
| **Foreign particles, rough surfaces** | Surfaces lower the nucleation barrier | Dust, a scratched pot, a grain of salt |
| **High supersaturation** | Many nuclei form at once | Cooling syrup well before beating |
| **High temperature with low supersaturation** | Few nuclei, fast growth | Stirring hot syrup: few large crystals, grainy |

The key relationship: **crystal size depends on how many nuclei form relative to how much material is available to grow on them.** Many nuclei share the sugar and each stays tiny. The tongue detects crystals larger than roughly 20–30 µm as gritty, so smooth confections need crystals below that.

### Sugar: controlled versus prevented crystallization

| Product | Goal | How it is done |
|---|---|---|
| **Fondant, fudge** | Controlled: many tiny crystals | Cook to soft ball (~115–118 °C); cool **undisturbed** to ~40–50 °C (high supersaturation, viscous syrup); then beat continuously so that many nuclei form at once. It turns opaque and creamy |
| **Hard candy, pulled sugar, isomalt work** | Prevented: an amorphous glass | Cook to hard crack (~150 °C) with glucose syrup; do not stir; cool fast. The syrup becomes too viscous for molecules to organize |
| **Caramel, caramel sauce** | Prevented | Clean pot, wash down the walls with a wet brush or lid briefly (steam dissolves crystals); add glucose syrup or a little acid; do not stir a wet caramel until it colours ([R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)) |
| **Italian meringue syrup** | Prevented (in the pot) | Same hygiene; the syrup is diluted into whites before it can crystallize |
| **Crystallized (candied) fruit, sugared nuts, sanded rims** | Deliberate coarse crystals | Seed and grow on purpose |

### Interfering agents

Interfering agents are substances that sit between sucrose molecules and make it harder for them to line up.

| Agent | How it interferes | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose syrup (corn syrup) | Glucose and long dextrin chains, different shapes; raise viscosity | 10–30 % of the sugar in caramel, candy, sorbet; standard professional practice |
| Invert sugar, honey | Glucose and fructose, different molecules | Fondant softness, ganache, cake shelf life |
| Acid (cream of tartar, lemon juice) | Inverts part of the sucrose during boiling, producing interfering glucose and fructose | A pinch in syrups and fondant |
| Fat (butter, cream) | Coats crystals, physically separates molecules | Fudge, caramel sauce, toffee |
| Proteins (milk solids) | Adsorb on crystal surfaces, raise viscosity | Fudge, dulce de leche |

Too much interfering agent is its own failure: fondant never sets, caramel stays sticky and absorbs humidity ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)).

### Cocoa butter polymorphism

Cocoa butter is a mixture of triglycerides with a narrow melting range, and those molecules can pack into six crystal forms (**polymorphs**), numbered I to VI in order of increasing stability and melting point.

| Form | Approx. melting point | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| I | ~17 °C | Unstable; forms in rapid, deep cooling |
| II | ~23 °C | Unstable |
| III | ~25.5 °C | Unstable; soft, dull |
| IV | ~27.5 °C | Unstable; soft, dull, bloom-prone; what chocolate left to set on its own usually forms |
| **V** | **~33–34 °C** | **Desired: gloss, snap, contraction (releases from moulds), melts just below mouth temperature** |
| VI | ~36 °C | Most stable; forms slowly from V over months in storage; associated with bloom and a harder, less pleasant melt |

(Values follow the standard numbering in [Beckett]; reported melting points vary by a degree or so.) Untempered chocolate that is simply melted and cooled forms a mixture of unstable forms, sets slowly, stays soft and dull, and blooms within days.

### The tempering curve logic

The goal is to end with a fluid chocolate containing a small number of form V crystals and nothing else, so that as it sets, form V templates the rest of the fat.

| Step | Dark chocolate | Milk chocolate | White chocolate | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Melt completely | 45–50 °C | 40–45 °C | 40–45 °C | Erase all crystal memory |
| 2. Cool (with movement) | 27–28 °C | 26–27 °C | 25–26 °C | Supersaturate; form crystals (a mix of IV and V) |
| 3. Rewarm to working temperature | 31–32 °C | 29–30 °C | 28–29 °C | Melt forms IV and lower; only V survives |

These temperatures are standard professional ranges; follow the manufacturer's curve printed on the pack, because milk fat and formulation shift them. Milk fat interferes with cocoa butter crystallization ("eutectic" softening), which is why milk and white chocolate temper lower.

**Seeding** replaces step 2: melt to 45–50 °C, then add 20–30 % finely chopped *already tempered* chocolate (which is full of form V) and stir until it cools to the working temperature ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)). The seed provides the form V nuclei directly.

**Checking temper:** dip the tip of a knife or a strip of parchment and leave it at ~20 °C. Well-tempered chocolate sets in about 3–5 minutes, evenly glossy, with a clean snap. Streaky or soft after 10 minutes means it is out of temper.

Working chocolate thickens over time as more crystals form ("over-crystallized"); warm it by a degree or add a little warm untempered chocolate. Exceeding the working temperature by more than ~1–2 °C melts the form V seeds, and you must temper again.

### Bloom: fat versus sugar

| | Fat bloom | Sugar bloom |
|---|---|---|
| Looks | Grey-white, soft haze or streaks | Dry, white, dusty or rough surface |
| Touch | Greasy; melts away with a warm finger | Dry, gritty; does not smear |
| Water test | Unaffected by a drop of water | A drop of water dissolves it |
| Cause | Poor temper; storage too warm or with temperature swings; form V → VI; liquid fat migrating from nut fillings | Condensation (fridge to warm room) dissolves surface sugar; it recrystallizes as the water evaporates |
| Prevention | Correct temper, stable 15–18 °C storage, barrier layers | Store dry; bring cold chocolate to room temperature in its wrapping |

Both are cosmetic, not a safety issue.

### Other crystals in pastry

- **Starch retrogradation** is crystallization of starch chains in stale bread ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)).
- **Fat crystals in butter and shortening** (alpha, beta-prime, beta forms) set plasticity and creaming ability ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)).
- **Honey** crystallizes because it is supersaturated in glucose; creamed honey is seeded, controlled crystallization.
- **Ice crystals.** In frozen desserts, small crystals feel smooth and large ones icy. Fast freezing with agitation makes many small crystals; sugar lowers the freezing point so part of the water stays unfrozen; temperature swings in the freezer melt small crystals and regrow large ones. This is the core of Stage 2 ice cream work.

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Seeding a syrup (30 minutes).** Boil 150 g sugar with 50 g water to 115 °C. Pour into two small heatproof glasses and let them cool undisturbed to ~50 °C. Drop a few grains of granulated sugar into one. Within minutes it clouds and sets; the other stays clear much longer. Hot syrup burns; handle carefully.
2. **Fondant vs grit.** Split another 115 °C syrup: beat half immediately while hot, let the other cool undisturbed to ~45 °C before beating. Rub each between tongue and palate: grainy vs creamy.
3. **Knife-tip temper test.** Melt 100 g dark chocolate to 45 °C, let a smear set on parchment (untempered). Then seed the rest with 30 g chopped chocolate to 31–32 °C and smear again. Compare after 5 minutes and after 24 h.

## Misconceptions

- **"Crystallized sugar means I overcooked it."** Temperature determines concentration; crystallization is triggered by seeds and agitation. A syrup at the right temperature can still seize.
- **"Tempering is about the final temperature."** It is about which crystal forms exist. A chocolate at 31 °C that was never cooled to form crystals is not tempered.
- **"Bloom means the chocolate is spoiled."** It is a crystal or sugar surface effect; the chocolate is safe and can be melted and retempered.
- **"More glucose syrup is always safer."** Excess interfering agent gives sticky, humidity-sensitive, never-setting products.

| Question | Instrument | Stage |

| How gritty is it? | Tongue test against a reference; later, a micrometer or grind gauge for particle size | Stage 1 / Stage 2 |

## Check yourself

1. Why does fondant need to be cooled to ~45 °C *before* beating?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Cooling raises supersaturation and viscosity without triggering crystals. When beating begins, a huge number of nuclei form at once and each crystal stays tiny (below ~20–30 µm), which feels creamy. Beating hot forms few nuclei that grow large.

</details>

2. Your dark chocolate was tempered at 31.5 °C. You warm it to 33.5 °C to thin it. Predict the result.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

It will likely lose temper: 33.5 °C is at the melting point of form V, so the seed crystals melt. It will set soft and dull and may bloom. Retemper.

</details>

3. A white, powdery film appears on truffles taken from the fridge a few hours earlier. A drop of water dissolves it. Diagnosis?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sugar bloom from condensation. Let chocolates come to room temperature in a sealed container before unwrapping, or store them at 15–18 °C instead of the fridge.

</details>

4. Why does a small amount of cream of tartar help prevent crystallization in a syrup?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Acid plus heat inverts some sucrose into glucose and fructose, which act as interfering agents that disrupt the sucrose crystal lattice.

</details>

5. Why do milk and white chocolate temper at lower temperatures than dark?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Milk fat mixes with cocoa butter and disrupts its crystallization, lowering the temperatures at which the stable crystals form and melt.

</details>
