## The one-paragraph model

Carbohydrates are built from small sugar units. Single units (glucose, fructose) and pairs (sucrose, maltose, lactose) dissolve in water, taste sweet, grab water, and — if they are "reducing" sugars — brown readily. Long chains of glucose form **starch**, packed by the plant into dense, semi-crystalline granules. Cold water barely touches those granules. Heat them in enough water and they swell, lose their crystalline order and thicken or set whatever they are in: that is **gelatinization**, and it is what turns batter into cake, dough into crumb and milk into pastry cream. After cooling, the starch chains slowly re-align into crystals again — **retrogradation** — which firms bread and cakes (staling), weeps water from creams and is fastest at refrigerator temperature.

## The mechanism

### Sugars: the small carbohydrates

| Sugar | Built from | Where you meet it | Reducing? | Relative sweetness (sucrose = 100) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose (dextrose) | single unit | Glucose syrup, invert sugar, honey | Yes | ~70–80 |
| Fructose | single unit | Honey, fruit, invert sugar | Yes | ~110–170 (sweetest cold) |
| Sucrose | glucose + fructose | Granulated, caster, icing and brown sugars | **No** | 100 |
| Maltose | glucose + glucose | Released from starch by amylase in dough; malt | Yes | ~30–50 |
| Lactose | glucose + galactose | Milk, milk powder | Yes | ~15–40 |
| Invert sugar | equal glucose + fructose, from split sucrose | Invert syrup, trimoline, honey (roughly) | Yes | ~95–125 |

Sweetness figures are ranges because perceived sweetness depends on concentration, temperature and what else is present; treat them as rank order, not exact values.

**Reducing sugars** have an exposed reactive group (a carbonyl) that can react with amino groups of proteins — the start of the Maillard reaction ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)). Sucrose has that group locked up in the link between glucose and fructose, so pure sucrose does not take part until it is split.

**Inversion** splits sucrose into glucose and fructose. Three routes matter in the kitchen:

- Yeast carries the enzyme invertase, so sucrose in a bread dough is largely inverted within the first part of fermentation ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)).
- Acid plus heat inverts sucrose; the reason a spoonful of lemon juice or cream of tartar in a sugar syrup helps prevent crystallization ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)).
- Buying it: invert syrup or honey.

**Hygroscopicity** — how strongly a sugar attracts and holds water — ranks roughly fructose > invert sugar and honey > glucose > sucrose > lactose. More hygroscopic sugars keep cakes and cookies softer for longer, brown faster (they are reducing) and make crisp items go soft in humid air.

### What sugar does in a formula besides sweeten

| Function | Mechanism | Bench effect |
|---|---|---|
| Tenderizes | Competes for water with gluten and starch; slows gluten formation and starch gelatinization | Softer, more tender cakes and cookies |
| Retains moisture | Binds water, lowers aw ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)) | Longer soft shelf life |
| Delays setting | Raises protein coagulation and starch gelatinization temperatures | Cake rises longer before it sets; high sugar → finer, taller crumb up to a limit, then collapse |
| Aerates | Sharp crystals cut air into fat during creaming | Lighter creamed cakes ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)) |
| Browns | Maillard (reducing sugars) and caramelization | Colour and flavour ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)) |
| Feeds yeast | Fermentable sugar | Faster start at low levels; osmotic slowdown at high levels |
| Spreads cookies | Dissolves as dough heats, liquefying the dough | More sugar → more spread ([07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md)) |

### Starch: amylose and amylopectin

Starch is two kinds of glucose polymer:

| | Amylose | Amylopectin |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Mostly linear chains | Highly branched, tree-like, very large |
| Share in wheat starch | ~25 % | ~75 % |
| Behaviour after cooking | Leaches out, forms a firm gel quickly as it cools | Gives viscosity; re-crystallizes slowly over days |
| Role in staling | Sets the crumb in the first hours after baking | Main driver of staling over days |

Waxy starches (waxy maize, waxy rice) are almost all amylopectin: they thicken but form soft, non-gelling pastes that resist weeping. Cornstarch has similar amylose to wheat and forms a firm, cuttable gel.

Starch lives in **granules**. Wheat has two populations: large lens-shaped granules (~10–35 µm) and small round ones (under ~10 µm). Flour is roughly 68–75 % starch by weight. During milling a few percent of granules are mechanically damaged (typically ~5–9 % in bread flours); **damaged starch** absorbs several times more cold water than intact granules and is more easily attacked by amylase, which is why stronger-milled flours need more water ([02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md)).

### Gelatinization and pasting

In cold water, intact granules absorb only a little water (roughly a third of their weight) and settle out. Heat changes that:

| Stage | Wheat starch, approximate | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Swelling begins | ~50–55 °C | Water enters the amorphous regions |
| Gelatinization | ~55–70 °C | Crystalline order melts; granules swell many times their volume; the mixture thickens sharply |
| Pasting | ~70–95 °C with stirring | Amylose leaks out, swollen granules crowd each other, viscosity peaks |
| Breakdown | Continued heat and shear | Swollen granules fragment; viscosity drops |
| Setback / gelling | On cooling | Leached amylose forms a gel network; the paste firms |

Two conditions must both be met: **heat and enough water**. Starch needs roughly its own weight of water or more to gelatinize fully. In a bread dough (~60–75 % hydration on flour, of which gluten takes a share) starch gelatinizes only partly; in shortbread and sablé doughs, which are low in water and high in sugar and fat, much of the starch stays ungelatinized, giving a sandy, short texture.

Things that shift gelatinization:

| Factor | Effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | Raises gelatinization temperature (can push it above ~80 °C in high-sugar cakes and creams) and lowers peak viscosity | Sugar competes for water and stabilizes granule structure |
| Fat | Slows water entry; amylose–lipid complexes reduce firmness | Fat coats granules; emulsifiers in commercial bread slow staling this way |
| Acid, prolonged heating | Lowers viscosity, thins pastes | Acid hydrolyses (cuts) starch chains |
| Amylase | Liquefies gelatinized starch | Enzyme cuts chains ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) |
| Shear (vigorous stirring, blending after thickening) | Lowers viscosity | Breaks swollen granules |

### Starch thickening in practice

| Thickener | Relative dose for similar thickness | Paste on cooling | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wheat flour | ~2× cornstarch | Opaque, soft gel | Only ~70 % starch; protein dulls flavour and gloss; must be cooked to remove raw taste |
| Cornstarch | 1× (reference) | Opaque, firm, cuttable gel | Needs to reach a boil to fully thicken; standard in crème pâtissière (~7–10 % of the milk weight, combined with flour or alone) |
| Potato or tapioca starch | < 1× | Clearer, stringy, softer | Thickens at lower temperature; thins with prolonged boiling |
| Waxy/modified starches | varies | Clear, soft, freeze–thaw stable | Industry standard for fillings; Stage 2 topic |

**The amylase trap.** Egg yolks contain α-amylase, which survives gentle heating. A pastry cream thickened with starch but not brought to a full boil for about a minute or two after it thickens can look perfect, then thin to soup overnight as the surviving enzyme digests the starch. Boiling inactivates it. You test this in [X1-34](experiments/X1-34-starch-thickeners.md) and apply it in [12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md).

**Acid fillings.** In lemon meringue filling or acidic fruit pies, add the lemon juice after the starch has thickened, or use more starch: cooking starch in acid thins it.

### Retrogradation and staling

After gelatinization, starch chains are disordered and hold a lot of water. On cooling they start to realign:

- **Amylose** retrogrades within minutes to hours. In bread this is part of what sets the crumb so a loaf can be sliced; in a pastry cream it is the reason the cream firms as it cools.
- **Amylopectin** retrogrades over days. Its branches slowly form crystals, pulling water out of the gel and hardening the crumb. This is the main cause of **staling**: bread firming and losing flavour even when it has not lost weight.

| Storage | Staling rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature (~20–25 °C) | Moderate: noticeable in 1–2 days for lean bread | Slow recrystallization |
| Refrigerator (~0–5 °C) | Fastest; roughly several times faster than room temperature | Chains are mobile enough to move but cold enough to crystallize readily |
| Freezer (−18 °C or below) | Nearly stopped | Chains are immobile |
| Reheating to ~60 °C+ in the crumb | Reverses staling temporarily | Amylopectin crystals melt; they re-form faster the second time |

**Syneresis** (weeping) is the same process in a gel: as a cornstarch pudding or pastry cream retrogrades, the tightening network squeezes out liquid. Waxy starches, sugar, fat and emulsifiers all slow retrogradation; so do pre-gelatinized starch pastes like tangzhong ([06.2](stage-1/module-06/lesson-02.md)), which bring more fully hydrated starch and bound water into the dough. Enzymes that trim amylopectin branches (certain amylases) slow bread staling; this is the anti-staling principle of many commercial bread improvers ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)).

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Gelatinization curve with a spoon.** Stir 10 g cornstarch into 200 g cold water; it settles. Heat gently while stirring with a thermometer in the pan; note the temperature where the liquid turns from milky and thin to translucent and thick (usually in the 60s–70s °C for cornstarch). Keep going to a boil and note the final thickness, then cool half in a cup.
2. **Sugar delays thickening.** Repeat demo 1 with 10 g cornstarch, 200 g water and 60 g sugar. The thickening temperature is higher and the cooled gel is softer.
3. **Fridge vs counter staling.** Cut three equal slices from a fresh loaf; bag one at room temperature, one in the fridge, freeze one. After 24 h, press each with a fingertip and taste. Then warm the fridge slice in a toaster or 150 °C oven for a few minutes and taste again.

## Misconceptions

- **"Stale bread is dry bread."** Staling is mainly starch recrystallization. Stale bread can have almost the same moisture as fresh; that is why brief reheating revives it.
- **"The fridge keeps bread fresher."** It slows mould but accelerates staling. Freeze for storage beyond a day or two.
- **"Sugar is sugar."** Sucrose, glucose, fructose, invert and lactose differ in sweetness, browning, hygroscopicity and crystallization; swapping them changes texture and colour, not just taste.
- **"More starch means thicker cream, always."** Acid, surviving amylase, excessive stirring after thickening and too much sugar can all thin a starch paste regardless of dose.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Has my starch paste reached gelatinization / a boil? | Probe thermometer plus visual cue (translucency, large slow bubbles) | Stage 1 |
| Is the loaf baked through? | Probe thermometer: internal ~96–98 °C for lean bread | Stage 1 |
| How firm is my bread after N days? | Consistent finger press or a weight-on-slice compression test, recorded in the lab notebook | Stage 1 (improvised) |
| How much sugar is dissolved in my syrup or filling? | Refractometer (°Brix) | Stage 2 |
| How does this starch thicken vs temperature? | Viscometer / rapid visco analyser; texture analyser for crumb firmness | Stage 3 (lab equipment) |

## Check yourself

1. You swap 20 % of the sucrose in a sugar cookie for glucose syrup solids. Predict two changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Browner cookies (glucose is reducing) and a softer, more moisture-retaining texture (more hygroscopic); also slightly less sweet.

</details>

2. Why does a shortbread with ~0 % added water stay sandy while a sponge cake crumb becomes soft and springy?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Starch needs water as well as heat to gelatinize. In shortbread there is too little water (and a lot of fat and sugar), so granules remain largely intact; the sponge has ample water from the eggs, so starch gelatinizes and sets a soft, continuous crumb.

</details>

3. A customer complains your pastry cream tarts are weeping by the next morning, though the cream was fine when set. Name two starch-related causes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Retrogradation/syneresis of the starch gel on refrigeration, and amylase from yolks surviving because the cream was not boiled long enough after thickening. (Acid from fruit touching the cream is a third contributor.)

</details>

4. Which is the better way to keep a sandwich loaf for five days: fridge or freezer? Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Freezer. At −18 °C amylopectin cannot move enough to recrystallize; in the fridge it recrystallizes fastest.

</details>
