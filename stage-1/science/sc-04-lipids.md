## The one-paragraph model

Baking fats are mostly **triglycerides**: three fatty-acid chains hung on a glycerol backbone. Straight, saturated chains pack neatly and are solid at room temperature; kinked, unsaturated chains pack badly and are liquid. Every real fat is a mixture of many triglycerides, so it does not melt at one temperature but over a **melting range**, and at any temperature it is part solid crystals, part liquid oil. That solid fraction — the **solid fat content** — decides whether the fat is brittle, plastic or runny. A fat that is plastic (it deforms without cracking and without melting) can trap air when creamed, form sheets in lamination and coat flour to shorten a dough. Fat crystals can form in different shapes (polymorphs); small crystals give smooth, aerating fats, large ones give grainy textures. Temperature is therefore the main control variable whenever you work with solid fat.

## The mechanism

### Triglycerides and fatty acids

| Fatty acid | Chain | Saturation | Where it dominates | Melting behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Palmitic | C16 | Saturated | Palm oil, butter, cocoa butter, lard | Solid well above room temperature |
| Stearic | C18 | Saturated | Cocoa butter, lard, beef fat | Solid, high melting |
| Oleic | C18:1 | Mono-unsaturated (one kink) | Olive oil, lard, cocoa butter, high-oleic oils | Liquid at room temperature |
| Linoleic | C18:2 | Poly-unsaturated (two kinks) | Sunflower, soybean, corn oils | Liquid; oxidizes more readily |
| Short chains (butyric C4, etc.) | C4–C10 | Saturated but short | Butter (source of its flavour, and rancid butyric smell) | Lower melting |

Two rules predict most behaviour:

- **Longer and more saturated → higher melting.** Straight chains stack like pencils.
- **More unsaturated → lower melting, more prone to oxidation.** Each double bond is a kink and a weak point for oxygen attack.

*Trans* fats (a straightened form of unsaturated chains produced by partial hydrogenation) behave like saturated fats in a crystal; they are now largely removed from shortenings for health reasons, replaced with palm-based and interesterified fats.

### Melting range and solid fat content

A fat's **solid fat content (SFC)** is the percentage of it that is crystalline at a given temperature. It is the most useful single description of how a fat will behave on the bench.

Approximate SFC of butter (milk fat varies with season and cow diet):

| Temperature | Approximate SFC | Butter on the bench |
|---|---|---|
| 5 °C (fridge) | ~50 % | Hard, brittle, cracks when bent |
| 10 °C | ~40 % | Firm; still cracks if sheeted |
| 15 °C | ~25–30 % | Cold-plastic: ideal lamination butter |
| 20 °C | ~15–20 % | Soft-plastic: ideal creaming butter |
| 25 °C | ~10 % | Greasy, starting to lose structure |
| 30 °C | ~5 % | Oily, collapses |
| 35 °C and above | ~0 % | Fully melted (butter clear at ~35–37 °C) |

Useful melting points for comparison (end of melting, approximate):

| Fat | Complete melt | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Butter | ~32–37 °C | Narrow plastic window; melts in the mouth cleanly |
| Coconut oil | ~24 °C | Brittle when cool, liquid on a warm day |
| Cocoa butter (stable form V) | ~34 °C | Snaps at room temperature, melts in the mouth ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)) |
| Lard | ~30–45 °C (varies) | Coarse crystals; flaky pastry |
| All-purpose shortening | ~40–47 °C | Wide plastic range; can feel waxy in the mouth |
| Liquid oils | below 0 °C | No crystals at bench temperature |

### Plasticity: the working window

A fat is **plastic** when it holds its shape under light pressure but deforms without cracking under more force. Mechanically it is a network of fat crystals holding liquid oil in its gaps, like a sponge. As a rule of thumb, fats are plastic when roughly 15–40 % of them is solid: below that they are greasy and flow; above it they are hard and brittle.

| Task | Butter target temperature | What you are aiming for |
|---|---|---|
| Creaming (cakes, cookies) | ~18–21 °C | Soft-plastic: can stretch around air bubbles |
| Lamination (puff, croissant) | ~13–16 °C butter, dough slightly colder or similar | Cold-plastic: sheets without cracking or melting into dough ([15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)) |
| Rubbing-in / flaky pie | ~4–8 °C, in pieces | Solid pieces that survive mixing and create steam pockets |
| Brioche (added to dough) | ~15–18 °C, pliable | Blends into the gluten without melting (dough kept ≤ ~26 °C) |

Shortening's plastic range is much wider than butter's, which is why it forgives a warm kitchen and why shortening pie crusts are easy to make but taste less rich.

### Polymorphism, headline version

The same triglycerides can crystallize in different packings (polymorphs), each with its own melting point:

| Form | Stability | Crystals | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| α (alpha) | Least stable, lowest melting | Tiny, transient | Forms first on rapid cooling; converts quickly |
| β′ (beta-prime) | Intermediate | Small, fine needles | Butter and good shortenings: smooth, creamy, aerate well, plastic |
| β (beta) | Most stable, highest melting | Large, coarse | Lard (coarse, flaky pastry); grainy texture in badly stored fats; "sandy" butter after melting and resetting |

Slow cooling and temperature cycling push fats toward larger, more stable crystals. That is why butter that has been melted and resolidified is grainy and no longer creams well, and why chocolate needs tempering: cocoa butter has six forms, and only form V gives snap and gloss. The cocoa butter details belong to [SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md) and [16.3](stage-1/module-16/lesson-03.md).

### Butter, oils and shortening compared

| Fat | Fat % | Water % | Other | Baking behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Butter (unsalted) | ~80–82 % (EU minimum 82 %, US minimum 80 %) | ~16 % | 1–2 % milk solids (protein, lactose); salted adds ~1.5–2 % salt | Flavour, browning from milk solids, water turns to steam in lamination, narrow plastic range |
| Clarified butter / ghee | ~99+ % | ~0 | — | No water, no milk solids; nutty if browned first |
| Shortening | 100 % | 0 | Often ~10–12 % nitrogen gas whipped in | Wide plastic range, more tender, no flavour |
| Liquid oil | 100 % | 0 | — | Cannot cream or trap air; coats flour very effectively: moist, tender, even crumb (chiffon, muffins) |
| Margarine / baker's margarine | ~80 % (spreads lower) | ~16–20 % | Emulsifiers | Engineered melting profile; lamination margarines are very plastic |

Two conversions that trip people up:

- Replacing butter with oil at equal weight adds ~20 % more fat and removes ~16 % water: expect greasier, denser results. Use about 80 % of the butter weight as oil, and add back the missing water, if you want to match composition.
- Butter's 16 % water counts toward true hydration ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)).

### The jobs fat does

| Job | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Tenderizing ("shortening") | Coats proteins and starch, blocks gluten networks and interrupts crumb | Short dough, brioche softness |
| Aerating | Plastic fat traps air cells during creaming; β′ crystals line the bubbles | Pound cake, creamed cookies ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)) |
| Layering | Solid sheets separate dough layers; water in butter turns to steam | Puff pastry, croissant |
| Flakiness | Discrete fat pieces melt, leave gaps, release steam | Pie crust, scones |
| Moistness and mouthfeel | Liquid fat lubricates | Oil cakes feel moist even when cold |
| Flavour carrier | Many aroma compounds dissolve in fat | Butter flavour; brown butter (Maillard on milk solids, [SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)) |
| Heat transfer | Hot fat conducts heat efficiently | Frying, greased pans |

### Oxidation and rancidity

| Type | Cause | Smell | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidative | Oxygen attacks double bonds; accelerated by light, heat, traces of metal (iron, copper) | Cardboard, paint, fishy | Cool, dark, airtight storage; fresh nuts; antioxidants (vitamin E) in commercial fats |
| Hydrolytic | Water plus lipase enzymes (or heat) cut fatty acids off glycerol | Sour, cheesy (butyric acid in butter) | Cold storage; heat-treated ingredients |

Nuts, whole-grain flours (germ oils) and poly-unsaturated oils go rancid fastest. Store nuts and wholemeal flour cool and use within weeks to a few months; freeze for longer.

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Bend test across temperatures.** Cut three 1 cm-thick strips from the same block of butter. Leave one in the fridge, one at ~15 °C (cool room or a few minutes out of the fridge; check with a probe) and one at ~21 °C for an hour. Bend each slowly: it snaps, bends like clay, or smears. You have just felt the plastic window.
2. **Melted and reset butter.** Melt 30 g butter, pour into a cup and refrigerate until solid, then bring it and an untouched piece to ~20 °C. Beat both with a small whisk and a spoon of sugar. The reset butter is grainy and creams poorly: crystals formed in the wrong size.
3. **Oil vs butter coating.** Rub 20 g oil into 50 g flour and 20 g soft butter into another 50 g flour; add 20 g water to each and knead 1 minute. The oil dough is shorter and less elastic: liquid oil coats proteins more completely.

## Misconceptions

- **"Butter melts at one temperature."** It melts over a range from below 0 °C to ~35 °C; at every bench temperature it is a mixture of crystals and oil.
- **"Fat makes things moist because it contains water."** Oil has no water. Fat gives perceived moistness by lubricating and tenderizing; it can make a product *less* hydrated if it replaces liquid.
- **"Cold butter is always right."** Each technique needs its own window: pieces for flakiness, cold-plastic for lamination, soft-plastic for creaming. Too cold is a failure in creaming and lamination.
- **"Saturated fats oxidize; oils don't."** The opposite: unsaturated fats are far more vulnerable to oxidative rancidity.

## Check yourself

1. Your kitchen is 28 °C. Predict what happens if you try to cream butter and sugar for a pound cake, and name one fix.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The butter warms into its greasy zone (~10 % solid or less) and cannot hold air; the batter looks slick and the cake is dense. Fixes: start with butter at ~16–18 °C, chill the bowl, cream in shorter bursts, or use a fat blend with a wider plastic range.

</details>

2. Why does lard give a flakier pie crust than butter, all else equal?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lard tends to form large β crystals and has a wider plastic range, so pieces stay solid and discrete through mixing and rolling, giving distinct layers; butter's narrower range and water content make it smear more easily. (Butter still wins on flavour.)

</details>

3. You replace 100 g butter with 100 g oil in a muffin. Name two compositional differences you introduced.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 18–20 g more fat and about 16 g less water (plus no milk solids). Expect a more tender, possibly greasier crumb and slightly less browning.

</details>

4. Why do wholemeal flour and walnuts need cooler storage than white flour and butter?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

They contain poly-unsaturated oils (and, in wholemeal, active lipases) that oxidize and hydrolyse quickly to rancid flavours.

</details>
