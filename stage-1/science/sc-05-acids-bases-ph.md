## The one-paragraph model

**Acids** release hydrogen ions (H⁺) in water; **bases** mop them up. **pH** is a logarithmic scale of how many free hydrogen ions there are: 7 is neutral, lower is acidic, higher is alkaline, and each whole step is a tenfold change. pH is a control knob on almost every reaction in baking. It sets the electrical charge on proteins (so it changes gluten strength, egg-foam stability and custard setting), the speed of browning (faster when alkaline), the colour of cocoa and fruit pigments, which microbes thrive, and — when an acid meets baking soda — how much carbon dioxide you get and when. Many ingredients are **buffered**: they resist pH change, so you cannot predict a batter's pH just by averaging its ingredients.

## The mechanism

### The pH scale

pH = −log[H⁺]. Because it is a log scale:

- pH 4 is 10 times more acidic than pH 5 and 100 times more than pH 6.
- A levain dropping from pH 4.5 to pH 3.8 has roughly five times the free H⁺.
- Small numerical changes near neutral (6.8 → 7.8 in a cookie dough with soda) are large chemical changes.

pH measures *intensity* (free H⁺ right now). **Total titratable acidity (TTA)** measures *quantity* (all the acid present, including acid that has not released its H⁺). Two sourdoughs at the same pH can have different TTA and taste differently sour; see Measuring it below.

### Acids and bases you actually use

| Ingredient | Active species | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Buttermilk, yogurt, sour cream | Lactic acid (~0.8–1 % in buttermilk) | Reacts with soda; tenderizes; tang |
| Lemon juice | Citric acid (~5–6 %) | Flavour; reacts with soda; inverts sugar in syrups |
| Vinegar | Acetic acid (~5 %) | Reacts with soda; used in some meringues (pavlova) |
| Cream of tartar | Potassium hydrogen tartrate (an acid salt) | Stabilizes egg foams; acid in some baking powders; anti-crystallization in syrups |
| Natural cocoa, chocolate | Organic acids | Reacts with soda (devil's food) |
| Honey, molasses, brown sugar | Organic acids | Mildly acidic, can react with soda |
| Sourdough | Lactic and acetic acids from bacteria | Flavour, keeping quality, gluten effects ([SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)) |
| Baking soda | Sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO₃ (base) | Releases CO₂ with acid; raises pH |
| Baking powder | Soda + one or more leavening acids + starch | Self-contained gas system ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)) |

### Neutralization: acid + soda → CO₂

The core reaction:

NaHCO₃ + H⁺ (from acid) → Na⁺ + H₂O + CO₂

| Rule | Number |
|---|---|
| Soda per gram of lactic acid for full neutralization | ~0.93 g (molar masses 84 vs 90) |
| Soda per gram of acetic acid | ~1.4 g |
| Soda per gram of citric acid | ~1.3 g (citric acid gives three H⁺) |
| Soda to neutralize ~250 g cultured buttermilk (~0.8–0.9 % lactic acid) | ~2 g |
| Soda that decomposes without acid when heated | 2 NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂: only half the CO₂, and it leaves washing soda (alkaline, soapy, metallic taste, yellow-green crumb, excess browning) |

Commercial leavening acids are rated by **neutralizing value (NV)**: grams of soda neutralized by 100 g of the acid. Cream of tartar ~45, sodium acid pyrophosphate (SAPP) ~72, monocalcium phosphate (MCP) ~80, sodium aluminium sulfate ~100 (standard industry values). Acids also differ in *when* they react: MCP and cream of tartar react mostly on mixing; SAPP and sodium aluminium sulfate react mainly with heat. That timing is what "double-acting" baking powder means ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md), [X1-21](experiments/X1-21-leaveners.md)).

Most recipes deliberately leave the batter slightly acidic or slightly alkaline: slight excess acid gives a paler, more tender, tangier result; slight excess soda gives a darker, more open, spread-prone result.

### Buffers

A **buffer** is a mixture that absorbs added acid or base with only a small pH change. Flour proteins, milk proteins and phosphates, egg proteins and the phosphate salts in baking powder all buffer. Consequences:

- You cannot calculate a batter's final pH from the ingredient table; you must measure or test it.
- A sourdough levain resists pH drop at first (flour buffers), then acidifies as bacteria outrun the buffering.
- Whole-grain flours (more minerals, more bran protein) buffer more than white flour, which is one reason whole-grain levains can hold more total acid (higher TTA) at a similar pH.

### What pH changes

| Property | Lower pH (more acidic) | Higher pH (more alkaline) |
|---|---|---|
| Maillard browning ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)) | Slower, paler | Faster, darker, more roasted flavour (pretzels dipped in alkaline bath) |
| Gluten | Mild acid tightens and shortens dough; strong acid plus protease activity weakens it (overfermented sourdough goes slack) | Weaker, more extensible gluten; spread in cookies |
| Egg-white foams | Near pH 8 (with cream of tartar): finer, more stable, harder to overbeat | Old, alkaline whites whip fast but give less stable foams |
| Egg coagulation | Sets at lower temperature, firmer, whiter | Sets softer; egg whites turn yellowish or grey-green |
| Cocoa colour | Natural cocoa in acidic batter: lighter, reddish-brown | Dutch-processed or soda-rich: darker, redder to near black |
| Anthocyanins (blueberries, red cabbage, red berries) | Red to purple | Blue, then green-grey (blueberry muffins with excess soda turn green around berries) |
| Starch pastes | Prolonged heating in acid thins them ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)) | Little effect at food pH |
| Sucrose | Acid + heat inverts it ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)) | No inversion |
| Pectin gels | High-methoxyl pectin needs ~pH 2.8–3.5 plus high sugar to set | Will not set |
| Microbes | Most pathogens inhibited below ~pH 4.6; lactic bacteria and yeasts tolerate it | Wider range of bacteria grow |
| Flavour | Tangy, brighter | Soapy or bitter if soda is in excess |

### pH in sourdough

A levain is an ecosystem of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria ([SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)). As bacteria make lactic and acetic acid, pH falls from ~6 (fresh flour-water) toward ~3.5–4.0 at peak and below if left long. Practical anchors:

| pH | Levain / dough state |
|---|---|
| ~5.5–6 | Just fed; mostly flour buffer |
| ~4.2–4.5 | Active, rising, mildly tangy |
| ~3.8–4.0 | Peak or just past; ready to use for most formulas |
| < ~3.6 | Overripe; sharp sourness, weakened gluten, slower yeast |

Warm, wet levains favour lactic acid (milder, yogurt-like); cool, stiff levains favour relatively more acetic acid (sharper, vinegary). You track this in [X1-14](experiments/X1-14-levain.md).

## What it predicts on the bench

| If you change... | Prediction | Why | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Replace buttermilk with milk but keep the soda | Soapy taste, yellowish crumb, more browning, less rise | No acid to neutralize soda | [X1-21](experiments/X1-21-leaveners.md), [08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md) |
| Swap natural cocoa for Dutch-processed in a soda-leavened cake | Flatter, denser cake; darker colour | Dutch cocoa brings no acid for the soda | [16.1](stage-1/module-16/lesson-01.md) |
| Add ¼ tsp extra soda to cookie dough | More spread, darker, crisper edges | Higher pH weakens gluten and speeds Maillard | [07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md) |
| Add cream of tartar (~1 g per 100 g white) | Slower-forming, finer, more stable foam | Lower pH moderates protein aggregation | [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md) |
| Use levain at pH 3.5 instead of ~3.9 | Sourer loaf, slacker dough, possibly less volume | Excess acid and protease activity weaken gluten | [05.4](stage-1/module-05/lesson-04.md) |
| Warm (28 °C) vs cool (20 °C) levain | Warm: faster, milder-tasting; cool: slower, sharper | Temperature shifts bacterial balance and acid profile | [X1-14](experiments/X1-14-levain.md) |
| Add lemon juice to a syrup | Less crystallization | Acid inverts some sucrose | [13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md) |

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Red cabbage pH indicator.** Chop 100 g red cabbage, cover with 300 g just-boiled water for 10 minutes, strain. Put 30 g of the purple liquid in each of five glasses and add: lemon juice, vinegar, nothing, a pinch of baking soda, milk. Red → pink → purple → blue-green. This is exactly what blueberries do in an over-alkaline muffin.
2. **Fizz on schedule.** In three glasses put 2 g baking soda + 50 g water, 2 g baking powder + 50 g water, and 2 g baking soda + 50 g buttermilk. Watch how much foam appears in the first minute. Then warm the baking powder glass in a pan of hot water (~60 °C) and watch a second burst: that is the heat-activated acid.
3. **Green blueberries.** Mix two small spoonfuls of any plain batter, add a pinch of soda to one, stir in a few crushed blueberries and microwave or bake both briefly. The alkaline one goes grey-green.

## Misconceptions

- **"Baking soda is a leavener on its own."** Without acid, heat releases only half its CO₂ and leaves bitter, alkaline sodium carbonate. It needs an acid partner.
- **"pH 4 is a little more acidic than pH 5."** It is ten times more acidic. Log scales compress big changes into small numbers.
- **"Sourness equals pH."** Perceived sourness tracks total acid (TTA) and acid type (acetic tastes sharper than lactic) as much as pH.
- **"Dutch and natural cocoa are interchangeable."** They differ in pH, colour and flavour, and they change how much leavening acid is in the system.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Roughly how acidic is this? | pH test strips (resolution ~0.5 pH; coloured foods can mask the result; use strips with a 3–6 or 4–7 range for sourdough) | Stage 1 |
| Is my levain at pH 3.9 or 4.3? | Pocket pH meter with glass electrode; calibrate with pH 4.00 and 7.00 buffers before each session (add 10.01 for alkaline work); store the electrode in storage solution, not distilled water; measure at a consistent temperature or use automatic temperature compensation | Stage 1 useful, Stage 2 standard |
| pH of a dough or thick batter | Spear-tip food electrode, or the slurry method: blend 10 g sample with 90–100 g distilled water and measure the slurry | Stage 2 |
| How much total acid is there? | Total titratable acidity (TTA): titrate a 10 g sample slurry with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide to pH 8.5, record mL used | Stage 2/3 |
| Will my batter be acidic or alkaline after baking? | Measure crumb pH with a slurry | Stage 2 |

Record the pH, time since feeding and temperature together; pH without temperature and time is hard to interpret.

## Check yourself

1. A muffin formula uses 240 g buttermilk (~0.9 % lactic acid) and 4 g baking soda. Is the soda in excess? What will you taste and see?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lactic acid ≈ 240 × 0.009 = 2.2 g, which neutralizes ≈ 2.0 g soda. About 2 g soda is in excess (unless baking powder acid or other acids cover it): expect soapy/metallic notes, a darker, yellowish crumb and coarse texture.

</details>

2. Why do egg whites from week-old eggs whip faster but give a less stable meringue than fresh ones treated with cream of tartar?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Old whites have lost CO₂ and risen toward pH 9; thinner and more alkaline, they foam quickly but their proteins bond less effectively into a stable film. Cream of tartar brings the pH down toward ~8 and moderates aggregation, giving a finer, more stable foam.

</details>

3. Your sourdough loaf is sour, slack and flat. The levain was used 14 hours after feeding at 27 °C. Predict its pH range and the mechanism.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Likely below ~3.6 (overripe). Excess acid and protease activity weakened the gluten, and yeast activity was declining, so the dough could not hold shape or gas.

</details>

4. You swap natural cocoa for Dutch-processed in a devil's food cake leavened only with soda. Name two changes.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Less CO₂ (no acid to react with the soda) so a denser cake, and a darker, less reddish colour with a soapy edge if soda is left unreacted. Fix: switch part of the soda to baking powder.

</details>
