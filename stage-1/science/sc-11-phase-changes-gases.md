## The one-paragraph model

Almost every rise in baking is a gas expanding, and almost every temperature ceiling is water boiling. Turning liquid water into steam soaks up a huge amount of energy (latent heat), so anything wet is pinned near 100 °C until the water is gone: the crumb of a loaf never passes ~99 °C, and the crust cannot brown until its surface dries. That same change from liquid to vapour multiplies the volume by about 1,600, which is what inflates puff pastry, choux and pita. Gases already present, air beaten in and CO2 from yeast or baking powder, expand more modestly with heat (about a third of a percent per degree), and CO2 dissolved in the dough comes out of solution as it warms. Dissolved sugar raises the boiling point, so a syrup's boiling temperature tells you its concentration; altitude lowers the boiling point for everything. With these few rules you can predict oven spring, puff, crust formation and syrup stages.

## The mechanism

### Latent heat: the 100 °C ceiling

Heating water from 20 to 100 °C takes ~335 J per gram. Turning that gram into steam at 100 °C takes another **~2,260 J**, nearly seven times as much, without any rise in temperature ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)). In the oven this creates two zones in a loaf:

| Zone | Temperature | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Crust (outer few mm) | Climbs from ~100 °C to 150–200 °C once dry | Water has evaporated; surface can now brown ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)) |
| Evaporation front | ~100 °C | Water turning to steam; the front moves inward during the bake, thickening the crust |
| Crumb | Rises to ~96–99 °C and stays there | Still wet; latent heat and steam inside keep it below 100 °C |

That is why a centre temperature of 96–99 °C means "done" for bread regardless of oven temperature, and why a longer bake gives a thicker, darker crust rather than a hotter crumb ([X1-11](experiments/X1-11-bake-time.md)). Bread typically loses about 10–20 % of its dough weight as water during baking and cooling (standard bakery range; more for small, crusty items).

Evaporation also cools: a loaf fresh from the oven keeps steaming for an hour or more, and the crust softens as water from the crumb migrates outward.

### Steam leavening

One gram of liquid water occupies ~1 cm³. As steam at 100 °C and atmospheric pressure it occupies about **1,600–1,700 cm³**. Even a little evaporation inside a closed structure makes a lot of gas.

| Product | Where the water is | What traps the steam | What sets the shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puff pastry | Water in the dough layers and ~16 % water in the butter | Hundreds of thin gluten sheets separated by fat | Gluten and starch in each layer set as they dry |
| Choux | A wet paste (~50 % water overall) | A stretchy egg–starch skin | Egg proteins coagulate, starch sets, shell dries ([14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md)) |
| Pita | A thin, well-hydrated sheet | Two gluten skins that seal as the surfaces set | Very hot floor (250 °C+) vaporizes water fast enough to split the sheet ([R1-03](recipes/R1-03-pita-flatbreads.md)) |
| Popovers, Yorkshire puddings | Thin egg batter | Egg and flour skin | Protein coagulation |

Steam leavening needs **high heat early** (to generate steam faster than it can leak) and a **structure that stretches then sets**. Opening the oven before the structure sets lets the product collapse as steam condenses ([X1-38](experiments/X1-38-choux-oven.md)).

### Gas laws: how much does trapped gas expand?

At constant pressure, a gas's volume is proportional to its absolute temperature (Charles's law), so it expands by about **1/273 of its volume per kelvin** near 0 °C, roughly 0.3 % per degree at room temperature.

| Heating trapped gas from | to | Volume factor |
|---|---|---|
| 27 °C (300 K) | 60 °C (333 K) | ×1.11 |
| 27 °C | 100 °C (373 K) | ×1.24 |

So thermal expansion of the gas already in the dough contributes only about +20–25 %. The rest of oven spring (often +30 to +100 % in volume for a lean loaf) comes from:

1. **Yeast's last burst** until it dies around 55–60 °C ([SC-08](stage-1/science/sc-08-microbiology.md)).
2. **CO2 coming out of solution.** Dough water holds a lot of dissolved CO2; its solubility falls as temperature rises (roughly halving from 20 to 50 °C), releasing gas into existing cells.
3. **Ethanol and water vaporizing.** Ethanol boils at ~78 °C; the water vapour pressure rises steeply toward 100 °C, adding vapour to every gas cell.

Oven spring stops when the structure sets: gluten proteins stiffen and starch gelatinizes between ~60 and 80 °C, and the crust hardens. Anything that sets the crust too early (no steam, too-dry oven, fan at full speed) cuts spring short.

Gases never create new bubbles; they only inflate **existing** ones. New gas diffuses into cells that were created during mixing and creaming. This is why creaming and mixing set the number of cells, and leavening sets how big they get ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)).

### Condensation: steam on cold dough

When you add steam to the oven at loading, the dough surface (~25 °C) is far below the dew point of the humid oven air. Steam condenses on it, releasing ~2,260 J per gram straight into the surface, which heats it quickly while keeping it wet. Consequences:

- The surface stays soft and stretchy for the first minutes, so the loaf can expand more (better oven spring, cleaner ear on scores).
- Surface starch gelatinizes in the film of water and later dries into a thin, glossy, crackly crust.
- Condensation stops once the surface reaches ~100 °C; after that, steam in the oven only slows drying. That is why you vent the steam after ~15–20 minutes for bread.

You compare open oven, added steam and covered pot in [X1-10](experiments/X1-10-steam.md).

### Boiling point elevation: syrups tell you their concentration

Dissolved particles make it harder for water molecules to escape, so a solution boils at a higher temperature than pure water. The more concentrated the syrup, the higher the boiling point. As you boil a sugar syrup, water leaves, the concentration rises, and the temperature climbs. The temperature therefore **reads out concentration**:

| Stage | Temperature | Approx. sugar concentration | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple syrup | ~101–103 °C (214–217 °F) | 50–65 % | Cake soaking |
| Thread | 110–112 °C (230–234 °F) | ~80 % | Syrups, some glazes |
| Soft ball | 112–116 °C (234–240 °F) | ~85 % | Fondant, fudge |
| Firm ball | 118–120 °C (244–248 °F) | ~87 % | Italian meringue (commonly 118–121 °C), caramels |
| Hard ball | 121–130 °C (250–266 °F) | ~90 % | Nougat, marshmallow |
| Soft crack | 132–143 °C (270–290 °F) | ~95 % | Toffee, butterscotch |
| Hard crack | 149–154 °C (300–310 °F) | ~99 % | Pulled and blown sugar, hard candy |
| Caramel | 160 °C+ (320 °F+) | ~100 % | Browning begins ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)) |

Values follow the standard confectioners' table [McGee]. The reading depends on the local boiling point of water (next section) and on the thermometer's calibration, so calibrate in boiling water first ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)).

### Altitude and pressure

Lower air pressure means water boils at a lower temperature: roughly **1 °C lower per ~300 m** of altitude (about 95 °C at 1,500 m, ~93 °C at 2,000 m). Effects:

- Syrup stages: subtract the local boiling-point depression from every target (at 1,500 m, soft ball is ~5 °C lower).
- Bread crumb and cakes set at a lower maximum temperature; water evaporates faster; gases expand more at lower pressure. High-altitude baking typically reduces leavening, increases liquid and raises oven temperature a little (standard high-altitude practice).
- A pressure cooker does the reverse: higher pressure, boiling near 120 °C.

### Other phase changes you meet

| Change | Where | Key number |
|---|---|---|
| Melting of fat | Butter in lamination, cookies spreading | Butter mostly melted by ~32–35 °C ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)) |
| Melting/freezing of water | Ice for DDT; frozen dough | 334 J/g latent heat of fusion |
| Freezing-point depression | Sugar and salt lower the freezing point | Why ice cream and sorbet are scoopable (Stage 2) |
| Sublimation | Freezer burn on unwrapped dough and cakes | Ice goes straight to vapour in dry freezer air |
| Crystallization from solution | Sugar in fondant, fudge | [SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md) |

## What it predicts on the bench

| If you change... | Prediction | Why | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add steam for the first 15 min | Bigger loaf, glossier and thinner crust | Condensation keeps surface soft and gelatinizes surface starch | [X1-10](experiments/X1-10-steam.md), [04.1](stage-1/module-04/lesson-01.md) |
| Bake longer at the same temperature | Thicker, darker crust; crumb temperature barely changes | Evaporation front moves inward; crumb capped ~99 °C | [X1-11](experiments/X1-11-bake-time.md), [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md) |
| Open the oven during the first 20 min of choux | Collapse | Steam condenses before the shell is set | [X1-38](experiments/X1-38-choux-oven.md), [14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md) |
| Bake pita at 200 °C instead of 260 °C | No pocket, or a partial one | Steam produced too slowly; surfaces set before splitting | [R1-03](recipes/R1-03-pita-flatbreads.md) |

| Bake a retarded dough straight from the fridge instead of warming it first | Firm dough scores cleanly; small blisters on the crust are common | Cold dough holds more dissolved CO2, which comes out of solution as it heats; a cold, wet skin condenses more steam | [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md) |
| Freeze unwrapped cakes | Dry, pale patches | Sublimation | [04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md) |

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Syrup readout (15 minutes).** Boil 200 g sugar with 100 g water, reading the thermometer every minute. Note where the temperature climbs slowly (lots of water to remove) and where it accelerates (little water left). Drop a little into cold water at 112, 118, 130 and 150 °C and feel the stages. Handle with care: sugar at these temperatures causes severe burns.
2. **Steam puff.** Roll a piece of lean dough to 3 mm, cut two discs, bake one at 260 °C on a preheated tray and one at 200 °C on a cold tray. Only the first puffs into a pocket.
3. **Charles in a bag.** Seal a zip bag with a little air, put it in the freezer for 15 minutes, then in a 60 °C oven for 2 minutes (not hotter). Watch it shrink and then swell: modest, about 20 %, compared with the violent puff of steam.

## Misconceptions

- **"Oven spring is hot gas expanding."** Thermal expansion alone gives ~20–25 %. Most spring comes from yeast's last activity, CO2 leaving solution and water and ethanol vaporizing.
- **"A hotter oven makes the inside of the loaf hotter."** The crumb stays below ~99 °C. A hotter oven makes the crust form and brown sooner.
- **"Steam makes the crust soft."** Early steam makes it *thinner and crisper*: it delays crust setting and then dries into a glassy layer. A soft crust comes from late steam, covering, or bagging warm.
- **"Syrup temperature is about heat."** It is about concentration; temperature is simply the easiest way to measure it.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Is the crumb done? | Probe thermometer at the centre (bread 96–99 °C) | Stage 1 |
| What stage is my syrup? | Calibrated probe or candy thermometer; cold-water test | Stage 1 |
| How much water did the bake remove? | Scale: dough weight vs cooled loaf weight (bake loss %) | Stage 1 |
| What is my local boiling point? | Thermometer in rolling boil; note it in your notebook | Stage 1 |
| What is the syrup's concentration directly? | Refractometer (°Brix), for syrups below ~85 % | Stage 2 |
| How does the loaf's interior temperature and volume change during baking? | Logged thermocouples; time-lapse through the oven window | Stage 2 |
| Pressure and gas release in dough | Rheofermentometer, oven-rise recorders | Stage 3 |

## Check yourself

1. A baguette bakes at 250 °C for 25 minutes. What is the crumb temperature at the end, approximately, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 96–99 °C. As long as the crumb contains liquid water, evaporation holds it below 100 °C whatever the oven temperature.

</details>

2. Estimate how much a gas bubble grows from thermal expansion alone when a dough warms from 25 °C to 90 °C.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(90 + 273) / (25 + 273) = 363 / 298 ≈ 1.22, so about +22 %.

</details>

3. A syrup reads 125 °C at sea level. Roughly what is its sugar concentration and what stage is it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Hard ball, roughly 90 % sugar.

</details>

4. Why does puff pastry rise more when the butter is cold and plastic than when it is soft?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Distinct fat layers keep the dough sheets separate. Steam generated in each dough layer pushes against its neighbours and lifts them. With soft butter the layers merge, steam escapes through the torn sheets, and there is nothing to separate.

</details>

5. You bake at 2,000 m. Your pastry cream seems to take a long time to thicken, and your meringue syrup recipe says 121 °C. What do you adjust?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water boils around 93 °C at 2,000 m, so every syrup target drops about 7 °C: aim ~114 °C for the same concentration. The cream still reaches a boil, but at a lower temperature; allow a little longer at the boil to gelatinize the starch fully and inactivate yolk amylase ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)).

</details>
