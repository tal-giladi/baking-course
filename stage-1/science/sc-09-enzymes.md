## The one-paragraph model

Enzymes are proteins that act as catalysts: each one grips a specific kind of molecule and cuts (or joins) a specific bond, over and over, without being used up. Because they are proteins, they have a temperature at which they work fastest and a temperature at which they unfold and die (denature), and a pH range outside which they barely work. In baking, a handful of enzymes explain a surprising number of outcomes. **Amylases** in flour cut starch into sugars that feed yeast and brown the crust; too few give a pale, slow loaf, too many give a sticky, gummy crumb. **Proteases** cut gluten proteins and slacken dough over long fermentations, and fruit proteases stop gelatin from setting. **Invertase** from yeast splits sucrose in minutes. And an amylase in egg yolk will quietly turn a pastry cream to soup unless you boil it. Predicting enzyme effects means asking three questions: is the substrate available, is the enzyme present, and how long does it spend at a temperature and pH where it works before heat kills it?

## The mechanism

### How an enzyme behaves

An enzyme's folded shape forms an **active site** that fits its substrate. Reaction speed rises with temperature (below the optimum, many enzyme reactions roughly double per 10 °C), until the heat starts unfolding the protein faster than it works. Above that point activity collapses and does not return on cooling: the enzyme is **denatured**. Heat stability varies a lot with the enzyme's source, which is why "the same" enzyme from malt, a fungus or a bacterium behaves differently in the oven.

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Faster up to the optimum, then irreversible loss |
| pH | Each enzyme has a working range; far outside it activity drops |
| Water | Enzymes need free water; a dry flour barely reacts, a wet dough reacts fast ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)) |
| Substrate access | An enzyme can only cut what it can reach: intact starch granules resist amylase, gelatinized starch does not |
| Time | Enzyme effects accumulate: long, cold fermentations give enzymes hours to work |

### Amylases: alpha and beta

Flour contains two starch-cutting enzymes, in very different amounts.

| Enzyme | How it cuts | Main product | Heat stability (cereal source) | Level in sound wheat flour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha-amylase** | *Endo*: cuts randomly inside the chains | Dextrins (shorter chains), which quickly thins a starch paste ("liquefying") | Survives to ~75–80 °C in dough | Low; rises sharply in sprouted grain |
| **Beta-amylase** | *Exo*: nibbles two glucose units (one maltose) at a time from chain ends; stops at branch points | Maltose | Inactivated at ~60–70 °C | Plentiful |

The two work as a team. Beta-amylase alone runs out of work quickly, because it can only attack ends and stalls at every branch. Alpha-amylase creates new ends by cutting chains in the middle, giving beta-amylase new places to start. So **alpha-amylase is usually the limiting enzyme** for sugar production in a dough.

### The substrate: damaged starch

Native starch granules are tightly packed and resist amylase at room temperature. During milling some granules are cracked, and this **damaged starch** (typically a few percent up to ~10 % of flour weight, higher for hard wheat and fine milling) absorbs more water and is readily attacked. It is the main source of maltose that keeps yeast fed after the flour's own free sugars run out ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)).

In the oven a second window opens. Wheat starch gelatinizes at roughly 55–70 °C ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)), making all of it attackable, while cereal alpha-amylase survives to ~75–80 °C. For a few minutes of the bake, enzymes feast on gelatinized starch. A modest amount of this gives sugars for crust colour and a softer crumb; too much gives sticky dextrins.

### Falling number: a measure of amylase

The **falling number** (Hagberg) test heats a flour–water slurry in a tube until the starch gelatinizes and then times how long a plunger takes to fall through it. More alpha-amylase liquefies the gel faster, so **a low falling number means high enzyme activity**.

| Falling number (s) | Enzyme activity | Typical bread outcome |
|---|---|---|
| > ~350 | Low | Slow fermentation late in the process, pale crust, drier crumb: add malt |
| ~250–350 | Balanced | Normal; typical target for bread flour |
| < ~200 | High (sprout-damaged wheat) | Sticky dough, dark crust, gummy crumb that is hard to slice |

These bands are standard milling-industry practice; mills often supplement low-enzyme flour with malted barley flour or fungal amylase, and the bag says so ("malted barley flour" in the ingredient list).

### Malt: diastatic versus non-diastatic

| Product | Enzymes | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Diastatic malt powder (malted barley or wheat flour) | Active amylases | Small additions (well under 1 % of flour; follow the supplier's rate) to feed long fermentations and improve crust colour in low-enzyme flour |
| Non-diastatic malt (malt extract, syrup, heat-treated powder) | Inactivated | Flavour, sweetness and colour only; bagels, pretzels |

Overdosing diastatic malt behaves like sprouted flour: slack, sticky dough and gummy crumb.

**Fungal alpha-amylase** (from *Aspergillus oryzae*, common in commercial flour) is inactivated at a lower temperature than cereal amylase, so it works during fermentation but stops early in the bake: low risk of gumminess. **Bacterial alpha-amylase** is so heat-stable that it can survive baking and keep liquefying the crumb afterwards; bakers avoid it except in controlled anti-staling blends.

### Proteases: cutting gluten

Proteases cut proteins into shorter pieces. For gluten that means a weaker, more extensible, eventually sticky dough ([SC-12](stage-1/science/sc-12-rheology.md)).

| Source | Where it matters | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Native flour proteases | Low activity in white flour; more in wholegrain; acid-activated | Slow slackening over very long or acidic fermentations |
| Long preferments, sourdough | Acidity plus many hours of enzyme activity | Over-ripe poolish or levain makes a slacker final dough; dough that "breaks down" after a very long cold retard |
| Autolyse (flour + water rest, 20–60 min) | Mostly hydration and gluten self-organization; a little protease activity | Smoother, more extensible dough with less mixing [Calvel] |
| Fungal protease (commercial additive) | Crackers, biscuits, some pizza flours | Low-shrinkage, extensible dough |
| Pineapple (bromelain), kiwi (actinidin), papaya (papain), fig (ficin), fresh ginger | Gelatin desserts, fruit mousses, cream fillings | Gelatin never sets, or sets and then liquefies; dairy can turn bitter |

Fruit proteases are destroyed by cooking. Canned pineapple and briefly boiled fresh purée (bring to a boil for a minute or two) set normally. Agar or pectin gels are not proteins and are unaffected.

A related, non-enzymatic weakener: **glutathione** leaking from dead yeast cells breaks gluten disulfide bonds. It is why old or dead yeast, and some active dry yeasts, soften dough.

### Yeast's own enzymes

**Invertase** sits in the yeast cell wall and splits sucrose into glucose and fructose so fast that most of the sucrose in a sweet dough is inverted within minutes of mixing. **Maltase** inside the cell splits maltose into glucose once it is taken in. The practical upshot: added sucrose in dough behaves, for yeast and for browning, like invert sugar ([07.1](stage-1/module-07/lesson-01.md)).

### Lipase and lipoxygenase (briefly)

**Lipase** releases free fatty acids from fats. In wholemeal flour, where the germ's lipids and lipase coexist, this is the start of rancidity, which is why wholegrain flour keeps weeks rather than months at room temperature (store it cold). **Lipoxygenase** oxidizes unsaturated fatty acids with oxygen from mixing; a side effect is bleaching of the flour's yellow carotenoid pigments. Intensive, oxygen-rich mixing gives a whiter crumb with less wheaty flavour, a trade-off [Calvel] described for French bread; enzyme-active soy flour is added in some industrial doughs for exactly this whitening.

### The egg-yolk amylase trap

Egg yolk contains an alpha-amylase. In a pastry cream, the yolks and starch are together; if the cream is only heated until it thickens (~80–85 °C) and then pulled off, the yolk amylase can survive, and over the next hours it cuts the gelatinized starch: the chilled cream turns thin and runny. The fix is standard professional practice: **bring the cream to a full boil and keep whisking it at the boil for 1–2 minutes** after it thickens. The starch protects the yolk proteins from curdling at that temperature, which is why a starch-thickened cream can boil and a crème anglaise cannot. You test this in [X1-34](experiments/X1-34-starch-thickeners.md).

### Enzymes as anti-staling tools (industry)

Industrial bread stays soft for a week or more partly because of **maltogenic amylase**, a bacterial enzyme of intermediate heat stability. It survives long enough into the bake to trim the outer branches of amylopectin, which slows retrogradation ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md), [Gray 2003]), and is then inactivated before it can make the crumb gummy. Other bakery enzymes include xylanases (hemicellulases, for dough handling and volume, especially in wholemeal), glucose oxidase (dough strengthening), lipases (crumb softness) and transglutaminase (protein cross-linking). These are Stage 3 formulation tools.

## Kitchen demonstrations

1. **Pineapple versus gelatin (10 minutes plus 3 h chill).** Make three small cups of gelatin set with water: plain, with 30 g fresh pineapple purée, and with 30 g pineapple purée boiled for 2 minutes and cooled. Only the fresh-pineapple cup fails to set.
2. **Saliva amylase in a starch paste.** Cook 5 g cornstarch in 100 g water to a thick paste, split in two, cool to ~37 °C. Stir a teaspoon of saliva into one half. Within 10–20 minutes it is visibly thinner. That is exactly what yolk amylase does to an under-boiled pastry cream.
3. **Iodine starch test.** A drop of iodine tincture turns starch blue-black. Put drops on pieces of the saliva-treated and untreated paste after 30 minutes; the treated one stains less intensely as chains are cut.

## Misconceptions

- **"Enzymes are alive."** They are proteins, not organisms. They work in flour straight from the bag once there is water, and they stop when heat denatures them, whether or not any yeast is alive.
- **"More enzyme activity is always better for flavour and colour."** There is an optimum. Sprouted, high-enzyme flour makes gummy bread; the target is balance.
- **"Autolyse works because proteases break down gluten."** Mostly it works through hydration and the gluten network organizing itself without mechanical work. Protease activity in white flour is small over 20–60 minutes.
- **"Boiling a pastry cream will curdle the yolks."** With enough starch present, the cream tolerates and needs a boil.

## Measuring it

| Question | Instrument | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Is my flour malted? | Read the ingredient list | Stage 1 |
| Is my dough over-enzymed? | Stickiness, slackening over time, gummy crumb, dark crust | Stage 1 (observation) |
| Did my pastry cream survive storage? | Spoon-coat or line-spread test at 0 h and 24 h | Stage 1 |
| How much alpha-amylase is in this flour? | Falling number apparatus (Hagberg) | Stage 3 (mill labs; ask your supplier for the spec sheet in Stage 2) |
| How much damaged starch? | Enzymatic or amperometric damaged-starch methods | Stage 3 |

## Check yourself

1. A mill sends two lots of bread flour: falling number 420 s and 180 s. Predict how each performs in a 16-hour cold-retarded loaf, and what you would add to one of them.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The 420 s flour has low amylase: yeast may run short of maltose late in the long fermentation, and the crust will be pale; add a small dose of diastatic malt. The 180 s flour is high-enzyme: over 16 h expect a slack, sticky dough and a gummy, dark-crusted loaf; shorten fermentation or blend with a higher-falling-number flour.

</details>

2. Why does the enzyme activity during the first minutes of baking matter more than the activity during mixing, for crumb gumminess?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

During mixing, only damaged starch is available. In the oven, starch gelatinizes at ~55–70 °C and becomes fully accessible while alpha-amylase survives to ~75–80 °C, so that window produces the dextrins that make crumb gummy.

</details>

3. You add sucrose to a brioche. Ten minutes after mixing, what sugars are in the dough?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Mostly glucose and fructose: yeast invertase splits sucrose very quickly. Plus maltose from starch.

</details>

4. A kiwi-and-cream filling for a roulade turns bitter and weepy after a few hours. Explain and fix.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Actinidin in fresh kiwi is a protease that attacks milk proteins (bitter peptides) and any gelatin. Cook the kiwi purée briefly to inactivate it, or keep fruit and cream apart until service.

</details>
