## Why this lesson exists

A good paste is only half of a good éclair. The same paste piped with a worn tip, baked in a fan oven at the wrong temperature, taken out five minutes early and filled the night before gives a lopsided, collapsed, soggy product. This lesson covers everything after the paste: piping to a repeatable size, controlling how the surface opens (fork marks, egg wash, craquelin), choosing an oven profile for your oven, drying the shell, filling, glazing and storing. It ends with a clinic, because choux faults are very diagnosable once you know the mechanism: most have one of about six causes, and the shell itself usually tells you which.

## You will be able to

- Pipe éclairs and choux balls to a target length, diameter and weight, within ±10 % of each other.
- Make and use craquelin, and explain why it gives an even, round rise.
- Choose and justify an oven profile (conventional or fan) and judge doneness by colour, weight loss and rigidity, not by time.
- Fill, glaze with fondant at 35–37 °C, and schedule assembly so shells are crisp when eaten.
- Diagnose a failed choux from its appearance and the batch record, and name the correction.

## Piping

Consistent piping is the first control on consistent baking: pieces of different size on one tray cannot all be done at the same time.

| Piece | Tip | Target raw size | Raw weight (R1-31 paste) | Spacing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Éclair** | **16–18 mm French star** (many fine teeth) or plain | **12–14 cm** long, 2.5–3 cm wide | 40–45 g | 4–5 cm between pieces |
| **Choux ball / profiterole** | 12–14 mm plain | **3–4 cm** diameter, ~2 cm high | 12–18 g (15 g for 3.5 cm) | 4 cm |
| Chouquette, mini profiterole | 10 mm plain | 2.5 cm | 7–8 g | 3 cm |

**Why a French star for éclairs.** A French star tip has many narrow teeth. The ridges it leaves are lines of weakness that run along the length of the éclair, so the surface opens along them in fine, parallel cracks as the shell expands, instead of bursting at one random point. The result is straighter, more even éclairs. A plain tip followed by fork marks (below) does the same job.

**Technique.**

1. Line a flat tray with parchment or a perforated silicone mat. A dab of paste under each corner stops parchment lifting. Draw guide lines on the underside of the parchment (for example two lines 13 cm apart for éclairs, or a grid of 3.5 cm circles) with a pencil, and turn it over.
2. Fill the bag no more than two-thirds, twist it closed and push out air pockets; an air pocket gives a gap in the piping.
3. **Éclairs:** hold the bag at about 45°, tip about 1 cm above the tray. Squeeze with steady pressure and move at a steady speed so the width stays constant; at the end, stop squeezing, then flick the tip back over the end of the éclair to cut the paste. Do not drag the tip up at the end, which leaves a thick tail.
4. **Balls:** hold the bag vertical, tip about 1 cm above the tray. Squeeze without moving until the ball reaches the diameter, stop squeezing, then flick the tip sideways in a small circle to cut off.
5. **Weigh.** Pipe the first two pieces onto a tray on the scale. Adjust until they are within ±2 g of the target, then pipe the rest by eye against the guide lines and check-weigh every fifth piece.
6. **Flatten peaks.** Tap down any point or tail with a fingertip dipped in egg wash or water. A point dries and browns before the rest and can crack the top.

## Controlling the surface

### Egg wash and fork marks

Egg wash (whole egg plus a pinch of salt, or egg with 10–20 % water or milk, beaten and strained) gives a glossy, even, golden-brown surface and smooths small irregularities. Apply it thinly with a soft brush. Two cautions: wash that runs down onto the tray glues the base and restrains the rise on that side, giving a lopsided piece; and heavy wash browns early and can set the surface before the shell has finished expanding.

On éclairs piped with a plain tip, draw the back of a fork, dipped in egg wash, lightly along the length of each éclair. The shallow grooves give the same controlled, parallel cracking as a French star tip.

For craquelin-topped pieces, skip the egg wash: the craquelin gives colour and a surface of its own.

### Craquelin

Craquelin is a thin disc of sweet crumble dough laid on top of the raw choux. It is made from **butter, brown sugar and flour in roughly 1 : 1.2 : 1** by weight, mixed to a smooth dough, rolled between parchment to **1–2 mm**, frozen, and cut into discs the size of the piped piece (for éclairs, strips).

In the oven the disc does three things:

1. **Its weight and cohesion hold the surface together.** As the choux expands, the craquelin stretches and cracks into a fine, even crackle, spreading the stress over the whole top. The steam pushes the shell up evenly, so balls come out **round, symmetrical and uniform** instead of with one bulging crack.
2. **It delays the surface setting.** The disc shades and insulates the top, so the choux surface stays extensible a little longer and expands further before it sets.
3. **It adds a crisp, sweet, browned crust** that is pleasant to eat and hides small piping flaws.

The disc must be the same diameter as the piece (or 1–2 mm wider) and applied frozen or very cold, straight onto the raw paste. Too thick (over ~2 mm) and it weighs the piece down and flattens it; too thin and it melts into a patchy film. Craquelin can be coloured (a little powdered food colour in the dough) or flavoured (cocoa for part of the flour).

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Pastry shops pipe choux onto trays, top with frozen craquelin discs, freeze the trays, then store the frozen pieces in bags. They bake from frozen as needed, usually with 3–5 minutes added. Frozen raw choux behaves at least as well as fresh; many chefs find the rise more even. It is also how you should run your own experiments that need several bakes of identical pieces ([X1-38](experiments/X1-38-choux-oven.md)).

</div>

## Baking: the oven profile

The oven's job, from [14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md), is to deliver enough heat early to vaporize water fast and inflate the shell before its surface sets, and then to dry the shell thoroughly without burning it.

| Oven | Profile | Éclairs 40–45 g | Balls 15 g |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conventional (top and bottom heat, no fan)** | Preheat to **200 °C (390 °F)**; bake 15 min; reduce to **170–180 °C (340–355 °F)** without opening the door | 35–45 min total | 28–35 min total |
| **Fan (convection)** | Constant **170–190 °C (340–375 °F)** depending on the oven; start at 180 °C | 30–40 min | 25–30 min |
| **Both** | Do not open the door for the first ~20 min (25 min for éclairs); in the last 5–10 min, prop the door open 1–2 cm with a wooden spoon handle to vent steam (optional) | | |

Why these numbers:

- **Too hot throughout** (220 °C and above, or strong top heat): the surface sets and browns before the shell has finished expanding. The top bursts irregularly and the shell looks done while the walls are still wet; it collapses on cooling.
- **Too cool throughout** (160 °C and below): the water heats slowly, the rise is slow and small, and the paste may slump before it sets. You get flat, dense, pale pieces.
- **The step-down:** high heat for the expansion, lower heat for the long drying, so the shell is fully dry by the time it is golden-brown.
- **Fan ovens** dry and set the surface faster, so they need a lower temperature than conventional ovens; a strong fan can also blow the soft pieces lopsided early in the bake. If your fan is strong, set the lowest temperature in the range, or turn the fan off for the first 15 min.
- **Venting** lets the steam out of the oven so the atmosphere around the shells is drier in the final phase; it makes the last part of the drying more efficient.

Bake **one tray at a time**, on the middle shelf, unless you have tested two trays. X1-38 compares four profiles side by side in your own oven.

### Doneness

Choux is judged by several cues together:

| Cue | Done |
|---|---|
| Colour | Even, deep golden-brown, **including inside the cracks and ridges**. Pale cracks mean wet walls |
| Weight | Pieces feel light. Weighing a piece before and after baking: bake loss typically about **35–45 %** of raw weight (record your own number once you have a result you like) |
| Rigidity | Press the side of a piece gently: it resists and does not dent |
| Sacrificial piece | Cut one piece open: the cavity is large; the walls are thin (2–4 mm) with only a thin, slightly moist membrane inside, not a layer of wet paste |

When done, take the tray out, and immediately **pierce each piece** in its base with a skewer or a small piping tip, so the remaining steam escapes instead of condensing inside. Cool on a rack. For extra dryness, you can return pierced shells to the switched-off oven, door ajar, for 10 min.

## Filling

Choux are filled cold, through small holes in the base, or split and filled.

- **Crème pâtissière** ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)), beaten smooth for 20–40 s ([12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md)), plain or flavoured (coffee, chocolate). The classic éclair filling: firm enough to hold in a long shell.
- **Crème diplomate** (in R1-26): lighter, softer; good for profiteroles and choux balls.
- **Chantilly** ([R1-27](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md)): lightest; must be eaten within a few hours.
- **Ice cream**: classic profiteroles, served with warm chocolate sauce.

**Filling through the base.** Make 2–3 holes along the base of an éclair (one for a ball) with a 5–6 mm plain tip. Fill with a 6–8 mm plain or Bismarck (long, narrow) tip, squeezing until you feel resistance and cream just appears at the next hole. The most reliable check is weight: weigh a shell, fill it, and weigh again. For a 13 cm éclair, **40–50 g** of pastry cream; for a 3.5 cm ball, **15–20 g**. Wipe the holes flat.

**Why choux go soggy.** The filling has high water activity (pastry cream is well above 0.9) and the dry shell very low; water migrates from the cream into the shell until the two approach equilibrium, and the shell softens from the inside out. This begins within an hour and is obvious after 4–6 h. It is the same moisture-migration problem as in filled tarts ([X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md), [17.1](stage-1/module-17/lesson-01.md)). Controls: fill as close to serving as possible, dry the shells well, keep filled choux cold, and, in professional practice, spray the inside with melted cocoa butter as a fat barrier.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Filled choux hold pastry cream, a rich, moist, egg-and-milk food. Keep them at **≤ 5 °C** and eat within **24 h** (best the same day). Do not leave them at room temperature for more than 2 h in total ([FDA-FoodCode]). Allergens: gluten, egg, milk; nuts if using praline.

</div>

## Glazing

### Fondant

Pâtissier's fondant is a mass of very small sucrose crystals in a saturated syrup ([13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md), [SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)). The fine crystals give it its opaque, white, glossy look. The whole technique is temperature control:

| Fondant temperature | Result |
|---|---|
| Below ~32 °C | Too thick; coats unevenly and heavily; tears the shell's surface |
| **35–37 °C** | Flows, coats in a thin layer and sets glossy within 1–2 min |
| Above ~38–40 °C | The smallest crystals dissolve; on cooling the sugar recrystallizes onto the remaining crystals, which grow large enough to scatter light: the glaze sets **dull, grey and brittle**, and may crack |

**Method.** Warm the fondant in a bowl over barely simmering water or in short microwave bursts, stirring, to **35–37 °C** (probe). Thin with a little 1:1 sugar syrup ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)), a few grams at a time, until it coats the back of a spoon in an opaque layer that just flows. Flavour and colour: coffee extract, or melted dark chocolate (about 15–25 % of the fondant weight, thinned with a little more syrup). Dip the top of each éclair, lift it vertically so the excess runs off, wipe the edges clean with a finger, and set it upright. Re-warm the fondant to 35–37 °C whenever it thickens.

### Chocolate glaze

A glaze of chocolate and cream (a ganache) or a shiny chocolate glaze is the other classic finish. The mechanism (an emulsion of cocoa butter and cocoa solids in a water phase) is in [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md) and [R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md). For now, R1-32 gives a simple dipping ganache used at 30–35 °C.

## Storage

| State | How | Life |
|---|---|---|
| Raw paste | Covered, film in contact, ≤ 5 °C | 24 h |
| Piped raw pieces | Frozen on trays, then bagged | About 1 month; bake from frozen, +3–5 min |
| Baked, unfilled shells | Airtight, room temperature | 1–2 days; re-crisp at 160 °C for 5–8 min, cool before filling |
| Baked shells, frozen | Airtight | About 1 month; thaw 10 min, re-crisp as above |
| Filled, glazed choux | ≤ 5 °C | Same day for texture; 24 h maximum |

## The choux clinic

Choux faults show on the shell. Before diagnosing, gather the batch record: panade loss, egg weight, paste temperatures, piece weight, oven profile, total bake time and bake loss. Then use the [choux troubleshooting page](troubleshooting/choux.md) and work through these cases. Write your diagnosis before opening each answer.

**Case 1.** Éclairs rise well and are golden-brown at 25 min. The baker takes them out. Ten minutes later they are wrinkled and about 30 % lower. Cut open, the walls are 5–6 mm thick and pasty inside.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Underbaked. The shells had expanded and coloured, but the walls had not dried; the shape was supported by steam, which condensed on cooling. Correction: bake to rigidity and weight loss, not colour. Lower the second-stage temperature (e.g. to 170 °C) so they can stay in longer (35–45 min for éclairs) without over-browning; pierce and vent. See [Choux collapsed after baking](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=choux-collapsed-after-baking).

</details>

**Case 2.** Profiteroles spread to 5 cm puddles on the tray before baking and bake into flat, smooth-topped discs about 1.5 cm high. The paste "flowed off the spatula". The baker used all 250 g of egg because "the recipe said 250 g".

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Over-egged paste: the panade could not absorb 250 g of egg on this occasion (likely under-dried, or smaller-absorbing flour). The paste failed the V test (long, dripping tail) and could not hold a piped shape. Correction: reserve 10–20 % of the egg and stop at the V test; dry the panade to a film and record the weight loss. To rescue a runny paste, make a small extra panade (no eggs) and beat it in. See [Paste too runny](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-too-runny).

</details>

**Case 3.** Choux balls without craquelin, fan oven at 200 °C. They rose tall but with one large, jagged split on top, and all lean in the same direction, towards the back of the oven.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fan oven too hot, and strong airflow. The surface set early and fast on the side facing the fan, so the steam forced its way out through one weak point instead of cracking evenly; the side exposed to the fan set first and the pieces leaned away from it. Correction: 170–180 °C fan, or fan off for the first 15 min; flatten peaks before baking; use craquelin or a thin egg wash for an even surface; rotate the tray only after 20–25 min if needed. See [Cracked irregular tops](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=cracked-irregular-tops) and [Lopsided or uneven rise](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=lopsided-or-uneven-rise).

</details>

**Case 4.** Éclairs were perfect at 17:00. They were filled with pastry cream at 18:00, glazed, and served at 13:00 the next day. The shells were soft and chewy and the fondant had lost its shine.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Two problems of time. Moisture migrated from the pastry cream into the shells over 19 h, and the fondant absorbed moisture in the refrigerator and dulled. Correction: bake and store shells dry (or frozen), fill and glaze within 1–4 h of serving; if production requires earlier filling, dry the shells harder and line them with a cocoa-butter spray. See [Soggy shells after filling](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=soggy-shells-after-filling).

</details>

**Case 5.** The paste looked greasy after drying the panade for 5 minutes on high heat "to be sure". It absorbed only 180 g of egg, looked grainy, and the shells were flat and oily with a heavy base.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Over-dried panade: the paste was driven so dry that it could no longer hold the butterfat, which separated. A split paste cannot form a smooth emulsion with the egg, absorbs less of it, and bakes flat and greasy. Correction: dry for 1–3 min on medium heat, stop at the film and 75–80 °C, and weigh the loss (15–35 g for R1-31). A mildly split paste can sometimes be rescued by beating in the first eggs vigorously; a badly split one should be remade. See [Paste split or greasy](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-split-or-greasy).

</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why does craquelin give rounder profiteroles than egg wash?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The cohesive, weighted disc holds the top surface together and cracks finely and evenly as the shell expands, spreading stress over the whole top instead of letting the steam break out at one weak point. It also shades the top so the surface stays extensible a little longer. Egg wash does not restrain the surface; it browns and can set it early.

</details>

2. A fan oven set to 200 °C gives dark, cracked shells with wet interiors at 25 min. What do you change first, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Lower the temperature to 170–180 °C fan and bake longer (30–40 min). Fan heat sets and browns the surface fast; at 200 °C the colour arrives long before the walls have dried. A lower temperature lets drying keep pace with browning.

</details>

3. Your éclairs lose 25 % of their weight in baking. The ones you liked best last time lost 40 %. What does that tell you?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

They are underbaked (walls still wet) and will soften or collapse on cooling and go soggy faster after filling. Bake longer at the second-stage temperature, vent, and pierce the shells.

</details>

4. You warmed the fondant to 45 °C "so it would flow". What will you see an hour later and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A dull, greyish, possibly cracked glaze. At 45 °C the finest sugar crystals dissolved; as the glaze cooled, the sugar recrystallized onto fewer, larger crystals, which scatter light differently and form a brittle layer. Keep fondant at 35–37 °C and thin it with syrup, not heat.

</details>

5. You need 60 profiteroles for an event at 19:00. Write a schedule for making them so they are crisp when served.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Example: 2–3 days ahead, make paste, pipe with craquelin and freeze raw (or bake and freeze shells). The day before, make pastry cream ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)) and chill. On the day, bake from frozen by 14:00–15:00 (or re-crisp frozen baked shells), cool fully; make diplomate around 16:00–17:00; fill between 17:00 and 18:00; keep at ≤ 5 °C; serve at 19:00. Filling within 1–2 h of service keeps the shells crisp.

</details>
