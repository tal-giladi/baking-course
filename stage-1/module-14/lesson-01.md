## Why this lesson exists

Choux paste has no yeast, no baking powder and no whipped foam, yet it rises two to three times its piped height and bakes into a hollow shell. The only leavening is the water already in the paste turning to steam. That makes choux the purest steam-leavened product you will make, and a demanding one: the paste must be soft enough to stretch as steam inflates it, strong enough to set before the steam escapes, and dry enough afterwards to stand up on its own. Every one of those properties is set on the stove and in the mixing bowl, before the tray goes near the oven. This lesson explains what each step of the paste builds, so that when you add "eggs until it is right" you know what "right" is measuring.

## You will be able to

- Explain how a choux paste rises and sets, naming the roles of steam, gelatinized starch, gluten, egg protein and fat.
- Make a panade to a measured endpoint (film on the pan, 75–80 °C, recorded weight loss) and explain what the drying step changes.
- Add eggs to a paste at ≤ 60 °C and stop at the V-test endpoint, recording the egg weight as a batch variable.
- Predict the effect of milk versus water, bread flour versus all-purpose flour, and under- or over-egging on the baked shell.

## What choux is, as a system

Choux (French for "cabbage", for the shape of a cream puff) is a **cooked paste**: flour is gelatinized in boiling liquid and fat, then diluted with raw egg until it is soft and pipeable. In the oven, water inside the paste vaporizes and inflates it; the egg proteins and starch then set the expanded walls into a rigid shell. It is related to two things you already know:

- The **tangzhong** of [06.2](stage-1/module-06/lesson-02.md): cooked starch holds far more water than raw starch. Choux carries this to the extreme; the finished paste holds roughly three times the flour weight in water.
- **Oven spring** in bread ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)): gas expanding inside a dough until the structure sets. In bread the gas starts as CO₂ in yeast-made bubbles. In choux there are no bubbles to start from; steam forms inside the paste and pushes it apart into one large cavity.

The finished paste is a soft, glossy emulsion: a starch–gluten matrix gelled by cooking, with butterfat dispersed through it and held there partly by egg-yolk emulsifiers ([09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md)), and a large amount of free water from the milk, water and egg.

### The formula and what each ingredient does

The standard proportions in [R1-31](recipes/R1-31-pate-a-choux.md) are a classic balance widely used in professional practice:

| Ingredient | Grams | Baker's % (flour = 100) | Job in the system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 125 | 89.3 | Main source of steam; dissolves salt and sugar; gelatinizes the starch |
| Whole milk | 125 | 89.3 | Water plus milk protein and lactose: more colour, more tender shell |
| Unsalted butter | 110 | 78.6 | Tenderizes, carries flavour, keeps the paste extensible; too much weakens the shell |
| Salt | 5 | 3.6 | Flavour; tightens gluten slightly |
| Sugar | 5 | 3.6 | Colour and flavour; kept low so the shell does not brown before it is dry |
| Bread flour (or all-purpose) | 140 | 100 | Starch to gelatinize and gluten proteins to stretch: the framework |
| Whole egg | about 250 (220–270) | about 179 (157–193) | Water for steam, protein to set the walls, yolk emulsifiers, colour |

The egg line is a range on purpose. The amount is decided by the paste, not by the formula. That is the single most important idea in this lesson, and it is why you **weigh** the eggs you add and record the number.

### How much water is in the paste?

Using typical composition values ([USDA-FDC]): whole milk is about 87–88 % water, butter about 16 %, whole egg about 75–76 %.

| Source | Water (g) |
|---|---|
| Water | 125 |
| Milk (125 × 0.875) | ~109 |
| Butter (110 × 0.16) | ~18 |
| Egg (250 × 0.76) | ~190 |
| Minus evaporation while drying the panade | −15 to −35 |
| **Water in the finished paste** | **~410–425 g** |

That is roughly 290–300 % of the flour weight, in a paste that still holds a piped shape. Only gelatinized starch makes that possible. It is also about 55 % of the paste by weight: this is the steam that will inflate the shell, and most of it must leave in the oven for the shell to be crisp.

## Step 1: the panade

"Panade" is the cooked flour paste before the eggs go in.

### Boil liquid and fat together

Water, milk, butter (cubed, ~1–2 cm), salt and sugar go into the pan together over medium heat. The aim is that **the butter is completely melted at the moment the liquid reaches a full, rolling boil**. If the liquid boils for minutes while a block of butter is still melting, you lose an unknown amount of water and the paste needs a different amount of egg. Cubing the butter and using medium, not maximum, heat solves this.

The boil matters for two reasons. First, the flour must meet liquid at or near 100 °C so its starch gelatinizes at once. Second, a rolling boil means the fat is dispersed as fine droplets through the liquid rather than floating as a layer; the flour then hydrates evenly.

### Add all the flour at once

Take the pan off the heat, add the sifted flour **in one go** and stir hard with a wooden spoon or stiff spatula. Within 20–30 seconds it forms a lumpy mass that pulls into a smooth paste. Adding the flour gradually does not work: the first part gelatinizes instantly in excess liquid into a thin paste, and the later additions form lumps that are coated in gel on the outside and dry inside. This is the same lump mechanism as in pastry cream ([12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md)), handled here by speed and force instead of dispersion in sugar.

### Dry it: dessèchement

Put the pan back over medium heat and keep stirring, pressing the paste against the base and folding it over, for **1–3 minutes**. Endpoints:

| Cue | What you see or measure |
|---|---|
| Paste forms a ball | Leaves the sides of the pan cleanly, moves as one smooth mass |
| Film on the pan | A thin, dry skin of paste coats the base of a stainless or aluminium pan (non-stick pans do not show it: use the other cues) |
| Temperature | Paste at about **75–80 °C** (probe in the centre of the mass) |
| Weight loss | Weigh the pan and contents before adding the flour and after drying: typically **15–35 g** of water lost for the R1-31 batch |
| Sheen | Slightly glossy; a faint sheen of fat is fine; beads or pools of butter are not |

What drying does:

1. **Completes gelatinization.** At 75–80 °C with this much water, most of the wheat starch has swollen and the paste has set into a firm gel ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)). Swollen starch is what later holds the egg's water without the paste turning liquid.
2. **Removes water.** Every gram of water driven off here is room for more egg later. More egg means more protein to set the walls and more water for steam, which is why a well-dried panade gives bigger, lighter shells.
3. **Works the gluten.** Stirring hot paste stretches and partly heat-sets the gluten proteins. They are not developed like a bread dough's, but they give the paste its elastic stretch in the oven. Bread flour gives more of this.

There is a limit. Dried too long or too hot (5 minutes or more, or on high heat), the paste gets so dry and the gluten–starch matrix so tight that it can no longer hold the fat: butter seeps out and the paste looks greasy and grainy. A split panade absorbs eggs poorly, gives a greasy, heavy paste and flat, oily shells. See [Paste split or greasy](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-split-or-greasy).

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** The panade is where the choux "structure budget" is fixed. Flour sets how much starch and gluten there are; the drying sets how much water is left; together they decide how much egg the paste can absorb before it becomes too soft to hold a shape. Two cooks using the same formula get different egg quantities mainly because they dried the panade differently. Recording the panade weight loss turns that from a mystery into a measurement.

</div>

## Step 2: cooling and eggs

### Cool to 60 °C or below

Transfer the panade to a mixer bowl (paddle) or a large bowl and beat it on low speed, or by hand, for **1–3 minutes** until steam stops rising strongly and the paste is at **≤ 60 °C**; 50–60 °C is ideal.

- **Too hot (above ~65 °C):** egg proteins that meet the hot paste start to set on contact ([09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md): egg white begins to set around 62–65 °C). You get specks of cooked egg and lose some of the egg's ability to stretch and then set in the oven.
- **Too cold (below ~35–40 °C):** the butterfat firms, the paste stiffens, the eggs take longer to incorporate, and the finished paste is duller and less smooth. It still bakes, but less well.

### Add the eggs gradually

Beat the eggs together (and strain if they contain stringy chalazae), weigh them, and set aside 10–20 % (in R1-31, about 40–50 g of the 250 g). Add the rest in **4–6 additions**, each fully absorbed before the next.

Each addition goes through the same sequence, which is worth recognising:

1. The paste breaks into slippery, separated pieces that slide around the bowl. It looks ruined.
2. With 20–40 seconds of beating, the egg is absorbed into the gel and emulsified with the fat.
3. The paste comes back together, smoother and softer than before.

Only add the next portion once step 3 is reached. Adding too fast leaves a paste with free egg in it that never fully comes together, and it tempts you to add too much.

As the egg goes in, the paste changes from a matte, stiff, "short" mass that tears to a **smooth, glossy, elastic paste** that stretches before it breaks.

### The endpoint: the V test

Stop adding egg when the paste passes this test, then check again after every small addition of the reserve (5–10 g at a time):

| Test | Correct paste |
|---|---|
| **V test** (bec d'oiseau, "bird's beak") | Scoop some paste on a spatula or paddle, lift it and turn it so it hangs. It slowly falls away and leaves a hanging **V-shaped tail, 3–5 cm**, that holds its shape and does not drip |
| Trench test | Draw a finger or spatula through the paste in the bowl: the trench closes slowly, over several seconds, with soft edges |
| Gloss | Surface shiny, like satin |
| Piping | Pipes smoothly; the piped shape holds its outline; the tip of a piped ball settles without spreading into a puddle |

Too stiff: the paste falls off the spatula in a lump or stays on it without forming a tail; the trench stays open; the surface is dull. Too soft: the tail stretches long and drips; the trench closes at once; piped shapes spread.

There is no fixing an over-egged paste except by making more panade (without eggs) and combining it. That is why you reserve part of the egg and approach the endpoint slowly. Any egg left over goes into the egg wash.

<div class="callout key">

**Key idea:** The amount of egg is set by the endpoint, not the formula. The formula gives a range; the V test gives the answer; your scale records it. When you run [X1-37](experiments/X1-37-choux-eggs.md), you will see that a 20 % error either way changes the shell as much as any other variable in the process.

</div>

### Paste temperature and holding

The finished paste is usually at **30–40 °C**. Warm paste pipes smoothly and bakes well. Pipe it within about an hour, keeping the bowl covered (a damp cloth or film in contact) so the surface does not dry into a skin that makes lumps in the bag. Paste can be refrigerated, film in contact, for up to 24 h; it pipes stiffer and is best rewarmed briefly by beating. Professional kitchens more often pipe the shapes and **freeze them raw** on trays, then bag them; they bake from frozen with a few extra minutes ([R1-31](recipes/R1-31-pate-a-choux.md) gives the details).

## Step 3: in the oven

The oven work is covered in detail in [14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md) and tested in [X1-38](experiments/X1-38-choux-oven.md); here is the mechanism.

| Phase (conventional oven, 200 °C falling to 175 °C, 40 g éclair) | What happens inside | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 min | Paste heats from ~30 °C; the surface dries and a thin skin begins to form | Little change; surface loses its shine |
| 5–15 min | Interior reaches ~100 °C; water vaporizes. Water becoming steam at 100 °C and atmospheric pressure occupies roughly 1,600–1,700 times its liquid volume ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)). The soft, extensible paste is pushed outward; small steam pockets merge into one large cavity | Rapid rise; the surface splits into cracks as it stretches; still pale |
| 15–20 min | Egg proteins coagulate (65–80 °C and above) and the starch gel dries and firms; the walls lose their stretch and set in the expanded shape | Rise stops; colour starts in the ridges of the cracks |
| 20–40 min | Drying: steam keeps leaving through the walls; the shell becomes rigid; the surface passes 100 °C and browns (Maillard reactions from milk and egg protein and sugars, [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)) | Colour deepens to golden-brown, including inside the cracks; pieces feel light |
| Cooling | Remaining steam condenses; pressure inside drops | A well-dried shell keeps its shape; an underbaked one wrinkles and sinks |

Two rules follow from this sequence and are behind most choux failures:

1. **Do not open the oven in the first ~20 minutes.** Until the walls have set, the shape is held up by steam pressure. Opening the door drops the oven temperature and pressure support at once; the unset walls sag and never fully recover.
2. **The shell must be dry before it leaves the oven.** A shell that looks brown but still has thick, moist walls is held up only by the steam inside it. When that steam condenses on cooling, the shell collapses. Colour is not enough; the pieces must feel light and rigid.

### Why the rise is even (or not)

The steam pushes equally in all directions, so the shape of the rise depends on where the paste is weakest. A uniform piped shape with an even surface expands evenly. A thick tail, an uneven thickness, a dried skin on one side, or a surface that set early in one place (a fan blowing across it, or strong top heat) makes the shell split and bulge there. Fork marks, egg wash and craquelin, all in [14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md), are ways of controlling where the surface gives.

## Ingredient choices

| Choice | Effect on the paste | Effect on the shell | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water only** (no milk) | Slightly softer panade | Paler, crisper, more rigid shell; browns slowly so it dries well before colouring | Very crisp shells; savoury choux (gougères); shells that will be glazed dark |
| **Milk only** | Richer | Browns faster and deeper (lactose and milk protein), softer, more tender, less crisp; can colour before it has dried, so bake a little cooler | Tender, richer shells eaten soon after filling |
| **Half milk, half water** (R1-31) | — | Balanced colour, crispness and tenderness | General use |
| **Bread flour** (~12–13 % protein) | Absorbs more egg; more elastic | Stronger shell, more volume, holds shape, more upright walls; can crack more boldly | Éclairs, larger pieces, pieces that will be glazed or stacked |
| **All-purpose flour** (~10–11 %) | Absorbs a little less egg | Slightly smaller, more tender shell | Profiteroles, most home use; works well |
| **Cake or pastry flour** (~7–9 %) | Absorbs less egg; weaker | Lower rise; prone to collapse | Not recommended |
| **More butter** (e.g. 100 % of flour) | Softer, richer paste | More tender and flavourful, weaker walls, flatter; greasy if the panade is over-dried | Rich choux eaten fresh |
| **Less butter** (e.g. 50 %) | Firmer | Crisper, more rigid, a little drier-tasting | Structural pieces |
| **More sugar** (> ~5 % of flour) | — | Browns earlier; risk of dark shells with wet interiors | Only with lower oven temperatures |

## What goes wrong

- **Paste too runny.** Too much egg, eggs added too fast, or an under-dried panade. See [Paste too runny](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-too-runny).
- **Paste split or greasy.** Over-dried panade, butter not melted before the boil, or eggs added to a paste that was too hot. See [Paste split or greasy](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-split-or-greasy).
- **Choux did not rise.** Paste too runny or too stiff, oven too cool, or the paste sat uncovered and skinned. See [Choux did not rise or baked flat](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=choux-did-not-rise-or-baked-flat).
- **Collapsed after baking.** Underbaked walls, or the door opened too early. See [Choux collapsed after baking](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=choux-collapsed-after-baking).

## Check yourself

1. Two bakers use R1-31 exactly. One uses 215 g of egg, the other 265 g, and both pass the V test. Give two likely reasons.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The main one is different drying of the panade: the second baker drove off more water (longer or hotter drying), so the paste could absorb more egg. Others: different flour protein and absorption, different egg composition (more yolk makes egg thicker, so less is needed to soften the paste), and different paste temperature when the eggs went in (a cooler, stiffer paste reads as needing more egg).

</details>

2. Why must the flour go in all at once, and why off the heat?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

All at once so that all of it meets hot liquid at the same time and gelatinizes into one paste; added gradually, the first flour thickens the liquid and the rest forms lumps with a gelled skin and dry centre. Off the heat so you can stir it in fast without the base scorching while the flour is still dry and lumpy; the paste goes back on the heat for drying once it is smooth.

</details>

3. A student adds eggs to the panade straight from the stove at 80 °C. What will they see in the bowl and in the oven?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Some egg protein sets on contact (egg white begins to coagulate around 62–65 °C), giving small cooked specks and a less smooth, less glossy paste. Part of the protein that should have stretched with the expanding shell and then set in the oven is already set, so the shells rise less and may be rougher.

</details>

4. Explain in terms of steam and setting why opening the oven at 10 minutes flattens choux, but opening it at 30 minutes does not.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 10 minutes the shell is still expanding and its walls are unset; its shape is held up by steam pressure inside a soft paste. Opening the door drops the air temperature, the surface cools and some steam condenses, so the walls sag, and once cooled and partly set they cannot stretch again. By 30 minutes the egg proteins have coagulated and the starch has dried, so the shell is rigid and holds its own shape.

</details>

5. You want crisper shells that stay crisp longer after filling. Name two formula changes and one process change.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Formula: replace the milk with water; use bread flour; reduce the butter a little (or the sugar). Process: bake longer at a lower second-stage temperature so the walls dry more (higher bake loss), vent the oven for the last few minutes, and pierce the shells to release steam. (Filling close to serving time matters most of all; see [14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md).)

</details>
