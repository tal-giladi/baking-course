## Why this lesson exists

Up to now your doughs have had four ingredients. Enriched doughs add sugar, fat, eggs and milk, and each of them changes fermentation, gluten development, crumb, crust colour and shelf life at the same time. If you treat an enriched dough like a lean dough with extras, it rises slowly, mixes badly and burns before it bakes through. This lesson gives you a model of what each enrichment does, the baker's-percentage ranges that define each family of enriched bread, and the arithmetic to compute the true water content of a dough whose liquid is mostly milk and eggs.

## You will be able to

1. Place any yeasted formula on the lean-to-rich spectrum from its sugar, fat and egg percentages, and predict how it will ferment, mix and bake.
2. Explain why a little sugar speeds fermentation and a lot slows it, and choose a yeast type and dose for sugar levels from 0 to 20 %.
3. Explain why fat is added after the gluten is developed, and what it does to crumb, volume and staling.
4. State the three jobs eggs do in dough and the two things milk does that water does not, including what the "scald the milk" advice is actually about.
5. Compute the true hydration of an enriched formula from the water content of milk, eggs, butter and honey.

## The lean-to-rich spectrum

Bakers describe yeasted doughs by where they sit on a scale from lean (flour, water, salt, yeast) to rich (heavy in sugar, fat and eggs). All values are baker's percentages on flour. The ranges are standard professional practice. Individual formulas vary, but a formula far outside its family's range usually behaves like a different family.

| Family | Sugar % | Fat % | Egg % | Milk | Example in this course |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lean | 0–2 | 0–2 | 0 | Rare | [R1-01](recipes/R1-01-lean-loaf.md) |
| Soft sandwich bread and rolls | 5–10 | 5–10 | 0–15 | Often all or part of the liquid | [R1-07 Milk bread](recipes/R1-07-milk-bread.md) |
| Egg bread | 8–12 (often honey) | 8–12 (usually oil) | 20–30 | Usually none | [R1-08 Challah](recipes/R1-08-challah.md) |
| Sweet dough (buns, cinnamon rolls, babka) | 10–20 | 10–20 | 10–25 | Common | variant of R1-07 |
| Brioche | 10–15 | 50–100 (butter) | 50–65 | Small amount or none | [R1-09 Brioche](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md) |

Read this table as a set of trade-offs rather than a menu. As you move down it:

- **fermentation slows** (sugar and fat), so yeast goes up and time goes up;
- **gluten development gets harder** (sugar competes for water, fat coats proteins), so mixing gets longer and more intensive;
- **the crumb gets softer, finer and more cake-like**, and the loaf keeps longer;
- **the crust browns faster** (sugar, milk, eggs), so ovens run cooler: 175–190 °C (350–375 °F) instead of 230–250 °C;
- **internal doneness drops** to about 88–93 °C, compared with ≥96 °C for lean bread, because eggs and fat set the structure at lower temperatures and you want to keep the moisture.

## Sugar: food at low levels, a brake at high levels

### Yeast food

Flour contains only a small amount of free sugar. In a lean dough, yeast depends on maltose that amylases make from damaged starch ([03.1](stage-1/module-03/lesson-01.md), [SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)). Add sucrose and the yeast's own invertase splits it into glucose and fructose within minutes of mixing, and the yeast ferments those directly. At low levels (about 1–5 %) the result is a **quick start**: more gas early, and residual sugar left for browning.

### Osmotic stress

Dissolved sugar lowers the water activity of the dough's water phase. When the solution around a yeast cell is more concentrated than the inside of the cell, water moves out of the cell by osmosis. The cell spends energy defending its internal water balance instead of fermenting. Gas production slows, and at very high sugar it can almost stop. You met the same effect for salt in M03. **Sugar and salt add up**: the yeast responds to the total dissolved load in the water it lives in, not to either ingredient alone.

It is more useful to think in terms of sugar concentration in the dough water than sugar on flour. A rough engineering number is sugar ÷ (sugar + dough water):

| Dough | Sugar % | True hydration % | Sugar in the water phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lean loaf | 0 | 70 | 0 % |
| Milk bread R1-07 | 10 | 74 | 10 ÷ 84 ≈ 12 % |
| Brioche R1-09 | 12 | 54 | 12 ÷ 66 ≈ 18 % |
| Sweet bun dough | 20 | 60 | 20 ÷ 80 = 25 % |

This explains something that puzzles many bakers: brioche, with only 12 % sugar, ferments more like a sweet dough than milk bread with 10 % sugar, because brioche has much less water for that sugar to dissolve in.

### Where the slowdown starts

Standard professional practice puts the threshold where sugar starts to cost you fermentation speed at **about 5–10 % of flour weight**. The exact point depends on hydration, salt, yeast strain and temperature. Above about 10 %, the slowdown is obvious, and at 20 % a standard instant yeast can take two to three times as long as in the same dough with little sugar. You will measure your own curve in [X1-15](experiments/X1-15-sugar-in-dough.md).

### What bakers do about it

| Sugar level | Yeast (instant, % of flour) | Yeast type |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 % | 0.7–1.2 % | Standard instant |
| 5–10 % | 1.0–1.5 % | Standard instant is fine. Osmotolerant helps at the upper end |
| 10–20 %+ | 1.5–2.5 % (less if the dough will retard overnight) | **Osmotolerant instant** |

**Osmotolerant instant yeast**, usually sold in a gold-labelled package, is a strain selected to keep fermenting in high-sugar doughs. It typically has lower invertase activity, so it does not flood its own surroundings with glucose and fructose, which are more osmotically active than sucrose because splitting one sucrose molecule doubles the number of dissolved molecules. It is the standard choice above about 10 % sugar. In lean dough it works but offers no advantage.

The other levers are the ones you already know from [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md): a warmer dough (24–27 °C for most enriched doughs), a longer time, and a sponge or preferment that lets the yeast multiply before it meets all the sugar.

### What else sugar does

Sugar is hygroscopic: it holds water. In dough, that has four visible consequences:

- **The dough feels softer and wetter** at the same water percentage, because dissolved sugar adds to the volume of the liquid phase. Sweet doughs are often formulated at a slightly lower hydration.
- **Gluten develops more slowly**, because sugar competes with the proteins for water. Very sweet doughs are sometimes mixed with part of the sugar held back and added once the gluten is formed.
- **Starch gelatinizes at a higher temperature** during baking, which keeps the crumb tender. This is the same mechanism you will study in cakes ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)).
- **The crust browns sooner and darker**, because residual sugar feeds Maillard reactions and caramelization ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md), [SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md)). A sweet dough baked at 230 °C burns long before its centre sets.

## Fat: lubricant and shortener

### What fat does to gluten

Fat coats flour proteins and gluten strands and interrupts the bonds that would link them into long, continuous sheets. Bakers call this **shortening**: the strands are literally made shorter, and the result is a softer, more tender crumb that tears easily instead of stretching. The same fat lubricates the dough, so it feels silky and extensible and the loaf can expand smoothly in the oven.

Small amounts of fat (about 2–5 % of flour) usually **increase** loaf volume slightly. The mechanism is still discussed in the cereal-science literature; the usual explanations involve fat at the surfaces of gas cells and between gluten and starch, which helps the cells expand without rupturing. Large amounts of fat reduce volume because they weaken the network more than they help it.

### Why fat goes in late

If you add fat at the start of mixing, it coats the dry proteins before they have hydrated and linked, and gluten development takes much longer. If you add it after the gluten has formed, it spreads over an existing network and lubricates it. The standard practice is therefore:

- **Up to about 5–10 % fat:** add softened butter after 3–5 min of mixing, or with the other ingredients if the dough is stiff. The penalty is small.
- **Above about 15–20 % fat:** develop the gluten first, to a good windowpane, then add the fat gradually. For brioche at 50 % butter this is not optional ([06.4](stage-1/module-06/lesson-04.md)).
- **Liquid oil in a stiff dough** (challah) is usually added with the eggs. A stiff dough takes a lot of mechanical work, which spreads the oil and develops the gluten anyway, at the cost of a longer mix.

### Solid fat versus oil

Butter is about 80–82 % fat, about 16 % water and about 1–2 % milk solids. It is plastic between roughly 13 and 18 °C, which matters for brioche, and because butterfat is partly solid at room temperature, butter breads are slightly firmer when cool and easy to shape cold. Its milk solids add a little browning. Oil is 100 % fat and liquid at room temperature: it gives a very soft, moist-feeling crumb and a slightly slick dough, and it is dairy-free, which is why challah traditionally uses it ([06.3](stage-1/module-06/lesson-03.md)).

### Fat and staling

Enriched breads stay soft longer. Fat is part of the reason: it slows moisture movement from crumb to crust and interferes with the recrystallization of starch (retrogradation) that makes crumb firm ([04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md), [SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)). The lipid chemistry is in [SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md).

## Eggs: structure, emulsion, colour and water

An egg is not one ingredient. In baker's terms it is three:

| Part | Water | Protein | Fat | Share of a large egg (~50 g without shell) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole egg | ~75 % | ~12 % | ~10 % | 100 % |
| Yolk | ~50 % | ~16 % | ~30 % (including phospholipids such as lecithin) | ~18 g |
| White | ~88 % | ~10 % | trace | ~32 g |

Eggs do four jobs in a yeast dough:

1. **Structure.** Egg proteins coagulate during baking (whites set from about 62 °C, yolks from about 65–70 °C). They reinforce the gluten network and let a soft, high-fat dough hold its shape. This is why brioche with 50 % butter still rises into a tall loaf.
2. **Emulsification and tenderness.** Yolk phospholipids, lecithin among them, sit at fat–water interfaces and help disperse fat through the dough. The crumb is finer, more even and more tender. Yolk-rich doughs are softer than doughs made with the same weight of whole egg.
3. **Colour and flavour.** Yolk carotenoids give a yellow crumb. Egg protein and the sugars in the dough brown fast, which is the colour of challah and brioche crusts, and the reason egg wash browns.
4. **Water.** Three quarters of a whole egg is water, and it counts towards hydration. Egg white in large amounts gives a dry, slightly tough crumb, which is why many egg breads add extra yolks rather than more whole eggs.

Details of egg chemistry are in [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md) and [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Raw egg and raw flour can carry pathogens (*Salmonella* in eggs; *E. coli* in flour). Do not taste raw enriched dough, wash hands and surfaces after handling raw egg, and keep dough that contains egg refrigerated if it will sit for more than a couple of hours outside a planned fermentation. See [00.3](stage-1/module-00/lesson-03.md) [FDA-eggs] [FDA-flour].

</div>

## Milk: lactose, protein and the scalding question

Whole milk is about 87–88 % water, about 3.3 % protein (about 80 % casein and 20 % whey proteins), about 4.8 % lactose and about 3.5 % fat. In dough, it does two things water does not.

### Lactose stays for the crust

Baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) has no lactase and cannot ferment lactose. Every gram of lactose in the dough is still there when the loaf goes into the oven. Lactose is a reducing sugar, and together with milk protein it drives Maillard browning. This is the reason milk breads colour faster and deeper than water breads baked the same way. Milk also gives a finer, softer crumb and slightly slower staling.

### Milk powder

Nonfat dry milk (milk powder) is about 50 % lactose and 35 % protein with 3–5 % water. Many bakers prefer it to liquid milk: you add it at 2–6 % of flour with the dry ingredients and use water as the liquid, the formula is easier to standardize, and powder keeps for months. As a rough equivalence, 100 g of whole milk contains about 9 g of nonfat milk solids plus 3.5 g of fat.

### Scalding: what the old advice is about

Old bread books tell you to scald milk (heat it to just below a simmer, about 82–85 °C, then cool it) before adding it to dough. The reason is not food safety. Cereal chemists found that **unheated milk weakens dough** and reduces loaf volume, and that heating the milk removes the effect. It has been traced to a heat-sensitive component of the whey-protein fraction. The effect is often described as similar to glutathione, the reducing agent in dead yeast and wheat germ that loosens gluten by breaking disulfide bonds. The exact mechanism is still discussed, so treat the glutathione comparison as a description of the symptom (slack, sticky dough, lower volume) rather than an established molecular explanation.

What this means for you:

- **Pasteurized milk** (typically about 72 °C for 15 s) has been heated, but only mildly, and only part of the whey protein is denatured. The effect is much smaller than with raw milk, so scalding is less critical than the old books suggest. It may not be zero.
- **UHT milk** and **high-heat milk powder** (the grade that bakery suppliers sell for bread) have been heated hard enough to remove the effect. This is why industrial bakers specify heat-treated powder.
- **Many professional bakers still scald or use powder** because it costs little and removes one variable. In this course, R1-07 gives you the choice: use pasteurized milk as it is, or scald it and cool it to below 40 °C. If you want to know whether it matters with your milk, that is a two-loaf experiment you can design yourself.

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** Scalded milk must cool before it touches the yeast. Yeast begins to die above about 50 °C and is dead by about 60 °C. Use milk at 20–40 °C, and put the temperature into your DDT calculation like any other liquid.

</div>

## True hydration in enriched doughs

In [01.2](stage-1/module-01/lesson-02.md) you met true hydration: the total water from every ingredient, divided by the total flour. In enriched doughs, the difference between "liquid %" and true hydration is large, so you need the water content of each ingredient:

| Ingredient | Water content (approximate, use for calculation) |
|---|---|
| Water | 100 % |
| Whole milk | 87 % |
| Whole egg | 75 % |
| Egg yolk | 50 % |
| Egg white | 88 % |
| Butter | 16 % |
| Honey | 17 % |
| Cream (35 % fat) | 58 % |
| Fresh yeast | 70 % |
| Milk powder, sugar, oil | ~0 % |

**Worked example: R1-07 milk bread** (300 g flour in total, including the flour cooked into the tangzhong):

| Ingredient | Grams | Water fraction | Water (g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water (in the tangzhong) | 105 | 1.00 | 105.0 |
| Whole milk | 90 | 0.87 | 78.3 |
| Whole egg | 45 | 0.75 | 33.8 |
| Butter | 30 | 0.16 | 4.8 |
| **Total water** | | | **221.9** |

True hydration = 221.9 ÷ 300 = **74 %**. The liquid ingredients (water, milk and egg) add up to 240 g, 80 % of flour, which overstates the real water by 6 points. Ignoring the milk and eggs and counting only the added water would give 35 %, which is meaningless.

The same calculation gives about **52 %** for [R1-08 challah](recipes/R1-08-challah.md) and about **54 %** for [R1-09 brioche](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md). Those numbers look stiff, but the dough will not feel stiff. Sugar, fat and egg yolk all make a dough feel softer and slacker than a lean dough with the same water. **Compare enriched doughs with each other, not with lean doughs.** A brioche at 54 % true hydration is soft and sticky; a lean dough at 54 % is almost too stiff to knead.

## What goes wrong

- **The sweet dough barely rises.** Too much sugar for the yeast type or dose, a cold dough, or salt and sugar together. See [slow or no rise in sweet dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=slow-or-no-rise-in-sweet-dough).
- **The crust is dark brown at 15 minutes and the centre is raw.** The oven is too hot for a sugar-, milk- or egg-rich dough. See [crust too dark before the inside is done](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=crust-too-dark-before-the-inside-is-done).
- **The dough never develops a windowpane.** Fat was added too early or the dough was under-mixed for its sugar level. See [dense brioche](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=dense-brioche); the same logic applies to any rich dough.
- **The crumb is dry and crumbly the next day.** Under-hydrated formula, or over-baked beyond 93 °C internal. See [dry crumb](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=dry-crumb).

## Lab notebook

For every enriched bake, write on the formula sheet: sugar %, fat % and type, egg % (whole and yolk separately), milk form, **true hydration**, sugar in the water phase, yeast type and %, dough temperature after mixing, mixing time before and after the fat went in, and time to your bulk target. After [X1-15](experiments/X1-15-sugar-in-dough.md), plot time to +75 % against sugar %. That plot, together with your fermentation curve from M03, is what you will use to set yeast levels for any new sweet dough.

## Check yourself

1. A bun dough has 18 % sugar, 1.8 % salt and 1 % standard instant yeast, and it took 4 h to rise by half. Give two changes to the yeast and one to the process.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Switch to osmotolerant instant yeast, and raise the dose to about 1.5–2 %. On the process side: ferment warmer (26–27 °C), or build a sponge with part of the flour, water and yeast and no sugar, so the yeast multiplies before it meets the sugar. Holding back part of the sugar until late in the mix also helps gluten development, though it does less for fermentation.

</details>

2. Why can milk bread with 10 % sugar ferment faster than brioche with 12 % sugar, even at the same yeast percentage?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

What the yeast feels is the sugar concentration in the dough water. Milk bread has about 74 % true hydration, so the sugar is about 10 ÷ 84 ≈ 12 % of the water phase. Brioche has about 54 %, so its 12 % sugar is about 18 % of the water phase. Brioche's 50 % butter also slows gas production and makes the dough heavier to lift.

</details>

3. A formula lists 500 g flour, 200 g whole milk, 100 g whole egg, 40 g yolks and 60 g butter. What is the true hydration?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Water = 200 × 0.87 + 100 × 0.75 + 40 × 0.50 + 60 × 0.16 = 174 + 75 + 20 + 9.6 = 278.6 g. True hydration = 278.6 ÷ 500 = 55.7 %, about 56 %.

</details>

4. Your milk bread made with raw farm milk was slack and low. The same formula with supermarket UHT milk was fine. What is the likely cause, and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Unheated milk contains a heat-sensitive whey-protein component that weakens dough and lowers volume. UHT milk has been heated enough to remove it. Scald the raw milk (about 82–85 °C), cool it below 40 °C, then use it. Or use high-heat milk powder and water.

</details>

5. Why is 50 % butter added to brioche after the gluten is developed, but 10 % oil can go into challah with the eggs?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat coats proteins and interferes with the formation of the gluten network. At 50 %, butter added early would prevent the network from forming in any reasonable mixing time. Challah is stiff, and 10 % oil only slows development, which the long, hard mixing of a stiff dough overcomes. It still takes longer than it would without the oil.

</details>
