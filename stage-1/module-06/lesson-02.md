## Why this lesson exists

Softness and slow staling are the defining quality attributes of a sandwich loaf or a dinner roll, and most of both come from water: how much the dough can carry and how much the crumb keeps. Adding more water to a dough makes it sticky and unshapeable long before the crumb gets noticeably softer. The Asian bakery technique of **pre-gelatinizing** a small part of the flour gets around this: cooked starch holds water that raw starch cannot. This lesson explains why that works, gives you the two standard methods (tangzhong and yudane), and makes it your first contact with starch gelatinization as a deliberate tool, a thread that runs through pastry cream, pie fillings and choux.

## You will be able to

1. Explain, in terms of starch granules and water, why a tangzhong makes a dough carry more water and makes the crumb softer and slower to firm.
2. Make a tangzhong (1 : 5 flour : water to 65–70 °C) and a yudane (1 : 1 flour : boiling water), and adjust a formula so that total flour and true hydration stay the same when you add one.
3. Mix, shape (four rolled pieces) and bake R1-07 to its specification: rim-height proof, 175 °C, 88–93 °C internal.
4. Measure softness and staling with a simple compression test, and separate the two causes of firming: moisture loss and starch retrogradation.

## Starch and water: the short version

You met starch in [02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md) and the oven in [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md). Here is the part you need now ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md) has the full picture).

- **Native starch granules** are semi-crystalline packages of two polymers: amylose (mostly linear) and amylopectin (highly branched). At room temperature they absorb only about 30–40 % of their weight of water, and only at the surface. In a lean dough, starch is mostly a filler sitting in the gluten network.
- **Gelatinization.** Heat starch in plenty of water and at some point, for wheat starch roughly 55–70 °C depending on how much water is available, the crystalline regions melt. The granules take up many times their weight of water and swell, amylose leaks out, and the mixture thickens into a paste. This is irreversible: the granules do not go back to their original state on cooling.
- **Retrogradation.** As gelatinized starch cools and ages, its molecules slowly re-associate into ordered, partly crystalline regions. Amylose does this within hours (which is part of why bread "sets" as it cools); amylopectin does it over days. Amylopectin retrogradation is the main molecular reason crumb gets firm as bread stales, together with water moving from crumb to crust and between starch and gluten ([04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md), [Gray 2003]).

In a normal loaf all of this happens in the oven. The dough enters the oven with native starch, which gelatinizes in the few minutes the crumb spends between about 60 and 95 °C. It is limited by the water available, which in bread is not much. A tangzhong moves part of that process **before mixing, with plenty of water**.

## Tangzhong and yudane

| | Tangzhong (water roux) | Yudane (scalded dough) |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Popularized in Chinese and Taiwanese bakeries; the name means "water roux" | Japanese; the name means roughly "hot-water dough" |
| Ratio flour : liquid | **1 : 5** (water, milk or a mix) | **1 : 1** boiling water |
| Method | Whisk cold, cook while stirring to **65–70 °C** | Pour boiling water over flour, mix to a stiff paste, cover and cool; traditionally refrigerated overnight |
| Typical share of total flour | **5–10 %** | **10–25 %** (often ~20 %) |
| Texture of the pre-ferment | Soft pudding | Stiff, sticky paste |
| Effect on bread | Very soft, moist, feathery crumb | Soft, slightly chewier, springy crumb; often described as sweeter |

Both are standard current practice rather than something defined by a single textbook. Within them, formulas vary widely.

### Why 65 °C and 1 : 5?

At five parts water to one part flour there is enough water for the starch to gelatinize extensively. Most wheat starch gelatinizes over roughly 55–70 °C in excess water ([Schirmer 2015] shows why "the" gelatinization temperature is really a range that depends on the water available and the measurement method). The paste thickens noticeably once enough granules have swollen, which is why the endpoint is both a temperature and a texture: **65 °C and the moment the spatula leaves trails**. Going a few degrees higher, to 70–75 °C, gives a thicker paste and is not a failure. Boiling it hard for a minute evaporates water, which you then have to put back, and makes a gluey lump that is harder to disperse.

With yudane the boiling water is poured over an equal weight of flour. The mix reaches perhaps 60–70 °C briefly and then cools. With so little water, gelatinization is less complete than in a tangzhong, but because the yudane uses 2–4 times more flour, the total amount of pre-gelatinized starch in the dough is similar.

### Why it makes the bread softer

Four effects stack:

1. **More water in the dough at the same stickiness.** The gelatinized starch holds its water tightly, so a dough at 74 % true hydration with a tangzhong handles about like a dough several points drier without one. You get extra water in the bread without the handling penalty.
2. **More water in the crumb after baking.** The crumb comes out of the oven moister, and the bake loss is usually slightly lower. More water plasticizes the crumb: it bends instead of snapping.
3. **Softer crumb at the same moisture.** Pre-swollen starch forms a softer, more continuous starch phase in the crumb.
4. **Slower perceived staling.** The crumb starts softer and moister, so it takes longer to firm to the point where you notice it. Whether a tangzhong also slows amylopectin retrogradation itself is less clear. Treat "stays soft longer" as the observation and "higher water content and softer starting point" as the reliable explanation.

There are side effects. Gelatinized starch is much more easily attacked by the flour's amylases, so a little more sugar is released during fermentation. This helps both the yeast and the crust colour. The loaf may be slightly smaller than a straight-dough control because part of the flour went in as a paste that contributes no gluten. At 5–10 % this is small.

### Adding a tangzhong to any formula

The rule is simple: **keep total flour and total water constant, then move part of each into the tangzhong.**

Worked example. You have a soft roll formula: 500 g flour, 330 g milk, and the rest. You want an 8 % tangzhong made with water.

- Tangzhong flour = 8 % × 500 = 40 g. Tangzhong water = 5 × 40 = 200 g.
- Water in 330 g milk = 330 × 0.87 = 287 g. After the tangzhong, the remaining liquid must carry 287 − 200 = 87 g of water, which is 87 ÷ 0.87 = 100 g of milk. (Or make the tangzhong with milk: 200 g milk in the roux, 130 g milk in the dough.)
- Main dough flour = 500 − 40 = 460 g.
- Check: total flour 500 g, total water 287 g, unchanged.

If you want the tangzhong to buy you **extra** softness, raise the true hydration by 2–5 points as well. That is how most commercial milk breads end up in the 72–78 % range. Do it only after you have made the unchanged version.

## Making R1-07

Read the full recipe, [R1-07](recipes/R1-07-milk-bread.md). The points that differ from what you know from lean bread:

### Mixing: two stages

Soft enriched doughs need **more** gluten development than lean artisan doughs, not less. The fine, even, feathery crumb you want comes from many small gas cells held by a well-developed, extensible network. Sugar and butter both slow development, so the mix is long: 12–15 min by stand mixer, 15–20 min by hand.

1. **Develop first**, without butter, to moderate development: the dough pulls away from the bowl, and a membrane stretches thin but tears raggedly.
2. **Add soft butter** (18–22 °C) a piece at a time. The dough will break apart and look ruined for a minute; that is normal. Mix on to a thin, translucent windowpane that tears with a smooth, round hole.

Watch the temperature. A stand mixer adds heat fast in a long mix. The target is 26 °C at the end.

### Fermentation and shaping

Bulk to +75–100 %, which is further than the +50–75 % you used for lean bread. For a pan loaf you want even, fine cells, and the full bulk gives flavour and extensibility that make shaping easy.

The **four-piece rolled shape** is not decoration. Each piece is rolled flat to degas it and make the cells even, folded to the pan's width, then rolled into a tight cylinder. That builds a spiral of aligned, stretched gluten. In the oven each cylinder expands upward, and when you tear the baked loaf it pulls apart along the spiral in long, soft strands. The four lobes also rise more evenly than one large piece in a narrow pan.

### Proof: judge by the pan

For pan breads, the pan is your volume gauge. For R1-07 in a 6–7 cm deep 22 × 11 cm pan, proof until the highest point is **about 1 cm below the rim**. Oven spring will take it 3–5 cm above the rim. Record the pan depth and the height at which you baked, and adjust next time: if the loaf tore at the sides, proof longer; if it sagged or the lobes wrinkled, shorter.

### Baking: colour control and internal temperature

| Parameter | Lean loaf R1-01 | Milk bread R1-07 | Why different |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oven | 230–250 °C (445–480 °F) | 175 °C (350 °F) | Sugar, lactose and egg brown fast |
| Steam | Yes | No; egg wash instead | A soft, thin crust is wanted |
| Time | 40–45 min | 30–35 min | Smaller, lower temperature |
| Internal endpoint | ≥96 °C | **88–93 °C** | Eggs and fat set the structure earlier; a higher endpoint dries the crumb |
| Demould | — | Within 5 min | Otherwise steam condenses on the sides and softens them |

The internal temperature range deserves a comment. Enriched breads set at lower temperatures than lean breads, because egg proteins coagulate and the crumb is shortened by fat. Most professional texts pull them in the high 80s to low 90s °C. The top of the range suits the loaf; the lower end suits small rolls, which have a larger crust fraction and lose moisture fast. If you are unsure, take 90 °C as the target and cross-check with the side colour when you tip the loaf out.

### Egg wash

A thin, even coat of beaten egg (loosened with a little milk or water and a pinch of salt, which breaks down the egg proteins so the wash flows) gives the glossy, deep golden top. Strain it. Apply it with a soft brush in light strokes, and never let it pool in the joins between lobes: pooled wash glues the pieces together and bakes into dark streaks. Challah in [06.3](stage-1/module-06/lesson-03.md) takes egg wash further.

## Measuring softness and staling

You cannot improve what you only describe as "softer". [X1-16](experiments/X1-16-tangzhong.md) gives you a simple, repeatable measurement:

1. Cut a **2.0 cm** slice from the centre of the loaf with a bread knife and a guide.
2. Lay a flat lid of known diameter (about 6 cm) on the centre of the crumb. Measure the slice height.
3. Add a fixed load, for example 200 g of coins in a small cup, for **30 s**. Measure the height under load.
4. Compression % = (height before − height under load) ÷ height before × 100. Remove the load and measure again after 30 s to get **recovery**.

A fresh, soft crumb compresses a lot and recovers almost fully. A stale crumb compresses less (firm) and recovers less (it has lost its elasticity). Weighing the loaf daily separates the two causes of firming. If a loaf lost almost no weight in a sealed bag but still firmed, that firming came mainly from starch retrogradation and moisture redistribution inside the loaf rather than from drying. This is the same distinction as in [X1-12](experiments/X1-12-staling.md), now used to compare formulas.

## What goes wrong

- **The sides of the loaf cave in after baking.** Under-baked (below 88 °C), over-proofed, or left in the pan. See [loaf collapses or caves in](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=loaf-collapses-or-caves-in).
- **Ragged tears where the lobes meet the pan wall.** Under-proofed, or the cylinders were rolled too tight. See [torn or burst sides](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=torn-or-burst-sides).
- **Dark top, pale sides, raw centre.** The oven is too hot or the rack too high. See [crust too dark before the inside is done](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=crust-too-dark-before-the-inside-is-done).
- **Crumb dry and firm by day 2.** Tangzhong too small or lumpy, hydration too low, over-baked, or stored unsealed or in the fridge. See [dry crumb](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=dry-crumb).

## Check yourself

1. You want to add a 6 % tangzhong to a formula with 400 g flour and 260 g water. Give the tangzhong and main-dough amounts.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Tangzhong flour = 0.06 × 400 = 24 g; tangzhong water = 5 × 24 = 120 g. Main dough: 400 − 24 = 376 g flour and 260 − 120 = 140 g water. Total flour and water are unchanged.

</details>

2. Your tangzhong weighed 108 g after cooling instead of the 126 g you put in the pan. What happened, and does it matter?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

18 g of water evaporated during cooking and cooling. On a 300 g flour loaf, that is 6 points of hydration (74 % → 68 %), which is enough to make the crumb noticeably less soft. Add 18 g of water back to the tangzhong, or to the main dough liquid.

</details>

3. A friend claims the tangzhong "stops staling". Using your understanding of starch, correct the claim.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Staling still happens: amylopectin retrogrades and water redistributes over days whether or not you use a tangzhong. The tangzhong loaf starts with more water in the crumb and a softer starch phase, so it begins softer and takes longer to reach the firmness at which you call it stale. X1-16 should show both loaves firming, with the tangzhong loaf consistently softer at each time point.

</details>

4. Your milk bread came out with a deep brown top after 20 min and read 84 °C inside. Give two changes for next time and one thing to do right now.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Now: tent the top with foil and continue baking until it reads 88–93 °C. Next time: bake at 170–175 °C (check the oven with a thermometer) and move the rack lower. Also check that the sugar and egg wash are not excessive (a thin coat, no pooling).

</details>

5. Why is the internal endpoint for milk bread (88–93 °C) lower than for a lean loaf (≥96 °C)?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Egg proteins coagulate and fat shortens the crumb, so the structure is set at a lower temperature. The goal is a soft, moist crumb. Taking an enriched crumb to 96–98 °C dries it and gives a crumbly texture. Lean bread needs the higher temperature to fully gelatinize its starch in a dough with no other setting agents and to drive off enough water to avoid gumminess.

</details>
