## Why this lesson exists

Brioche asks a gluten network to hold its own weight in butter, or more, and still rise into a tall, light loaf. It is where the rules from [06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md) stop being guidelines. If you add the fat before the gluten is developed, the dough never develops. If you let the dough warm by a few degrees, the butter melts out and the dough breaks. If you proof it warm, the butter leaks. Brioche is also the first dough in the course where **fat temperature and plasticity** control the process, the idea that later governs lamination, pie dough and chocolate. Mastering it gives you a model you will reuse for every high-fat product.

## You will be able to

1. Describe brioche as a developed gluten network carrying a plastic-fat dispersion, and explain what happens physically when the dough "breaks".
2. Plan the temperatures of a brioche mix (ingredients, base dough, butter, final dough) and act when a reading goes out of range.
3. Incorporate 50 % butter in stages, by stand mixer or by hand, to a smooth, glossy, extensible dough with a thin windowpane.
4. Retard, shape (Nanterre loaf and à tête) and proof brioche so that the butter stays in the dough, and bake it to specification.

## The brioche family

| Brioche | Butter (% of flour) | Eggs | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lean brioche / brioche-style buns | 20–30 | 20–40 % + milk | Bread-like, soft, easy to handle; burger buns, some sandwich loaves |
| **Classic brioche (R1-09)** | **40–60** | **50–60 %**, little or no other liquid | Tender, fine, rich, pulls into strands |
| Rich brioche | 70–100+ | 50–65 % | Cake-like, melts in the mouth; needs excellent development and strict temperature control |

These are practical ranges. French trade names for these categories vary between sources, so this course uses butter percentage, which you can measure. The principle across the family is the same: **the more butter, the more development the base needs before the butter goes in, and the tighter the temperature control.** [X1-17](experiments/X1-17-brioche-butter.md) shows you the family in one session.

## The model: a network carrying a plastic fat

### Butter is a composite, and its state depends on temperature

Butter is about 80–82 % fat, 16 % water and 1–2 % milk solids. It is a water-in-fat emulsion: tiny water droplets and fat globules in a continuous fat phase. Butterfat is a mixture of many triglycerides with different melting points, so butter does not melt at one temperature. At any temperature, part of it is solid fat crystals and part is liquid oil ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)).

| Butter temperature | State | Behaviour in dough |
|---|---|---|
| Below ~10 °C | Hard, brittle | Breaks into lumps; won't disperse; leaves butter streaks |
| **~13–16 °C** | **Plastic**: firm but bends, dents easily, not shiny | Smears into thin films and small particles between gluten strands |
| ~18–22 °C | Soft | Fine for low-fat doughs (milk bread); smears too readily in brioche and warms the dough |
| Above ~26–28 °C | Oily, liquid fat separating | Coats everything, leaks out; dough turns greasy and breaks |

**Plasticity** is the key property. A plastic fat behaves like a solid under small stresses and flows under larger ones, like modelling clay. At 13–16 °C, the mixer's shear spreads the butter into very thin layers and small particles distributed through the dough. At that point the dough holds it as a stable dispersion, helped by egg yolk lecithin and proteins at the fat–water interfaces. When the butter is too warm, a large share of its fat is liquid oil. The oil cannot be held as particles; it coats the surfaces and seeps out. What you see is a dough that is shiny, wet-looking, slack and curdled, which bakers call **broken** or **greasy**.

### The gluten network must come first

Fat coats proteins and blocks the bonds that form gluten. In a dough at 50 % butter, if the fat goes in first, the gluten never forms properly in any reasonable mixing time. So brioche is mixed in two stages:

1. **Base dough** (flour, eggs, milk, sugar, salt, yeast): about 46 % true hydration from eggs and milk. It is stiff and sticky, and it needs **10–15 min of intensive mixing** to reach a good windowpane. You will hear the transition: the dough stops smearing on the bowl walls and begins slapping around the hook.
2. **Butter stage:** plastic butter in 4–5 additions. Each addition temporarily breaks the dough apart, because the butter lubricates the network so well that the dough loses its grip on the hook and the bowl. The mixer keeps working, and the dough re-forms around the butter. After the last addition, 2–4 min more mixing gives a very thin, even windowpane.

A developed base dough is also why brioche gets taller with good technique. The network is what holds the gas; the butter only tenderizes it. An under-mixed base gives a dense, cakey brioche with a close, heavy crumb, whatever you do later.

## Temperature control: the whole game

### The heat budget

A stand mixer converts a lot of mechanical energy into heat in a long, intensive mix. Brioche runs 20–30 min in total, and the dough can gain **10–15 °C** over that time. You manage this like any other heat budget:

| Point | Target | How to control |
|---|---|---|
| Eggs and milk | 4–5 °C (from the fridge) | Cold liquid absorbs the heat of mixing |
| Flour | Room temperature, or chilled if the kitchen is above 24 °C | 30 min in the fridge |
| Base dough before butter | **≤24 °C** | Chill the bowl 10–15 min if above |
| Butter | **13–16 °C**, plastic | Beat between parchment; check with a probe |
| Final dough | **24 °C** (max 26 °C) | Above this, stop and chill, and shorten the bulk |
| Proof | **24–27 °C**, never above 27 °C | Proofing spot with a thermometer |

Work backwards from 24 °C: a 4 °C liquid, 20 °C flour and a friction rise of 10–15 °C give a base dough around 18–22 °C. That leaves a margin for the butter stage, which adds a few more degrees. If your mixer is small or slow, the mix takes longer and the dough warms more, so measure. Your first brioche is a data point for your friction factor on this dough.

<div class="callout pro">

**Professional practice:** Professional bakeries mix brioche in spiral mixers, which develop dough faster and with less heat than a planetary stand mixer. They often chill the base dough or use a proportion of ice or very cold egg. Many also retard the base dough before adding the butter in large batches. The principle is the same as yours: keep the fat plastic and the gluten developed.

</div>

### When the dough breaks

If the dough turns shiny and wet and separates into slippery lumps that won't re-form, the butter has melted out. Stop mixing. Check the temperature. If it is above 26 °C, put the whole bowl in the fridge for 15–20 min, then mix again on low for 1–2 min. Often the dough re-forms as the butter partly re-crystallizes. If it doesn't, or if it was never developed, the batch will bake into a dense, greasy brioche. Use it for bread pudding and restart with colder ingredients. [Greasy, broken brioche dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=greasy-broken-brioche-dough) has the full diagnosis.

### Stand mixer or hand?

A stand mixer is **strongly recommended**. It does 20–30 min of intensive work without warming the dough through your hands. The hand method in [R1-09](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md) uses the **rubaud** technique (fast scooping in the bowl) and slap-and-fold. It works, but it takes 40–50 min of hard, sticky work, and your hands warm the dough, so work in a cool room with cold ingredients. Make your first hand brioche at 30–35 % butter.

## Fermentation: slow on purpose

Brioche ferments slowly. It has 12 % sugar in only 54 % water (about 18 % sugar in the water phase), 50 % butter, and a heavy dough to lift. The standard schedule uses this:

1. **1 h at 22–24 °C** after mixing: fermentation starts and the dough rises about 30–50 %.
2. **Degas, flatten to 2–3 cm and retard overnight at 3–5 °C (8–16 h).** The flat shape cools through quickly, so the centre doesn't keep fermenting warm. The butter re-solidifies, and the dough goes from sticky and soft to firm and pliable, easy to divide and shape. Slow fermentation builds flavour.
3. **Shape cold (4–8 °C),** quickly, with a little flour.
4. **Proof at 24–27 °C for 2–3 h.** The first hour mostly warms the dough. The endpoint is 2–2.5× the shaped volume, a light, wobbly feel, and a very slow spring-back.

The **27 °C ceiling** matters. A home "proofing" setting of 30–35 °C, fine for lean dough, melts butter out of a brioche: grease pools under the moulds, the dough weakens, and the crumb bakes coarse and greasy.

Use osmotolerant (gold) instant yeast at about 1.5 %. With standard instant yeast, expect a much slower proof. The overnight retard gives the yeast time either way.

## Shaping

### Brioche à tête

The classic individual brioche has a head (*tête*) sitting on a fluted body. The head must be centred and deep, or it falls sideways in the oven.

1. Round a 55 g piece into a tight ball.
2. Lay the side of your hand on it about a quarter of the way along. Roll back and forth, pressing down, until a small head is joined to the body by a thin neck about 5 mm across. Don't cut through.
3. Hold the head, lower the body into a buttered mould, and push two or three fingertips down around the head to the bottom of the mould. The head should sit in a deep well.

The two-piece alternative (45 g body with a deep hole, and a 10 g teardrop sitting pointed end down in it) is easier and almost as good. A shallow well is the most common reason the head tilts or pops off.

### Brioche Nanterre

Six 75 g balls, rounded tight, in two rows of three in the 22 × 11 cm pan. Each ball rises into a lobe, and the loaf tears apart along the joins. Tight rounding matters more than anything else: loose balls merge and give a flat, uneven top.

### Egg wash

Wash twice, as with challah: once after shaping (gently), and once just before baking (very gently, because brioche at full proof is fragile). Don't let the wash pool at the base of the head or between the balls; pooled wash glues pieces and bakes into dark patches.

## Baking

| Product | Oven | Time | Internal | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| À tête, 55 g | 190 °C (375 °F) | 14–18 min | 88–93 °C | Uniform deep golden; unmould after 5 min |
| Nanterre, 450 g | 180 °C (355 °F) | 30–35 min | 88–93 °C | Tent at deep golden; check the sides |

Colour arrives fast: egg wash, sugar and butter milk solids all brown readily. The loaf is the harder product, because its centre needs time while its top wants to burn; tent it. Brioche is fragile warm, so let it firm for 5–10 min before unmoulding.

## What goes wrong

- **Dough turns shiny, wet and curdled during the butter stage.** Too warm, butter too soft, or butter added too fast. See [greasy, broken brioche dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=greasy-broken-brioche-dough).
- **Heavy, tight, cake-like crumb.** Base dough under-developed before the butter, under-proofed, or proofed too warm so the butter leaked. See [dense brioche](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=dense-brioche).
- **Proof takes hours longer than expected.** Standard yeast in high sugar, cold dough, or the fridge ran cold. See [slow or no rise in sweet dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=slow-or-no-rise-in-sweet-dough).
- **Loaf sinks, heads tilt or fall.** Over-proofed, under-baked, shallow wells. See [loaf collapses or caves in](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=loaf-collapses-or-caves-in) and [torn or burst sides](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=torn-or-burst-sides).

## Check yourself

1. Your base dough reads 25 °C and your butter is at 19 °C. What do you do before adding the butter?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Chill the covered bowl of base dough for 10–15 min to bring it to 24 °C or below, and chill the butter until it reads 13–16 °C: firm, pliable and not shiny. Adding 19 °C butter to a 25 °C dough risks a final dough above 26 °C and a greasy, broken mix.

</details>

2. Explain in one paragraph why brioche dough breaks when it gets too warm, using the word "plastic".

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

At 13–16 °C butter is plastic: a network of solid fat crystals holding liquid oil, which the mixer can smear into thin films and small particles dispersed between gluten strands, where the dough holds them. As the dough warms above about 26 °C, more of the butterfat melts. Liquid oil can't be held as particles, so it coats the dough surfaces and separates out. The dough goes slippery, shiny and curdled, and loses the cohesion and gas-holding strength of the gluten network.

</details>

3. A learner adds all the butter to the flour and eggs at the start "to save time" and mixes for 25 min. Predict the result.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Fat coats the proteins before they hydrate and link, so the gluten network barely develops. The dough will stay soft, greasy and cake-batter-like, will not windowpane, and will bake into a low, dense, cakey brioche with a close crumb. The long mix will also warm it and may melt the butter out.

</details>

4. Why is brioche retarded overnight as a flat 2–3 cm slab rather than in a ball in a bowl?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Dough conducts heat poorly. A thick ball takes hours to chill through, and its warm centre keeps fermenting and softening while the outside is cold, so the dough is uneven. A thin slab reaches 3–5 °C quickly and evenly, the butter firms throughout, fermentation slows uniformly, and the dough is ready to shape the next morning.

</details>

5. Your oven has a "proof" setting at 35 °C. Can you use it for brioche?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

No. At 35 °C much of the butterfat is liquid, and it seeps out of the dough and pools under the moulds. The dough weakens, and the crumb bakes greasy and coarse. Proof at 24–27 °C, for example in the oven with only the light on and the door ajar, and check with a thermometer.

</details>
