## Why this lesson exists

Challah is the first product in the course where the **shape is the product**. A braid can only hold if the dough is formulated for it: stiff enough that a rolled strand keeps its round cross-section, strong enough that the joins don't tear during oven spring, and proofed to a narrow window. It is also the first bread whose colour you manage deliberately, because an egg-, honey- and oil-rich crust browns far faster than a lean crust. This lesson teaches the formulation logic of an egg bread, a precise braiding notation you can follow and write down, and how to control shine and colour with egg wash and the oven.

## You will be able to

1. Explain how eggs and oil change the structure, crumb and handling of a yeast dough, and why challah is formulated at about 50–55 % true hydration.
2. Roll equal strands to a specified length and braid 3-, 4- and 6-strand loaves from written notation.
3. Judge final proof on a braided loaf and explain why under-proof tears the joins.
4. Apply a double egg wash that bakes glossy and even, and control crust colour with oven temperature, tray insulation and tenting.

## The egg bread as a system

### What the formula looks like

| Component | Challah range (baker's %) | R1-08 | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole egg + yolks | 20–30 | 20 + 8 = 28 | Structure (coagulating protein), emulsifier, colour, flavour, water |
| Oil (or butter) | 8–12 | 10 | Tenderizes, lubricates, softens crumb, keeps it moist |
| Sugar and/or honey | 8–12 | 5 + 5 = 10 | Sweetness, yeast food, browning, softness |
| True hydration | 50–56 | 52 | Stiff enough to braid |
| Salt | 1.6–2.0 | 1.8 | Flavour, gluten strength, fermentation control |
| Instant yeast | 1.2–2.0 | 1.4 | Sweet-dough level; osmotolerant preferred |

Challah is a religious and cultural bread. The Ashkenazi Sabbath and festival loaf is dairy-free (pareve) so it can be eaten with meat meals, which is why it uses oil rather than butter. Recipes range from lean and water-based to very rich and yolk-heavy. R1-08 sits in the middle.

### Eggs as structure

Eggs contribute about 12 % protein by weight, mostly in the white (ovalbumin and others) and the yolk (lipoproteins). During baking they denature and coagulate. White proteins begin to set at about 62 °C and yolk proteins at about 65–70 °C ([SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md), [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md)). This coagulated protein works alongside the gluten network. That is why:

- a challah can carry 10 % oil and 10 % sweeteners and still rise tall and hold a braid;
- the crumb sets at a lower internal temperature, 88–93 °C, than a lean loaf;
- the crumb is **close and even** rather than open: egg-set walls are thin, short and fine.

Too much **white** makes the crumb dry and slightly rubbery, because white is mostly water and protein that sets firm. Extra **yolk** does the opposite: its fat and lecithin tenderize and emulsify, so yolk-rich challah is softer, yellower and richer. That is why R1-08 uses yolks for 8 of its 28 egg points.

### Why stiff?

Think of a strand as a beam lying on the tray. It has to support its own weight for an hour of proof and then the expansion of oven spring, without slumping into its neighbour. Stiffness (high viscosity and elasticity, [SC-12](stage-1/science/sc-12-rheology.md)) comes from low water and a well-developed network. At 52 % true hydration with good development, a 150 g strand keeps its cross-section. At 58–60 % it flattens and the braid blurs into a blob. Egg yolk, oil and sugar all soften the dough, so an egg bread at 52 % feels like a lean dough at about 60 %.

The consequence for mixing: a stiff dough needs a long, hard mix. Budget 6–8 min on speed 2 in a stand mixer, or 10–12 min of firm hand kneading, to a membrane you can see light through. Under-developed challah tears at the joins and gives a dense, cakey crumb.

### Oil in a stiff dough

In [06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md) you learned to add fat after the gluten forms. Challah is the standard exception. With 10 % oil in a stiff dough, you can add the oil with the liquids. The mixing work is high enough to develop the network anyway, and oil disperses more evenly when it goes in with the eggs. Development will still take a couple of minutes longer than for the same dough without oil. If you replace the oil with butter, add it softened after 3–4 min of mixing.

## Braiding: a notation you can write down

### The convention

Professional bread texts use a **positional** notation:

- Number the **positions** 1 to *n* from left to right, not the strands. After every move, re-number.
- "*a* over *b*" means: take the strand now at position *a*, lift it over the strands between, and put it down so that it becomes position *b*. The strands it passed over each shift one position.

This makes braids reproducible and debuggable: if a loaf comes out lopsided, you can check your sequence as you would check code.

### The three standard braids

| Braid | Sequence | Strand weight and length (R1-08) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **3-strand** | 1 over 2, 3 over 2; repeat | 150 g, 30–35 cm | Flat, familiar plait. Braid from the middle out to each end for symmetry |
| **4-strand** | 4 over 2, 1 over 3, 2 over 3; repeat | 225 g, 38–42 cm | Taller, rounder, interlocking chain |
| **6-strand** | Once: 6 over 1. Then repeat: 2 over 6, 1 over 3, 5 over 1, 6 over 4 | 150 g, 40–45 cm | Tall, full, classic festival loaf |

For the 6-strand braid, run the four-move cycle aloud as you work. The pattern is: an outside strand goes to the far outside, and then the new outside strand is brought into the middle. After two or three cycles your hands learn it. Practise with six ropes of spare dough, or with six strips of cloth, before you braid real challah.

### Rolling strands

1. **Divide by weight** (±2 g). Equal strands give a straight loaf; a 10 g difference shows as a lean to one side.
2. **Pre-shape into logs and rest 15–20 min**, covered. This is not optional. Gluten that has just been shaped springs back (elastic recovery), and rested gluten extends.
3. **Roll on an unfloured bench** from the centre outward with flat palms. Friction grips the dough and lets you lengthen it. Flour on the bench makes strands slide, fold and trap flour seams that open in the oven.
4. **Taper the ends**, leaving the middle slightly thicker. The braid then has a raised centre and neat ends.
5. **Measure.** Use a ruler on the bench. Strands of equal length braid evenly; if one is short, the loaf curls.

If a strand keeps shrinking back, leave it and roll the next one, then come back to it. Forcing an unrested strand tears its surface. You will see those tears as rough, dull patches on the baked crust.

### Braid tension

Lay each strand snugly against its neighbour without stretching it. Stretched strands are under tension, and in the oven that tension is released at the weakest point: the joins tear open and show pale, unbaked-looking dough. A loose braid has the opposite fault. The strands separate as they proof and the loaf spreads. Tuck both ends under and press them gently so they don't unravel.

## Proofing a braid

The proof window for a braided loaf is narrower than for a pan loaf, because the joins are the weak points:

| Proof | What you see | What happens in the oven |
|---|---|---|
| Under (+30–50 %) | Strands still distinct, deep gaps, dough firm | Large oven spring rips the joins; pale cracks; tight, dense crumb |
| **Right (~+75 %)** | Strands swollen, gaps closing but visible, slow spring-back | Even expansion; clean, defined strands |
| Over (+100 % or more) | Gaps gone, loaf soft and wide, poke dent stays | Little spring; flattened, blurred braid; possible collapse; paler crust |

Egg bread proofs slower than lean dough at the same temperature, because of the sugar and the stiffness. At 25–26 °C, expect 45–75 min. Don't proof above about 30 °C: the outside proofs ahead of the centre, and the egg-washed surface can skin.

## Colour and shine: the egg wash and the oven

### Double egg wash

| Coat | When | Why |
|---|---|---|
| First | Immediately after braiding | Seals the surface so it doesn't dry during proof; dries into a thin continuous protein film |
| Second | Just before baking | Adds gloss and depth of colour; covers the stretched surface that proof exposed between strands |

The wash itself: whole egg for gloss and colour, plus an extra yolk for depth, a pinch of salt to loosen the egg proteins so the wash flows, and a pass through a fine sieve to remove the chalazae and clumps of white that cause blotches. Use a soft brush, light strokes, and a thin coat. Get into the joins, but never let the wash pool: pooled wash runs down the sides and bakes into dark tide-marks and streaks.

Variations bakers use: yolk and cream for a very dark, satiny finish (not pareve); whole egg and water for a lighter, more golden colour; a little honey or sugar in the wash for a lacquer (burns easily).

### Why challah browns fast

The surface carries egg protein and fat, sucrose, honey's glucose and fructose, and, in the dough, residual sugar that the yeast didn't use. All of these feed the Maillard reaction, and fructose also caramelizes at lower temperatures than sucrose ([SC-07](stage-1/science/sc-07-browning.md), [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)). A challah at 230 °C is black-brown in 15 minutes and raw inside. So:

| Control | Setting | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Oven temperature | 180 °C (355 °F) for a 900 g loaf; 185–190 °C (365–375 °F) for smaller loaves | Surface and centre finish at the same time |
| Tray | Two sheets stacked | Insulates the base, which otherwise scorches before the centre is done |
| Rack | Centre | Away from the top element and the floor |
| Tent | Loose foil once the top is deep golden, often at 15–20 min | Stops browning; the loaf keeps baking |
| Rotation | Halfway, if your oven map ([00.2](stage-1/module-00/lesson-02.md)) shows a hot side | Even colour |
| Endpoint | Internal 88–93 °C, joins coloured | Colour alone misleads on an egg bread |

Pale lines in the joins with a dark top usually mean the loaf was under-proofed (joins opened late in the bake and exposed fresh dough), or the second coat of wash missed the joins.

## What goes wrong

- **Joins tear open, showing pale dough.** Under-proofed, braided too tight, strands not rested, or under-developed dough. See [braids splitting or tearing](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=braids-splitting-or-tearing).
- **Streaks, drips and blotches on the crust.** Wash unstrained, too thick, pooled, or applied to a wet surface. See [egg wash streaks or blotches](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=egg-wash-streaks-or-blotches).
- **Dark top, pale sides or pale joins.** Oven too hot, rack too high, wash missed areas, or under-proof. See [uneven colour on challah](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=uneven-colour-on-challah).
- **Braid blurred into a flat loaf.** Dough too soft, or over-proofed. See [loaf collapses or caves in](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=loaf-collapses-or-caves-in).

## Check yourself

1. You have 916 g of dough and want a four-strand loaf. What strand weight and length do you use?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

916 ÷ 4 = 229 g; allowing for a little loss, use 4 × 225 g. Roll to about 38–42 cm. Longer, thinner strands give a longer, lower loaf; shorter, thicker strands give a taller one.

</details>

2. A friend adds 50 g more water to R1-08 "to make it softer". Predict the effect on the braid and on the crumb.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

True hydration rises from 52 % to 62 %. The dough will be much softer and stickier, the strands will flatten during proof, and the braid will blur and spread. The crumb will be softer and more open, but the loaf will be lower and wider. To soften the crumb while keeping the braid, add yolk or a little more oil, or use a small tangzhong, rather than water.

</details>

3. Your challah came out with deep pale cracks in the joins on one side. The rest of the loaf looks good. What happened?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The loaf was under-proofed, and the joins opened during oven spring, exposing unwashed, un-browned dough. Tearing on one side only suggests that side was braided tighter, or it faced the hotter side of the oven, which set its crust earlier. Proof to about +75 % with gaps closing, braid without stretching, and rotate the tray if your oven is uneven.

</details>

4. Why does a yolk-enriched challah have a softer crumb than one made with the same weight of whole egg?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Yolk is about 30 % fat, including phospholipids such as lecithin. The fat shortens and lubricates the gluten, and the phospholipids emulsify fat through the dough, giving a finer, more tender crumb. Whole egg has more white, which is mostly water and protein that sets firm, so it contributes more structure and a drier, slightly firmer crumb.

</details>

5. Write the sequence for a six-strand braid and explain what "2 over 6" means physically.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Once: 6 over 1. Then repeat: 2 over 6, 1 over 3, 5 over 1, 6 over 4. "2 over 6" means: pick up the strand currently in the second position from the left, lift it over the strands to its right, and lay it down at the far right, so it becomes position 6. Then re-number the positions from left to right before the next move.

</details>
