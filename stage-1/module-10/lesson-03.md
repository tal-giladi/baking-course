## Why this lesson exists

In creamed cakes, fat holds the air. In foam cakes, egg protein holds it, and there is so much air that the batter weighs half as much per litre as a pound-cake batter. That air is fragile: every stroke of folding, every minute of waiting, every drop of melted butter destroys some of it. Foam cakes are the base of most of classical pastry (layer cakes, rolled cakes, charlottes, entremets), so this lesson gives you three foam systems, the reason each is built the way it is, and a number (batter specific gravity) that tells you how much foam survived your folding.

## You will be able to

- Whip a warm whole-egg foam to the ribbon stage and explain why warming helps.
- Fold flour and fat into a foam with a measured loss of air, checked by specific gravity.
- Make a separated sponge (biscuit cuillère) and explain what separating the eggs changes.
- Build a chiffon cake from an oil-and-yolk batter and a meringue, and explain why it bakes in an ungreased tube pan and cools upside down.
- Choose among génoise, biscuit and chiffon for a given texture and use.

## Three foam systems

| | Génoise | Biscuit (separated sponge) | Chiffon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foam | Whole eggs + sugar, warmed, whipped | Yolks + sugar whipped; whites + sugar whipped to meringue; combined | Meringue (whites + sugar) folded into a fluid batter |
| Fat | Little melted butter (0–25 % of flour), folded in last | None (classic) | Oil, 40–60 % of flour, in the base batter |
| Chemical leavening | None | None | Baking powder, ~2–4 % of flour |
| Crumb | Fine, even, slightly dry, springy | Light, dry, slightly crisp outside; pipeable | Very light, tall, moist, soft, stays soft cold |
| Main uses | Layer cakes, soaked with syrup | Ladyfingers, charlotte linings, rolled cakes | Standalone cakes; layers for chilled cakes |
| Batter SG (indicative) | ~0.4–0.5 | ~0.3–0.45 (piping paste) | ~0.45–0.55 |

In all three, the structure builders are egg protein and flour starch, and the setting timing from [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md) still applies. The difference is that the air is protein-stabilized ([09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md)): an egg foam is a network of partly unfolded proteins at the air-water interface, thickened by dissolved sugar, and it is destroyed by fat, by over-whipping and by rough handling.

## Génoise: the warm whole-egg foam

Whole egg foams less readily than white alone because the yolk fat and the lower protein concentration make weaker films. Two things fix that: **sugar** (which thickens the water phase and stabilizes the films) and **warming**.

### Why warm the eggs

Warming whole eggs and sugar to **40–45 °C** over a bain-marie (a pan of barely simmering water), whisking constantly:
- dissolves the sugar completely, so the syrup phase is uniform and there are no sugar crystals piercing the bubbles;
- lowers the viscosity, so the eggs take in air faster and reach a greater volume;
- partially unfolds some proteins, helping them form films.

Above ~50 °C you start cooking the egg (lumps of coagulated yolk). Whisk constantly and check with a probe; remove from the heat at 40–45 °C. The mixture feels just warmer than your finger.

### The ribbon stage

Whip at high speed (stand mixer with whisk) for **5–8 min** (hand mixer: 10–12 min) until the foam is about **three to four times** its original volume, pale ivory, and has cooled to around body temperature. Then whip at medium for **1–2 min**: this refines the bubbles (breaks the largest into smaller ones) and makes the foam more stable.

**Ribbon endpoint:** lift the whisk; the foam falls in a thick ribbon that sits on the surface for **3–5 s** before sinking in. You can "write" a figure 8 and see it briefly. A foam that disappears at once is under-whipped; the cake will be dense with a heavy layer at the bottom. Indicatively, the foam's SG at this point is about 0.25–0.35.

### Folding the flour

Sift the flour (and any cornstarch) over the foam in **three additions**, folding each in with a large spatula or balloon whisk before the flour has time to sink.

**Folding technique:**
1. Cut down through the centre to the bottom of the bowl.
2. Sweep along the bottom and up the side towards you.
3. Lift and turn the batter over on top.
4. Rotate the bowl a quarter turn with your other hand. Repeat.

Use about 10–15 strokes per addition; stop as soon as no dry flour is visible, then check the bottom of the bowl for a flour pocket. Every extra stroke costs bubbles. This is where the specific gravity cup earns its place: an SG after flour of around 0.4–0.5 means you folded well; above ~0.55, you lost a lot of foam.

### Adding butter

Melted butter is the most destructive ingredient you can add to an egg foam: fat displaces the proteins from the bubble films and bursts them, and because it is dense, it sinks to the bottom, forming a greasy, rubbery layer.

The fix is to **temper** it: melt the butter to about **40–50 °C** (warm, not hot), stir about one-tenth of the batter (≈50 g) into it until homogeneous, then fold that lightened mixture back into the main batter in 5–8 strokes. The butter now has a density and texture close to the batter and disperses instead of sinking.

The butter adds tenderness, flavour and keeping quality; a génoise without butter is lighter and drier (génoise ordinaire vs génoise au beurre), and one with more than about 25 % butter on flour weight becomes hard to fold without losing volume.

### Baking

Pan in the oven within **2–3 min** of the last fold: the foam starts to drain and coarsen as it stands. Bake at **170–180 °C (340–355 °F)** until the top is golden, springs back when pressed lightly in the centre, and the edges pull away from the pan: about **22–28 min** for a 20 cm round. Unmould after 5 min and cool on a rack. [R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md) has the full procedure.

## Biscuit: the separated sponge

In a biscuit (biscuit à la cuillère, the ladyfinger batter), the eggs are separated and each part is whipped with its best partner:

- **Yolks + sugar**, whipped until pale and thick (the yolk foam is weak but the yolk emulsifiers make it creamy);
- **Whites + sugar**, whipped to a firm, glossy **meringue** ([09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md), [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md)): a white foam is far more stable than a whole-egg foam, so the batter can be **piped** and holds its shape.

A typical professional proportion is about **2 : 1 : 1** by weight of eggs : sugar : flour (for example 4 eggs ≈ 200 g, 100 g sugar, 100 g flour or flour plus some cornstarch), which is an educational starting point, not a single source's recipe.

**Procedure outline (makes about 30 ladyfingers or a 30 × 40 cm sheet):**
1. Whip 4 yolks (~75 g) with 35 g sugar to a pale, thick ribbon, 3–4 min.
2. Whip 4 whites (~125 g) to soft peaks, then add 65 g sugar gradually and whip to firm, glossy peaks that bend slightly at the tip.
3. Fold the yolk mixture into the meringue in 2 parts (5–8 strokes).
4. Sift 100 g flour (or 80 g flour + 20 g cornstarch) over it in 2 parts and fold until just combined. The paste should be thick enough to hold a piped line; SG indicatively 0.3–0.45.
5. Pipe with a 12–14 mm plain tube on parchment into 9–10 cm fingers, 2 cm apart. Dust twice with icing sugar, 5 min apart (the sugar "pearls" on the surface).
6. Bake at **190–200 °C (375–390 °F)** for **8–10 min** until pale golden and firm to touch; a sheet takes 8–12 min. Cool on the paper.

Biscuit has no fat, so it is dry and absorbent. That is its purpose: it takes syrup or cream (tiramisu, charlotte) without falling apart.

## Chiffon: oil, chemical leavening and a meringue

Chiffon is a hybrid. The base is a fluid batter of flour, sugar, baking powder, salt, **oil**, **yolks** and water (or juice); a meringue is folded into it. Because oil stays liquid at refrigerator temperature, a chiffon cake stays soft when chilled, which is why it is used for chilled and frozen cakes where a butter sponge would go hard.

How the parts divide the jobs:

| Component | Job |
|---|---|
| Meringue (whites + part of the sugar) | Main aeration: millions of stable cells |
| Oil | Tenderness, moist mouthfeel, softness when cold; less destructive than you might expect because it is already emulsified into the yolk batter before the meringue arrives |
| Yolks | Emulsify the oil into the water phase; richness |
| Baking powder | Enlarges the meringue's cells in the oven, adding volume the meringue alone cannot sustain under the weight of oil and liquid |
| Water | Dissolves sugar, gelatinizes starch; high moisture |

**Critical steps** (full detail in [R1-19](recipes/R1-19-chiffon-cake.md)):
- Whisk the base batter just smooth; it is fluid and needs no aeration.
- Whip the meringue to **firm but not dry** peaks, glossy, tips that bend slightly. An over-whipped, dry meringue does not fold in; it leaves white lumps and the cake has holes.
- Fold one-third of the meringue into the base vigorously (to lighten it), then the rest gently in two parts.

### Why an ungreased tube pan

A chiffon batter is tall, light and heavy with liquid. It rises best when it can **grip** the walls and climb them. On a greased or nonstick surface it slides down; it rises less and pulls away. The **central tube** lets heat reach the centre of a tall cake, so it sets before the middle over-expands.

### Why cool it upside down

When the cake comes out of the oven, the centre is still at 95–99 °C and its walls are soft: the starch gel is weak until it cools, and the steam in the cells is about to condense. Left upright, the cake's own weight compresses the soft crumb and it sinks by a quarter or more. **Inverted immediately** (hung on the tube over a bottle, or on the pan's feet), the cake hangs from the walls it is stuck to: gravity stretches the crumb instead of compressing it, holding it at full height while it cools and firms. That only works if the pan was ungreased. Leave it inverted **until fully cool, at least 1.5–2 h**, then release it with a thin knife.

## Folding, measured

Folding is where most foam cakes are won or lost, and it is hard to judge by eye. Use your SG cup:

1. Measure the SG of the foam (génoise) or meringue just before folding.
2. Measure the SG of the finished batter.
3. Record both and the number of strokes.

Over a few bakes you will see your personal loss (for example, foam 0.30 → batter 0.46) and learn what "fold until just combined" looks like in numbers. A sudden jump in batter SG is the earliest warning that you over-folded or that the butter was too hot.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Keep the bain-marie water below a simmer and your hands away from the steam. The foams in this lesson contain raw or lightly warmed egg; do not taste the batter. Hot tube pans are awkward to invert: use dry oven gloves and invert onto a stable bottle before you let go.

</div>

## What goes wrong

- **Génoise collapsed, dense or rubbery at the base:** under-whipped foam, over-folding, butter too hot or not tempered. See [Sponge cake collapsed or deflated](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=sponge-cake-collapsed-or-deflated).
- **Chiffon fell out of the pan or shrank:** greased or nonstick pan, not inverted, underbaked. See [Chiffon fell out of the pan or shrank](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=chiffon-fell-out-of-the-pan-or-shrank).
- **White lumps or holes in chiffon:** over-whipped, dry meringue, under-folded. See [Coarse, open crumb](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=coarse-open-crumb).
- **Egg foam that will not rise:** fat in the bowl, eggs too cold, not warmed. See [Eggs, foams, emulsions](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md).

## Lab notebook

- Egg weight (out of shell), sugar weight, warming temperature reached.
- Whipping time and speed; volume increase (mark the bowl or measure SG); ribbon time (s).
- Number of folding strokes per addition; SG before and after folding.
- Butter temperature at addition; tempering done or not.
- Time from last fold to oven; oven temperature; bake time; springback; internal temperature.
- Height after cooling (centre and edge), crumb, and for chiffon the height at unmoulding vs at removal.

## Check yourself

1. Why is a génoise foam warmed but a meringue for chiffon usually whipped from room-temperature whites?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Whole eggs foam poorly because of yolk fat and a lower protein concentration; warming dissolves the sugar, lowers viscosity and helps the proteins form films, giving much more volume. Egg whites foam readily on their own; the meringue's sugar is added gradually and dissolves during whipping, so warming is not needed (it is used for Swiss meringue for other reasons, [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md)).

</details>

2. You forget to temper the melted butter and pour it straight into the génoise batter at 70 °C. Predict the cake.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The hot, dense fat sinks, bursts bubbles on its way down and pools at the bottom; heat also thins the foam. Expect lower volume, a dense, greasy, rubbery layer at the base, and possibly streaks of butter in the crumb.

</details>

3. Why does a chiffon cake use baking powder when a génoise does not?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

A chiffon carries a lot of oil and water, which weigh down and weaken the foam; the meringue alone cannot lift that load to the target height. Baking powder releases CO₂ that enlarges the meringue's existing cells in the oven. A génoise has a much higher proportion of foam to heavy ingredients and relies on the foam's air and steam alone.

</details>

4. Your génoise batter SG is 0.62. What does that tell you, and what will the cake look like?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Much foam was lost: typically the foam was under-whipped or it was over-folded, or the butter deflated it. The cake will be low, dense and possibly have a firm, wet band at the bottom.

</details>

5. Explain why inverting a chiffon cake to cool keeps it tall, and why this does not work for a génoise baked in a buttered pan.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

While hot the crumb is weak and the steam in its cells is condensing; upright, its weight compresses it. Inverted, the cake hangs from the ungreased walls it adhered to, so gravity stretches rather than compresses it until the starch and protein structure has cooled and firmed. A génoise in a buttered pan does not adhere, so it would fall out; it is also shorter and stronger, so it needs no inversion.

</details>
