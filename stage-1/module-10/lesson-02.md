## Why this lesson exists

In [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md) you used the creaming method for a quick bread, where a little under-creaming barely shows. In a pound cake it is the whole story: there is no chemical leavener in the classic formula, so every bit of lift comes from air you beat into the butter and from the steam that expands it. Creaming is a process with a temperature window, a time window and an emulsion that can break. This lesson turns "cream until light and fluffy" into numbers you can hit and check.

## You will be able to

- Set up creaming correctly: butter temperature, sugar crystal size, mixer speed and time, with a measurable endpoint.
- Add eggs without breaking the emulsion, and rescue a curdled batter.
- Explain the alternating dry/liquid addition and when it applies.
- Bake a pound cake with a controlled split and judge doneness by internal temperature and skewer.
- Compare the creaming and reverse-creaming methods and choose between them for a given crumb.

## What creaming actually does

Plastic butter at the right temperature is a network of fat crystals holding liquid oil and a little water. When you beat sugar into it, the sharp edges of the sugar crystals cut into the fat and drag air in with them. The fat crystals, especially the small β′ crystals in butter, arrange themselves around each air pocket and hold it, like a wall made of tiny bricks ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md), [08.1](stage-1/module-08/lesson-01.md)). Each sugar crystal is a small air pump; the more crystals and the more edges, the more cells.

What you get after good creaming is a **fat-continuous foam**: thousands of small air cells stabilized by fat crystals, with undissolved sugar crystals dispersed through it. As you add egg, the water in the egg dissolves some of the sugar and is dispersed as droplets into the fat, stabilized by egg-yolk lecithin and proteins: the batter becomes an **emulsion-foam**. Those air cells are the nuclei of the final crumb ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)): in the oven they are inflated by air expansion, steam and (if present) CO₂ from baking powder, but no new cells appear.

### The variables that decide creaming

| Variable | Target | Too low | Too high |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Butter temperature** | **18–20 °C**: plastic, dents without resistance, not shiny or greasy | Below ~15 °C: crystals too firm, butter breaks into lumps and traps little air | Above ~23–24 °C: too much liquid oil, crystals cannot hold cells, batter greasy and heavy |
| **Sugar crystal size** | Fine granulated or caster sugar | Powdered (icing) sugar: no crystal edges and contains starch; little aeration, very fine, dense crumb | Coarse sugar: fewer crystals, fewer cells, some sugar left undissolved (spots on the crust) |
| **Mixer and speed** | Stand mixer with paddle at medium (KitchenAid 4–6); hand mixer at medium-high | Low speed: slow aeration | High speed: warms the butter by friction, splashes, and can overheat it |
| **Time** | **4–6 min** in a stand mixer at medium; scrape down every 2 min | Under 2–3 min: few cells, SG near 1.0 | Beyond ~8–10 min: butter warms, cells coalesce, little further gain |
| **Batch size vs bowl** | Butter at least 2–3 cm deep in the bowl | Too small a batch in a large bowl: the paddle barely touches the butter | — |

**Endpoint of creaming.** The mixture is visibly paler (from butter yellow to ivory), about 1.5–2 times its starting volume, soft and fluffy, and holds soft peaks on the paddle. Rubbed between two fingers, it still feels slightly grainy: the sugar is not dissolved yet, and it should not be. Measure SG on the full batter after the eggs rather than on the stiff creamed mass, which is hard to fill into a cup without voids.

<div class="callout science">

**Science:** Why 18–20 °C? Butter's solid fat fraction falls steeply between 15 and 25 °C. Around 18–20 °C there are enough crystals to hold air but enough liquid oil to let the mass deform and fold around new cells. Friction in the mixer adds 1–3 °C over 5 minutes, which is why you start near 18 °C rather than 22 °C. Shortening has a wider plastic range and more small crystals, so it creams to a lower SG over a wider temperature range than butter; that is its main advantage in production, at the cost of flavour.

</div>

## Adding the eggs: keeping the emulsion

A pound cake contains equal weights of butter and eggs: about 80 g of fat and 75 g of water from the eggs for every 100 g of each, plus the ~16 g of water in the butter. You are asking a fat-continuous system to take up a lot of water. It can, if you give it time.

**Rules:**
- **Eggs at room temperature, 18–22 °C.** Cold eggs chill the butter below its plastic range: crystals firm, the emulsion breaks and the mixture looks curdled.
- **Beat the eggs lightly first** so whites and yolks are uniform and you can pour them in small, even additions.
- **Add gradually:** 6–8 additions for a pound cake (roughly one egg's worth in two parts), at medium speed, beating 30–45 s after each until the mixture is smooth and glossy again before the next.
- **Scrape the bowl** after every second addition.

**Endpoint:** smooth, glossy, thick batter that holds its shape on the paddle; no visible droplets or wet sheen; temperature 20–23 °C. Creamed cake batters are commonly held to **21–24 °C** as standard professional practice.

### Curdled batter: causes and fix

A curdled batter looks grainy and wet, like cottage cheese; watery droplets separate from the fat. The fat-continuous emulsion has broken because there is more water than the fat can hold, or the fat is at the wrong temperature to hold it.

| Cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|
| Eggs cold (butter firmed) | Warm the outside of the bowl briefly (a warm damp towel, 10–20 s over a water bath, or a few seconds with a torch) while mixing, until smooth | Eggs at 18–22 °C |
| Eggs added too fast | Stop adding, beat 1–2 min; if still curdled, add 1–2 tablespoons of the weighed flour to absorb free water | Smaller additions, beat until smooth each time |
| Butter too warm (above ~24 °C) | Chill the bowl 5–10 min, then beat | Butter at 18–20 °C; shorter creaming in a hot kitchen |
| Very high egg-to-fat ratio | Add some of the flour with the last additions | Recognize the formula is at the limit (eggs ≥ fat is fine; eggs ≫ fat curdles easily) |

<div class="callout warn">

**Warning:** A slightly curdled batter that comes back together when the flour is added usually bakes acceptably. A badly broken batter loses air, bakes denser and coarser, and may leak butter. Fixing the temperature is almost always the answer; flour is a patch.

</div>

## Adding dry ingredients and liquids

In a pound cake there is no liquid besides eggs, so the flour (sifted with any salt and baking powder) is added in two or three parts on low speed, or folded in by hand, **only until no dry flour is visible**, about 30–45 s by mixer. Finish with a few strokes of a spatula from the bottom.

In butter cakes with milk (the general creaming method you met in [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md)), you **alternate**: dry in three parts, liquid in two, starting and ending with dry (D-L-D-L-D). The reasons:
- Adding all the milk at once would overload the emulsion and curdle it.
- Adding all the flour first would make a stiff paste that needs long mixing to take up the milk, developing gluten.
- Starting with flour gives the free water something to bind to; ending with flour leaves the batter smooth.

Mix each addition on low speed only until just combined (10–20 s). Total mixing after the flour first touches liquid should be short: in this batter, gluten forms whenever flour, water and mechanical work meet.

## The pound cake

[R1-17](recipes/R1-17-pound-cake.md) is a quatre-quarts: equal weights of flour, butter, sugar and eggs (200 g each for a 22 × 11 cm loaf pan). As you saw in [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md), it sits exactly on all three formula-balance lines. It has a naturally tight but tender crumb, a high fat content that keeps well, and a slow bake.

### Why a slow bake

A pound cake is a thick, dense mass (SG ~0.85, 6–7 cm of batter in a loaf pan) with little leavening. At 180 °C the outside sets and browns long before the centre heats; you get a thick dark crust, a big dome and a raw centre. At **160–170 °C (320–340 °F)** the heat front moves in more evenly. Expect **50–65 min** for 800 g of batter in a metal loaf pan.

### The controlled split

A loaf-shaped pound cake almost always cracks along the top: the sides and top set first and the still-fluid centre pushes up through the weakest point. You cannot stop it, but you can choose where it goes:

- **Pipe a line of soft butter** (about 5 g, piped with a paper cone or a small bag) along the centre of the batter surface before baking. That strip stays fluid longer, so the cake splits cleanly along it, giving the classic single central crack.
- **Or score it:** after 12–15 min, when a thin skin has formed on top, pull the cake out briefly and run a thin knife dipped in oil 1 cm deep along the centre. Work fast; the door should be open less than 20 s.

### Doneness

| Test | Done when |
|---|---|
| **Internal temperature** | **93–99 °C** in the centre; aim for about 96 °C for a pound cake. Measure in the thickest part, under the split |
| **Skewer** | A thin wooden skewer inserted into the centre of the split comes out clean or with a few dry crumbs, never wet batter |
| **The split** | The inside of the crack looks dry and lightly coloured, not glossy and wet |
| **Sides** | The cake has begun to pull away from the sides of the pan |

The range is wide because formulas vary: richer, sweeter cakes are safe at the lower end, leaner ones set higher. Your own records will narrow it. A pound cake pulled at 90 °C often looks fine, then shows a gummy streak just under the split when cut.

## Reverse creaming (the two-stage or "paste" method)

In **reverse creaming**, you do not aerate fat with sugar first. You mix all the dry ingredients including the sugar and leavener, then beat the soft butter into them until the mixture looks like damp sand; then add the liquids in two stages, beating the first stage for about a minute to aerate and build the batter.

| | Creaming method | Reverse creaming |
|---|---|---|
| First step | Butter + sugar beaten 4–6 min | Dry ingredients + sugar; butter paddled in 1–2 min until sandy |
| Where the air comes from | Sugar crystals cutting into fat | Beating the paste with the first part of the liquid |
| Gluten | Flour added last; some gluten can form when mixed with liquid | Flour coated with fat before water arrives; very little gluten forms |
| Crumb | Slightly more volume, more open, a little more domed | Finer, more even, very tender, "velvety"; flatter top |
| Tolerance | Sensitive to butter temperature and egg addition (curdling) | Less prone to curdling; more tolerant of long mixing |
| Typical use | Pound cake, many butter cakes | High-ratio and "velvet" layer cakes, cakes that must be cut into thin even layers |

Reverse creaming comes from industrial high-ratio mixing, where emulsified shortening is blended into the dry ingredients first. It suits formulas with liquid besides eggs; you will compare the two methods on the same butter-cake formula in [X1-27](experiments/X1-27-reverse-creaming.md).

**Basic reverse-creaming procedure (for a butter cake with milk):**
1. Sift flour, sugar, baking powder and salt into the mixer bowl; mix 30 s on low.
2. Add butter at 18–20 °C in pieces; paddle on low 1–2 min until no pieces are larger than a pea and the mixture looks like damp sand.
3. Whisk eggs, milk and flavouring together. Add about half with the mixer on low, then beat on **medium for 60–90 s**: the batter lightens and thickens. Scrape.
4. Add the rest of the liquid in two parts, 15–20 s on low each, until smooth. Measure SG and temperature.

## What goes wrong

- **Curdled batter:** cold eggs, eggs added too fast, butter too warm. See [Curdled creamed batter](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=curdled-creamed-batter).
- **Dense, heavy pound cake:** under-creamed, butter too cold or too warm, broken emulsion, overmixed after flour. See [Dense, heavy cake](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=dense-heavy-cake).
- **Tunnels and a peaked top:** overmixing after the flour, oven too hot. See [Tunnels](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=tunnels).
- **Gummy streak under the split:** underbaked, pulled below ~93 °C. See [Gummy layer or streak](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=gummy-layer-or-streak).

## Check yourself

1. Your kitchen is at 27 °C. Your butter has been out for three hours and is shiny. What will creaming give you, and what do you do?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The butter is probably 24–26 °C: too much liquid oil to hold air, so it creams to a greasy, heavy mass with a high SG, and the batter will curdle or turn soupy with the eggs. Chill it until it is 18–20 °C (check with a probe), chill the bowl, keep the eggs at about 18–20 °C rather than warmer, and shorten creaming slightly so friction does not overheat it.

</details>

2. Why does powdered (icing) sugar give a denser creamed cake than caster sugar at the same weight?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Creaming relies on sugar crystal edges to cut air into the fat. Powdered sugar is ground to tiny particles (with added starch), so it has no useful crystal edges and incorporates little air. The batter has fewer cells, a higher SG, and the cake is denser and finer.

</details>

3. A batter curdles after the fifth of eight egg additions. The eggs were at 12 °C. What happened, and how do you fix it without adding flour?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The cold eggs chilled the butter below its plastic range; the fat crystals firmed and could no longer hold the dispersed water, so the emulsion broke. Warm the bowl gently (warm damp towel or a few seconds over a water bath) while beating at medium until smooth and glossy, then continue with smaller additions. Next time bring eggs to 18–22 °C.

</details>

4. In a butter cake with milk, why start and end with the dry ingredients when alternating?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Starting with flour gives the free water something to bind to, so the emulsion is not overloaded when the first milk goes in. Ending with flour leaves a smooth, thick batter without extra mixing. Alternation avoids both curdling (all liquid at once) and a stiff paste needing long mixing and developing gluten (all flour at once).

</details>

5. You need a very fine, even crumb for a cake that will be cut into three thin layers. Which mixing method, and why?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Reverse creaming. Coating the flour with fat before water arrives limits gluten formation, and aeration happens by beating a fine emulsion rather than by sugar crystals, giving a finer, more uniform, very tender crumb and a flatter top that wastes less when levelled.

</details>
