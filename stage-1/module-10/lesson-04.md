## Why this lesson exists

The same batter can make a flat, even layer or a cracked, peaked, dark-edged one depending on the pan, its colour, how full it is and where it sits in the oven. Cakes are then judged "done" by tests that each measure something different, cooled in ways that can undo the bake, and stored in ways that stale them in a day. This lesson closes the loop from batter to slice and ends with the cake clinic: a set of failed cakes to diagnose using everything in M10.

## You will be able to

- Convert a formula between pan sizes by area, and choose a filling level.
- Predict how pan material and colour, baking strips and oven position change doming, crust colour and bake time.
- Judge doneness using at least three indicators and say what each one measures.
- Cool and unmould each cake type correctly.
- Make and dose a soaking syrup by weight.
- Store cakes to control staling and drying.
- Diagnose a failed cake from its symptoms to the most likely cause and the test that confirms it.

## Pans: size, area and filling

### Converting between pan sizes

Batter quantity scales with the **area** of the pan if you want the same depth (and therefore about the same bake time). For round pans, area scales with the square of the diameter:

**factor = (d_new ÷ d_old)²**

| Pan | Area (cm²) | Factor vs 20 cm round |
|---|---|---|
| 15 cm round | 177 | 0.56 |
| 18 cm round | 254 | 0.81 |
| **20 cm round** | **314** | **1.00** |
| 23 cm round | 415 | 1.32 |
| 25 cm round | 491 | 1.56 |
| 20 × 20 cm square | 400 | 1.27 |
| 23 × 23 cm square | 529 | 1.68 |
| 30 × 40 cm sheet (for rolled sponge) | 1,200 | 3.82 |

For rectangles, area = length × width. Loaf pans have sloping sides, so use volume instead: fill a pan with water, weigh it (1 g = 1 ml), and scale batter by the ratio of volumes at the same filling level.

**Worked example.** [R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md) makes 470.5 g of batter for a 20 cm round. For a 24 cm round: (24 ÷ 20)² = 1.44; batter = 470.5 × 1.44 = 677.5 g. Scale every ingredient by 1.44 (eggs 288 g, sugar 172.8 g, flour 172.8 g, butter 43.2 g).

If you change area but keep the batter weight, the depth changes, and so does bake time: a deeper cake takes disproportionately longer because heat has further to travel to the centre, and its edges may overbake before the centre sets. When depth increases by more than about a third, lower the oven 10 °C and expect a longer bake.

### Filling level

| Cake | Fill to | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Butter layer cakes, génoise | **½ to ⅔** of the pan depth | Room to rise without overflowing; side walls support the rise |
| Pound cake in a loaf pan | about ⅔ | Dense batter, modest rise, the split rises above the rim |
| Chiffon, angel food (tube pan) | about ⅔ | The foam climbs the walls and the tube |
| Cupcakes | ⅔ of the cup | Rounded top just above the paper |

A pan that is too empty gives a pale, thin cake with over-browned edges; too full, and the batter overflows or the centre, still liquid, sinks under its own weight.

### Pan material and colour

| Pan | Behaviour | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Light (shiny) aluminium | Reflects radiant heat, conducts well: even, light-coloured crust | Reference; formulas in this course assume it |
| Dark metal (nonstick, anodized dark) | Absorbs more radiant heat: darker sides and base, faster edge set, more doming | Lower the oven about 10–15 °C; check 5–10 min earlier |
| Glass or ceramic | Slow to heat up, then holds heat; edges overbake while the centre is slow | Lower about 10–15 °C; allow longer bake |
| Silicone | Poor conductor: pale, soft sides, slow bake; flexible (put on a tray) | Bake on a preheated tray; expect pale sides |

These adjustments are standard professional practice; confirm them with your own oven and a probe.

### Baking strips and heating cores

The edges of a cake heat first because the metal wall conducts heat into them. They set early, while the centre is still rising, so the centre domes up (and sometimes cracks). **Baking strips** (cotton or aluminized fabric strips soaked in water and wrapped around the outside of the pan) hold the pan wall near 100 °C while the water evaporates, so the edge and centre set closer together: flatter top, paler, softer sides. A foil-wrapped strip of wet kitchen towel does the same job.

For pans 25 cm and larger, a **heating core** (or an inverted metal flower nail, greased, placed in the centre of the pan) conducts heat into the middle.

### Oven position

- One cake: **centre rack**, centre of the oven. The top of the cake should be at the middle of the oven cavity.
- Several pans: at least **2.5–5 cm** between pans and from the walls, so air can circulate; do not stack pans directly above each other on two racks (the lower one shields the upper one's base and the upper one shades the lower one's top). If you must use two racks, stagger them and swap positions after the structure has set (about two-thirds of the bake).
- Do not open the door in the first half of the bake: a sudden drop in temperature before the structure sets can make the centre fall.
- Fan ovens: many cakes bake more evenly with the fan off, or with the fan at 10–20 °C lower than the conventional setting; a strong fan can blow light batters lopsided.

## Doneness

| Indicator | How | What it tells you | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Internal temperature** | Thin probe into the centre of the thickest part | Most cakes are done at **93–99 °C** in the centre; richer, sweeter cakes are safe at the lower end | Quick and objective, but a hole in a thin sponge is visible |
| **Springback** | Press the centre lightly with a fingertip | Springs back fully = the foam walls have set | Can mislead in a dense pound cake |
| **Skewer or cake tester** | Insert into the centre, withdraw | Clean or a few dry crumbs = no liquid batter | Fudgy cakes (brownies) are meant to have moist crumbs |
| **Pulling from the sides** | Look at the edge | The cake has set and begun to shrink as steam leaves | Late indicator: when very pronounced, often slightly overbaked |
| **Colour and sound** | Even golden top; a quiet cake (a wet one "crackles" or bubbles faintly) | Supporting indicators | Colour depends on sugar and pan, not doneness |

Use at least two: internal temperature plus springback or skewer. Record the value at which the cake was right for you; after a few bakes you will know, for example, that your pound cake is best pulled at 96 °C and your génoise at 97 °C.

<div class="callout warn">

**Warning:** A cake that is pulled too early can look perfect for five minutes and then sink as the steam in its cells condenses and the not-quite-set crumb cannot hold itself up. If a cake sinks only after leaving the oven, suspect underbaking before you suspect the formula.

</div>

## Cooling and unmoulding

| Cake | In the pan | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Pound cake, butter cakes | **10–15 min** on a rack: the crumb firms enough to handle | Run a thin knife around the edge, turn out, cool upright on a rack |
| Génoise | **5 min** | Turn out onto a rack (lined with parchment); many pastry chefs cool it upside down to flatten the top |
| Chiffon, angel food | **Invert immediately** | Cool fully inverted, 1.5–2 h; release with a thin knife ([10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md)) |
| Rolled sponge sheet | Out of the tin at once | Roll warm in a towel or parchment if it will be rolled later; cool covered so it stays flexible |

Too early and a butter cake breaks; too late and trapped steam condenses on the pan walls, making the sides damp and sticky and gluing the cake to the pan (especially where sugar has caramelized). Cool completely (below about 30 °C in the centre, typically 1–2 h) before wrapping, cutting into layers or filling; a warm cake crumbles when cut and melts buttercream.

## Soaking syrups

Génoise and biscuit are designed to be moistened after baking. A **soaking syrup** (also imbibing syrup) adds moisture, sweetness and flavour, and slows drying.

**Make it by weight:**

| Syrup | Sugar : water by weight | Sugar concentration | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | 1 : 1.5 | 40 % | Delicate sponges, fruit-flavoured cakes |
| Standard | **1 : 1** | **50 %** | Most génoise layers |
| Heavy | **1.35 : 1** | **57 %** | Dry biscuits, cakes that must keep several days; dilute with liqueur or juice |

Bring the water and sugar to a boil, stirring until the sugar dissolves completely, then cool. Add flavour off the heat: vanilla, citrus zest (infused, then strained), coffee, or liqueur once below about 40 °C so the alcohol does not evaporate. Cooled, covered and refrigerated, it keeps for about a week. Syrup concentrations and boiling are developed in [13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md) and [R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md).

**Dosing.** Weigh the cake layer, then weigh the syrup you will use. As a starting point, apply syrup equal to **25–40 % of the sponge's weight**: the low end for a buttery génoise, the high end for a dry biscuit. Brush it on with a soft pastry brush, working from the edge inwards, in two passes a few minutes apart so it soaks in rather than pooling. Too little and the layer tastes dry against the cream; too much and it turns soggy and the cake leaks at the base.

**Worked example.** A 20 cm génoise weighs 410 g after baking and cooling, split into three layers (about 137 g each). At 30 %: 0.30 × 137 ≈ 41 g of syrup per layer, 123 g in total. With a 1 : 1 syrup that is about 62 g sugar and 62 g water (make 130–140 g to allow for loss in the brush and pan).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Boiling syrup is well above 100 °C only when concentrated ([13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md)); even a 1 : 1 syrup scalds. Cool it before brushing, and keep children and pets away from the stove.

</div>

## Storage and staling of cakes

Cakes stale by the same mechanism as bread ([04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md)): gelatinized starch retrogrades and the crumb firms. But cakes stale **more slowly** than bread, because sugar, fat, egg yolk and emulsifiers interfere with starch recrystallization and hold water. In cakes, **drying at cut and exposed surfaces** is usually the bigger problem.

| Storage | Effect | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature, wrapped tightly once cool | Slowest staling; pound cake often tastes better on day 2 as moisture evens out | Unfilled butter and pound cakes: 3–5 days; génoise 1–2 days |
| Refrigerator | Retrogradation fastest (0–5 °C); butter in the crumb hardens, so butter cakes eat firm and dry until warmed | Only cakes with perishable fillings (cream, custard, fresh fruit), which must be refrigerated for safety |
| Freezer (−18 °C), tightly wrapped | Staling practically stopped; thaw wrapped to avoid condensation | Génoise layers, pound cake: best within about 1–2 months |

Chiffon, made with oil, stays soft when refrigerated; that is why it is favoured for chilled cakes. Syrup, liquid sugars (honey, invert syrup) and a higher proportion of yolk or fat all slow perceived staling.

## The cake clinic

For each case, write down (1) the stage where it went wrong (formula, mixing, pan, oven, cooling), (2) the most likely cause, and (3) the measurement or test that would confirm it. Then open the answer. Use the [cakes troubleshooting page](troubleshooting/cakes.md).

**Case 1.** A 20 cm butter cake rose well above the rim, then the centre sank 1.5 cm while still in the oven during the last 5 minutes. Crumb coarse, slightly sticky under the dip; top crust dark and sugary. Batter SG was normal.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: formula (or measuring). Likely cause: too much sugar or too much leavener for the flour and egg, so the batter set too late and over-expanded; the dark sugary crust supports excess sugar. Test: re-check the weighed formula against the balance rules ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)); weigh the baking powder on a 0.1 g scale; re-bake with sugar at or below flour weight. See [X1-29](experiments/X1-29-cake-formula-balance.md).

</details>

**Case 2.** A pound cake has a high, peaked, cracked dome, and when cut shows long vertical holes rising from the base towards the peak. Crumb slightly chewy.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: mixing (and oven). Likely cause: overmixing after the flour was added (gluten development) and/or oven too hot, setting the outside early. Test: log mixing time after flour; check oven with a thermometer; re-bake mixing flour only to just combined, at 160–170 °C. See [Tunnels](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=tunnels).

</details>

**Case 3.** A génoise is 2.5 cm tall instead of 4 cm. The bottom 8 mm is a dense, yellow, rubbery layer. The butter was melted in the microwave and folded in straight away.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: mixing (folding). Likely cause: hot, untempered butter sank and burst the foam, forming a greasy layer at the base; possibly the foam was also under-whipped. Test: measure SG of the foam and of the batter before and after the butter; next time melt to 40–50 °C and temper with a tenth of the batter. See [Sponge cake collapsed or deflated](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=sponge-cake-collapsed-or-deflated).

</details>

**Case 4.** A butter cake looks perfect in the oven, passes the colour test, is pulled at 27 minutes and sinks by 1 cm over the next 10 minutes on the rack. When cut, a grey, wet, gummy band runs just below the top.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: oven (doneness). Likely cause: underbaked; the centre had not set, so it collapsed as steam condensed. Test: probe the next cake: it must reach 93–99 °C in the centre and pass the skewer test, not just the colour test. See [Gummy layer or streak](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=gummy-layer-or-streak).

</details>

**Case 5.** Two identical butter cakes baked on the same day: one in a shiny aluminium pan is level and golden; one in a dark nonstick pan is domed, cracked, with dark hard sides.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: pan. The dark pan absorbed more radiant heat, set and browned the edges early, so the centre domed and cracked. Test: bake again in the dark pan at 10–15 °C lower, or with baking strips; compare heights at centre and edge.

</details>

**Case 6.** A creamed cake is low and dense with a tight, uniform crumb and a slightly greasy mouthfeel. The butter came from the fridge and was softened in the microwave until partly melted. Batter SG 1.02.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: mixing (creaming). The butter was too warm (partly liquid), so it could not hold air: SG 1.02 shows almost no aeration. Leavener had few cells to inflate. Test: repeat with butter at 18–20 °C checked by probe; SG should be around 0.8–0.9. See [Dense, heavy cake](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=dense-heavy-cake).

</details>

**Case 7.** A chiffon cake baked in a new nonstick tube pan: when inverted it slid out onto the counter after 10 minutes and ended up 30 % shorter, dense at the bottom.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Stage: pan (and cooling). The nonstick surface gave the foam nothing to grip, so it could not hang while cooling and its weight compressed the hot crumb. Test: bake in a plain, ungreased aluminium tube pan; measure height at removal and after cooling. See [Chiffon fell out of the pan or shrank](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=chiffon-fell-out-of-the-pan-or-shrank).

</details>

## On the bench

1. **Pan conversion bake.** Re-bake [R1-17](recipes/R1-17-pound-cake.md) or [R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md) in a different pan, scaling the batter by area or volume. Record bake time and internal temperature; compare with the original.
2. **Baking strip test (optional).** Bake two butter-cake layers from one batter, one with a wet strip. Measure centre and edge height of each.
3. **Syrup.** Make 150 g of 1 : 1 syrup. Split a génoise into three layers, weigh each, and brush one at 25 %, one at 35 % and one not at all. Taste after 1 h and after 24 h (covered, refrigerated if filled).
4. **Clinic.** Work through the cases above in your notebook before opening the answers, then do the same with any failed cakes from X1-26 to X1-29.

## What goes wrong

- **Cake stuck in the pan:** poor greasing, left too long in the pan, sugar caramelized at the wall. See [Cake stuck in the pan](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=cake-stuck-in-the-pan).
- **Lopsided rise:** uneven oven, pan not level, batter not levelled, fan. See [Uneven rise or lopsided cake](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=uneven-rise-or-lopsided-cake).
- **Crust too dark:** dark pan, oven too hot, high sugar. See [Crust too dark](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=crust-too-dark).
- **Soggy or sticky top:** wrapped while warm, underbaked, humid storage, too much syrup. See [Soggy or sticky top](troubleshooting/cakes.md?id=soggy-or-sticky-top).

## Lab notebook

- Pan: size, material, colour, volume (water-filled weight), filling level and batter weight.
- Oven: measured temperature, rack position, fan on/off, strips or core.
- Doneness: time; internal temperature; springback; skewer; sides.
- Cooling: time in pan, method; any sinking (height at removal vs after cooling).
- Syrup: ratio, weight applied, % of layer weight; tasting notes at 1 h and 24 h.
- Storage: method and days until noticeably dry or stale.

## Check yourself

1. A recipe written for a 23 cm round pan must be baked in an 18 cm round pan at the same depth. By what factor do you scale it?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

(18 ÷ 23)² = 0.7826² ≈ 0.61. Scale every ingredient by 0.61.

</details>

2. Why do baking strips reduce doming?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

The water in the strips keeps the pan wall near 100 °C while it evaporates, so heat reaches the batter at the edges more slowly. The edges and centre set closer together in time; the centre is no longer forced up through an already-set rim.

</details>

3. Name three doneness indicators and what each actually measures.

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Internal temperature: whether the centre has reached the setting range (93–99 °C for most cakes). Springback: whether the foam walls near the surface have set. Skewer: whether any liquid batter remains in the centre. Pulling from the sides: that the cake has set and started to lose steam and shrink (a late indicator).

</details>

4. You need 120 g of heavy syrup at 1.35 : 1. How much sugar and water?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Total parts = 2.35. Sugar = 120 × 1.35 ÷ 2.35 = 68.9 g; water = 120 × 1 ÷ 2.35 = 51.1 g. (Boil a few grams more to allow for evaporation.)

</details>

5. Why should a plain butter pound cake not be stored in the refrigerator?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer</summary>

Starch retrogradation is fastest at refrigerator temperatures, and the butter in the crumb hardens, so the cake tastes firm, dry and stale sooner. Store it tightly wrapped at room temperature for a few days, or freeze it.

</details>
