# Research report: how professional baking and pastry education is structured

Before designing this course, I studied how serious professional programmes organize baking
and pastry education, from French diploma schools to American degree programmes, artisan
bread institutes, trade certifications and university bakery-science degrees. This report
summarizes what they have in common, where they differ, what they leave out, and the design
decisions that follow for Stage 1. The raw notes, with confidence levels for each claim, are
in [research-notes.md](research/research-notes.md).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Common subjects

Almost every programme contains: food safety and hygiene (always first, usually with a
certified exam); tools, terminology and mise en place; ingredient functions (flour, sugar,
fat, eggs, dairy, leaveners); pastes and doughs (short, sweet, puff, choux); creams and
custards (pâtissière, anglaise, mousseline, ganache); sponges, cakes and biscuits; yeast
breads and viennoiserie; plated and frozen desserts; chocolate tempering and basic
confectionery; decoration; and costing.

### 2.2 The progression from beginner to advanced

The progression is remarkably consistent across Le Cordon Bleu, ENSP, the CIA, Johnson &
Wales and City & Guilds:

| Level | Content |
|---|---|
| **Foundation** | Safety, tools, weighing and baker's %, ingredient functions, then the "mother" preparations: pastes, creams, meringues, basic sponges and cakes, lean yeast dough, basic enriched dough, introduction to viennoiserie. |
| **Intermediate** | Assembly of components into composite products (entremets, tarts, laminated doughs), preferments and sourdough, tempering and moulding, plated desserts, confectionery, ice-cream balancing. |
| **Advanced** | Showpieces in sugar and chocolate, contemporary and creative design, production management, costing, supervision. |

**The key observation: difficulty rises by the number of components and controlled
variables, not by switching to new product families.** City & Guilds Level 2 and Level 3
teach the same families; Level 3 simply demands more of them. This is exactly the property
an append-only curriculum needs: Stage 2 can deepen the same families that Stage 1 opens.

### 2.3 Prerequisite relationships observed

- Food safety → any production.
- Ingredient function + baker's math → scaling and troubleshooting.
- Lean yeast dough and fermentation control → enriched doughs → laminated doughs (SFBI I → III;
  Le Cordon Bleu Basic → Advanced Boulangerie).
- Straight doughs → preferments → sourdough/levain → retarding (SFBI I → II).
- Base creams + sponges → entremets and mousses (ENSP module 1 → 4).
- Sugar cooking → confectionery → sugar work.
- Tempering → moulding → showpieces (Le Cordon Bleu Intermediate → Superior).
- Egg, starch and emulsion basics → ice cream, plated desserts.

### 2.4 Practical competencies expected at foundation level

Across CAP bloc 1, City & Guilds Level 2, Le Cordon Bleu Basic, SFBI Series I, JWU
Foundations and CIA Techniques, a foundation-level student is expected to: read and scale
formulas; organize a station; receive and store goods; make shortcrust, sweet and puff
pastry, choux, pastry cream, crème anglaise, three meringues, génoise, travel cakes and
cookies; make lean bread, a preferment bread, brioche and an introductory croissant; pipe,
glaze and finish simply; and evaluate the quality of their own product.

### 2.5 Food science in culinary programmes

It is applied and thin. ENSP devotes 22 hours to food science; the CIA has three credits of
culinary science plus a 1.5-credit ingredients-and-equipment course and an "Advanced Baking
Principles" course; Johnson & Wales' associate degree has only a nutrition course; Le Cordon
Bleu teaches "ingredient theory". The artisan bread schools are the exception: SFBI and
FERRANDI's bread programme teach flour function, hydration, gluten development and
fermentation control explicitly, and BBGA examines milling and baker's percentages.

### 2.6 What professional programmes omit that R&D needs

Comparing culinary programmes with Kansas State's bakery-science degree, AIB's Science of
Baking and the cereal-science literature, the following are absent or thin in culinary
training but essential for product development:

| Omitted subject | Where it appears in this course |
|---|---|
| Milling and flour specification (extraction, ash, protein, falling number, damaged starch) | 02.1, 02.3 (intro); Stage 2 S2-M01 |
| Dough rheology and flour testing | SC-12, 02.4 (intuitive); Stage 2/3 |
| Starch gelatinization and retrogradation; gluten chemistry | SC-02, SC-03, 04.2, 04.3, 06.2 |
| Enzymes | SC-09, 05.1, 12.2 |
| Water activity, moisture migration, shelf life, staling kinetics | SC-01, 04.3, X1-12, X1-43 |
| Microbiology: spoilage, sourdough ecology | SC-08, 00.3, 05.3 |
| Emulsifiers, hydrocolloids, conditioners | SC-06 (intro); Stage 2 SC2-15/16 |
| Heat and mass transfer, process calculations | SC-10, SC-11, 01.3, 04.1 |
| Sensory evaluation, statistics, experimental design | SC-14, 19.1, every experiment |
| Frozen/par-baked dough, scale-up, HACCP, labelling, reformulation | Stage 2/3 (anchored in 00.3, 18.1, 18.2) |

### 2.7 Culinary-school pastry vs artisan-bread training

| | Pastry programmes (LCB, FERRANDI, ICE, ENSP) | Bread programmes (SFBI, BBGA, LCB Boulangerie, FERRANDI bread) |
|---|---|---|
| Breadth | Many product families; bread is one module | Few product families |
| Depth | Composition, finishing, decoration, plating | Process control: fermentation time and temperature, preferments, levain, retarding, hydration, flour choice |
| Science | Ingredient-level | More explicit process science |
| Assessment | Finished product quality | Timed production, baker's % under time pressure, troubleshooting |

Viennoiserie sits between the two and is taught in both. An R&D baker needs the bread
schools' *process-control mindset* applied to the pastry schools' *breadth* — which is the
central design choice of this course.

### 2.8 Foundation vs advanced placement

**Foundation:** safety, equipment, weighing and baker's %, ingredient functions, the mother
preparations, straight-dough fermentation, basic enriched dough, introduction to preferments
and sourdough, introduction to lamination, basic chocolate, quality-evaluation vocabulary, and
introductory science (gluten, gelatinization, Maillard/caramelization, leavening gases,
emulsions).

**Advanced:** sourdough ecology and management, retarding and production scheduling,
entremets engineering (gelling agents, textures), chocolate and sugar showpieces, ice-cream
formulation, plated-dessert design, costing and management, and the R&D subjects in 2.6.

## 3. Design conclusions for Stage 1

1. **Follow the shared progression, but lead with the bread spine.** Culinary schools often
   start with pastes and creams; bread schools start with flour, water, salt and yeast. For an
   R&D-bound learner the bread spine comes first (M02–M05) because it introduces the
   foundational variables — hydration, gluten, fermentation, temperature, heat — in the
   simplest system, where one variable can be changed at a time.
2. **Teach ingredient systems before the products that combine them.** Fats (M08) and eggs
   (M09) are hub modules taught as systems; cakes, short doughs, custards, meringues, choux,
   lamination and chocolate then reuse them.
3. **Cover the full foundation repertoire.** Stage 1 includes every foundation-level
   competency in 2.4 (pastes, creams, three meringues, génoise, lean and enriched bread,
   preferments, sourdough, croissant, choux, basic chocolate), so the learner can stop after
   Stage 1 and bake at a professional-foundation level.
4. **Integrate science as a separate track, read on first use.** Culinary programmes are thin
   on science; university programmes front-load it. Here the 14 science units are read when
   the phenomenon first appears on the bench, at predictive depth.
5. **Adopt the bread schools' process-control habits for everything.** Baker's %, DDT,
   measured endpoints, timed production and troubleshooting apply to cakes and pastry too.
6. **Make experimentation and diagnosis first-class.** Every module has controlled
   experiments (44 in all) and a troubleshooting database in *symptom → cause → test →
   correction* form, anticipating the R&D subjects most programmes omit.
7. **Place intermediate/advanced topics in Stage 2 and 3 — but open their threads now.**
   Entremets, confectionery, sugar work, ice cream, advanced viennoiserie, rheology testing,
   DOE and shelf-life engineering are deferred, and each has a named anchor in Stage 1 (see the
   [extension architecture](extension/stage-2-3-architecture.md)).

The resulting architecture is the [course outline](curriculum/course-outline.md) and the
[curriculum map](curriculum/curriculum-map.md).

## 4. Limits of this research

Some details could not be verified from primary sources: ICE's exact module names (site
blocked), the CIA's semester-by-semester order, ACF pastry exam content, the current AIB
resident course, and Richemont's syllabi. None of the design conclusions depend on those
details.
