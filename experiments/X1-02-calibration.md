# X1-02 · Calibrating the kitchen: scale, thermometers and an oven map

**Module:** [M01 Measurement and baker's math](stage-1/module-01/README.md) · **Lessons:** [00.2 Setting up the kitchen lab](stage-1/module-00/lesson-02.md) · [01.1 Weighing everything](stage-1/module-01/lesson-01.md) · [01.3 Temperature as an ingredient](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md) · **Threads:** T1 Measurement · T4 Heat · T12 Experimental method · **Time:** Part 1: 30 min · Part 2: 30 min · Part 3: 2–3 h (mostly waiting)

## Question

How accurate are your instruments, and what does your oven actually do? Every later measurement in the course — dough temperature, custard endpoint, bake time — is only as good as the instruments behind it. This experiment establishes the offsets you will correct for.

It has three parts: **(1) scales**, **(2) thermometers**, **(3) oven map**. Do them in any order.

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write down before measuring:

1. Your guess of how far your main scale is off at 500 g, in grams.
2. What you expect your probe thermometer to read in iced water and in boiling water at your altitude.
3. Whether your oven runs hot or cold at the 180 °C setting, by how much, and where you think its hot spots are.
4. How long you think your oven takes, after switching on, to hold a stable temperature at 200 °C.

## Variables

| Part | Independent | Dependent | Controlled |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Scales | Known reference mass (several loads) | Displayed mass; error | Same position, level surface, fresh tare, no drafts |
| 2 Thermometers | Reference temperature (ice point, boiling point) | Displayed temperature; offset | Probe depth, stirring, time to stabilise |
| 3 Oven | Dial setting (180 / 200 / 230 °C) and time; position in the oven | Actual temperature; swing; browning pattern | Shelf position, oven mode (conventional, no fan unless tested separately), door closed, same thermometer |

## Batch and scale

Materials rather than a formula:

| Part | Materials |
|---|---|
| 1 Scales | Calibration weights (e.g. 1 g, 10 g, 100 g, 500 g, 1000 g) **or** coins of known specified mass (look up your mint's published values; for example U.S. 5-cent 5.000 g, €1 7.50 g, €2 8.50 g) — 10–20 identical coins; optional: 500 mL volumetric flask and water |
| 2 Thermometers | Tall glass, crushed ice (about 300 g), cold water, a saucepan of water, your altitude (from a map or phone) |
| 3 Oven | Oven thermometer (and a leave-in probe thermometer if you have one); 6–9 slices of the same white sandwich bread (or enough to cover a rack); 3 small foil cups with 1 tsp granulated sugar each |

**Scale-up:** ×2 — repeat Part 3 with the fan (convection) mode; ×2.5 — map a second shelf position (lower third, used for stones in M03–M05); ×3 — repeat Part 1 on a friend's scale or at a second position on your bench to test the effect of the surface.

## Procedure

### Part 1 — Scales

1. Place the scale on the surface where you will always weigh. Check it is level (a glass of water shows tilt). Switch on with nothing on it.
2. **Accuracy and linearity.** Weigh each reference mass (or stack of coins) at several loads spanning the range you use: for the main scale ~10 g, ~100 g, ~500 g, ~1000 g (and higher if you have references); for the precision scale ~1 g, ~10 g, ~50 g. For coins, weigh 10 identical coins together and compare with 10 × the specified mass.
3. **Repeatability.** Weigh the same ~500 g reference five times, lifting it off and re-zeroing between readings.
4. **Off-centre (corner) test.** Weigh the same ~500 g reference at the centre and at each of the four corners of the platform.
5. **Creep test.** Place a bowl on the scale, tare, and add salt a pinch at a time, slowly. Note whether the display moves with each pinch or stays at 0 until a larger amount accumulates.
6. **Precision scale check.** Weigh a single coin 5 times; compute the mean and compare with specification.

### Part 2 — Thermometers

1. **Ice point.** Fill a tall glass to the top with crushed ice, then add cold water to just below the ice surface. Stir for 1 minute. Insert the probe into the centre so the tip is at least 5 cm deep and not touching the glass. Stir gently and wait until the reading is stable (30–60 s). Record. True value: 0.0 °C.
2. **Boiling point.** Bring a saucepan of water to a full rolling boil. Hold the probe tip 3–5 cm into the water, not touching the bottom or sides. Record the stable reading. Expected value: 100 °C at sea level, about 1 °C lower for every 300 m of altitude (for example ~98.3 °C at 500 m). Weather changes air pressure and can move the boiling point by a few tenths of a degree, so the ice point is the more reliable reference.
3. Repeat for every probe thermometer you own. If one has a calibration or offset function, set it using the ice point.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Steam from a rolling boil burns. Hold the probe with an oven glove or clip it to the pan; keep your hand out of the steam column.

</div>

### Part 3 — Oven map

**3a. Temperature over time.**

1. Put the oven thermometer in the centre of the middle shelf, facing the door window if you have one. If you have a leave-in probe, clip its tip in free air next to the oven thermometer, not touching metal.
2. Switch on to 180 °C (350 °F), conventional (top and bottom heat). Start a timer.
3. Read the temperature every 5 min for 60 min **without opening the door** (use the window and the oven light, or the leave-in probe). Note when the preheat light goes off.
4. Set 200 °C (390 °F); read every 5 min for 30 min. Then 230 °C (450 °F) for 30 min.
5. At the end of the 230 °C period, open the door for 30 s (as when loading), close it, and read every minute for 10 min to measure the **recovery**.

**3b. Hot spots: toast test.**

1. Let the oven cool to room temperature (or run this test on another day). Preheat to 180 °C conventional for 30 min.
2. Cover the middle shelf with slices of white sandwich bread, edge to edge (mark each slice's position with a number written on a paper plan).
3. Bake until the palest slices are light golden, about 8–15 min. Do not open the door to check before 8 min.
4. Remove, lay the slices out in their oven positions, and photograph them. Darker slices mark hotter zones.

**3c. Threshold check: sugar test (optional).**

1. Place three small foil cups, each with 1 tsp of granulated sugar, in the centre of the middle shelf.
2. Set 175 °C; after 20 min, look at the sugar. Then 195 °C; look again after 20 min.
3. Sucrose melts and begins to caramelise at around 185 °C in these conditions. Sugar that stays granular at a set 175 °C and melts at a set 195 °C is consistent with an accurate oven around that range. This is a rough cross-check only: the melting behaviour of sugar depends on heating rate and purity, so trust the thermometer over the sugar.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Melted sugar is far hotter than boiling water and sticks to skin. Let the foil cups cool completely before touching them.

</div>

## Analysis questions

1. Is your main scale's error significant for flour? For salt? What does that tell you about when to use each scale?
2. What offset will you apply to each thermometer? Is the offset the same at 0 °C and 100 °C? If not, what does that imply for readings at 25 °C and at 82 °C?
3. How long does your oven need to be stable at 200 °C, compared with the preheat light? What preheat time will you use from now on (and for a stone, which needs more)?
4. Is the oven offset the same at 180, 200 and 230 °C? What dial settings will you use to get a true 180, 200 and 230 °C?
5. Where are the hot spots? Which shelf position and tray orientation will you use for even baking, and when will you rotate?
6. From the recovery test, how much temperature do you lose when loading, and what could you do to reduce the loss?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Scales.** A decent kitchen scale is usually within ±1–2 g up to 1 kg and repeatable to ±1 g. Errors of that size are negligible for flour (< 0.5 %) and large for salt, yeast and leaveners — confirming the two-scale rule from 01.1. Many kitchen scales show **creep**: they ignore slow pinches because the auto-zero tracking treats them as drift. Corner errors of 1–3 g are common on cheap platforms. Coins weighed ten at a time usually agree with specification within a gram; circulated coins may be slightly light from wear.

**Thermometers.** Most digital probes read within ±0.5–1 °C at the ice point. The boiling reading is often a bit less exact (altitude, weather, probe depth). If the offsets at 0 °C and 100 °C are different, the error is not a simple offset; for readings in between you can assume it changes linearly between the two points. For bread-dough work (±1 °C matters) an offset of 1 °C is worth correcting; for custards near 82–84 °C it matters a lot.

**Oven.** Typical findings in home ovens:

- The preheat light goes off after 10–15 min, but the reading keeps climbing, overshoots, and settles only after about 20–30 min. The walls are still heating.
- The average differs from the dial by anywhere from 0 to 15–20 °C, often with different offsets at different settings.
- The swing is typically 10–30 °C peak to peak as the thermostat cycles.
- Opening the door for 30 s drops the air reading by 20–50 °C; recovery takes a few minutes and is faster in a well-soaked oven.
- The toast map usually shows darker slices at the back and along the side walls, sometimes at the front near the door seal on one side. The centre is usually most even.

The mechanism is heat transfer ([SC-10](stage-1/science/sc-10-heat-transfer.md)): the air heats fast but has little heat capacity; the walls store heat and radiate it; zones nearer the elements and walls receive more radiant and convective heat. That is why you soak the oven, bake on the most even shelf, rotate trays where the product allows, and trust your oven thermometer over the dial.

**Sugar test.** In an accurate oven the sugar usually stays granular at 175 °C and melts at 195 °C; if it melts at 175 °C the oven probably runs hot. Treat it only as a cross-check.

</details>

## Going further

- Map the fan (convection) mode at the same dial settings and compare with conventional; check whether the "15–20 °C lower" rule holds for your oven.
- Map the lower third of the oven with your baking stone in place (you will use it from M03), and measure the stone surface with an IR thermometer every 10 min during a 60-min preheat.
- Repeat the oven map in six months; thermostats drift.
