# X1-16 · Tangzhong vs none: softness and staling

## Question

Does cooking 7 % of the flour into a tangzhong, with the same total flour and water, make milk bread measurably softer, and does it keep it softer over three days? How much of any difference comes from the crumb holding more water, and how much from how the crumb firms over time?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write down, before you mix:

- which loaf you expect to compress more under the same load on day 0, day 1 and day 3, and whether you expect the gap to grow, shrink or stay the same;
- which loaf you expect to lose more weight in baking;
- which dough you expect to feel stickier during mixing and shaping, and why;
- whether you expect a difference in loaf volume.

Give a reason for each, using [06.2](stage-1/module-06/lesson-02.md).

## Procedure

**Day 0, morning (or the evening before): tangzhong for A.** Whisk 10 g flour into 50 g water and cook to 65–70 °C, stirring, until it thickens and holds trails ([R1-07](recipes/R1-07-milk-bread.md), Mise en place). Cool covered. **Weigh it** and add back any water lost, so it weighs 60 g. For B, weigh 50 g of water and leave it at the same temperature as the cooled tangzhong.

**Mixing (offset 20 min: A at t = 0, B at t = 20 min).** Use the same method for both: by hand, or in the stand mixer if it can handle a 300 g dough (many can't; hand is safer at this size).
1. Combine all ingredients except the butter. Mix to moderate development (hand: slap-and-fold 7 min).
2. Add the butter, and continue to a thin windowpane (hand: 6 min more). Use the same times for both, even if one dough seems ready sooner. Record stickiness (1–5) and the dough temperature.
3. Bulk in marked containers at 25–27 °C until **+75–100 %** (about 60–90 min). Record the time.

**Shaping.** Divide each dough into **2 × 150 g** (±1 g; weigh the leftover). Rest 10 min, then shape each piece as in R1-07 (roll to about 18 × 9 cm, fold to 7 cm wide, roll up) and place both cylinders in the pan. **Weigh the filled pan** minus the empty pan weight: this is the dough mass for bake loss.

**Proof** at 26–28 °C until the top is **1 cm below the rim** of each pan. The times may differ; record both. If one is ready 15 min or more earlier than the other, refrigerate it until the second is ready, and note it.

**Bake together.** 175 °C (350 °F), same rack, pans side by side with 5 cm between them, egg-washed identically. Bake to **90 °C internal** (about 25–30 min); if one reaches 90 °C first, remove it and note the time. Demould within 5 min and cool on a rack.

**Day 0 measurements (3 h after baking, fully cool).**
1. Weigh each loaf: this is *M₀*. Bake loss % = (dough mass − *M₀*) ÷ dough mass × 100.
2. Measure the height at the centre. Optional: volume by seed displacement, filling a box of known volume around the loaf with rapeseed or small lentils.
3. Cut off the end of each loaf (discard or taste it), then cut one **2.0 cm** slice from each, using a guide (two 2 cm strips of wood or stacked cardboard on either side of the loaf) so the thickness is identical. Weigh the slices removed.
4. **Compression test** on the centre of each slice: measure the height *h₀*, lay a flat lid about 6 cm in diameter on the crumb, place a **200 g** load on it (a small cup of coins, weighed) for **30 s**, measure the height under load *h₁*, remove the load, wait 30 s and measure *h₂*.
   - Compression % = (*h₀* − *h₁*) ÷ *h₀* × 100.
   - Recovery % = (*h₂* − *h₁*) ÷ (*h₀* − *h₁*) × 100.
   Repeat on a second point of the same slice, avoiding the first, and average.
5. Blind tasting: someone else labels the slices X and Y. Score softness, moistness and staleness (1–5).
6. Bag each loaf separately in identical zip bags, press out the air, and store them side by side at room temperature, away from the stove and sun. Record the room temperature.

**Day 1 (24 h) and day 3 (72 h).** Weigh each loaf before cutting (*Mₙ*). Cut and discard a thin (about 5 mm) slice to expose fresh crumb, weigh it, then cut the 2.0 cm test slice, weigh it, and repeat the compression test and tasting.

**Moisture loss in storage** = *M₀* − (*Mₙ* + total mass of all slices removed after the day 0 weighing and before this weighing). Express it as % of *M₀*.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot compression % against day for both loaves on one chart. Does the tangzhong loaf start softer, stay softer, or both? Does the gap grow, shrink or stay constant?
2. Compare storage moisture loss for the two loaves. If both lost less than about 1 % in the sealed bags but both firmed, what does that tell you about the main cause of firming?
3. Which dough felt stickier at the same true hydration, and what does that say about where the water is held?
4. Compare bake loss. Is a difference of 1 percentage point meaningful given your scale resolution? Estimate the uncertainty.
5. Did the blind tasting agree with the compression test? Where did they disagree, and which would you trust for a product decision?
6. What single change to the experiment would most improve the reliability of the compression measurement?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Handling.** The control dough (B) usually feels noticeably stickier and wetter than the tangzhong dough (A) at the same true hydration. In A, 50 g of the water is held by gelatinized starch and is not free to wet your hands and the bench.

**Bake loss and volume.** A usually has slightly lower bake loss, often by around 0.5–1.5 percentage points on a loaf this size, because gelatinized starch holds water more tightly. Volume is similar; A is sometimes slightly lower because 7 % of its flour went in as a paste that contributes no gluten. A clearly smaller A suggests an under-developed dough or a lumpy tangzhong.

**Softness.** On day 0, A usually compresses more (softer) and recovers well. Both loaves firm over three days: compression % falls and recovery falls. A typically stays softer at every time point, and the gap is usually visible to the fingers and in tasting by day 3. Whether the gap widens or stays about the same varies between bakes; don't over-interpret a single run.

**Why.** In sealed bags, both loaves lose little weight, usually well under 1–2 % over 3 days, so drying of the loaf as a whole is not the main cause of firming. The firming comes mostly from amylopectin retrogradation and water moving within the crumb and into the crust ([04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md), [Gray 2003]). A starts with more water held in the crumb and a softer starch phase, so it begins softer and takes longer to reach a given firmness. Present this as "starts softer and stays softer longer". This experiment cannot show whether the tangzhong also slows retrogradation itself; that would need an instrument such as a texture analyser or differential scanning calorimetry, which is a Stage 3 tool.

**Measurement quality.** With a ruler, 0.5 mm on a 20 mm slice is 2.5 % of height, so differences in compression smaller than about 5 percentage points are within noise. A caliper, more test points per slice, and repeating the whole experiment on another day improve this more than anything else.

</details>

## Going further

- Add a third variant: a 15 % **yudane** (22.5 g flour scalded with 22.5 g boiling water, the remaining water reduced accordingly) and compare it with A and B.
- Increase A's true hydration by 4 points (add 6 g water). Does the extra water the tangzhong "allows" give an even softer loaf without a handling penalty?
- Store a pair of slices from each loaf in the fridge from day 0 and test on day 1. Refrigeration speeds retrogradation; which loaf suffers more?
- Stage 3 direction: replace the coin load with a kitchen scale under the slice and a flat plunger pushed to a fixed depth, recording force. That is a crude texture analyser, and it gives you a force number rather than a height.
