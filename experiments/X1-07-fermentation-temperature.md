# X1-07 · Fermentation temperature

## Question

How does bulk fermentation temperature (about 18, 24 and 30 °C) change the time a lean dough takes to rise 75 %, and how does it change the flavour and crumb of the baked bread?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write down in your notebook, before mixing:

- Your predicted time to +75 % at each temperature. Use the rule of thumb from [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md) and show the calculation.
- Which loaf you expect to taste the most complex, which the most "yeasty", and why.
- How you expect the dough to *feel* at the end of bulk at each temperature.

## Variables

| Type | Variable | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | Bulk fermentation temperature (dough **and** environment) | A ≈ 18 °C · B ≈ 24 °C · C ≈ 30 °C |
| Dependent | Time to +75 % volume | Minutes, from a marked container |
| Dependent | Dough feel at the end of bulk | Strength, stickiness, smell |
| Dependent | Baked loaf | Height, width, crumb structure, crust colour, flavour (blind tasting) |
| Controlled | Formula | Identical (below), same flour/yeast lot |
| Controlled | Mixing | Same method and duration |
| Controlled | Folds | 2 sets, at 25 % and 50 % of *each variant's own* expected bulk time |
| Controlled | End point of bulk | +75 % volume, not a fixed time |
| Controlled | Shaping, proof, bake | Identical shape. All three proofed at ~24 °C to the same poke-test level. Same oven programme |

**Why the dough must match the chamber.** If you mix all three doughs at 24 °C and put one in an 18 °C spot, it spends its first hour cooling, and you are no longer testing 18 °C. Set the water temperature so each dough *starts* at its target (DDT method, [01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)).

## Batch and scale

Per variant (identical for A, B and C):

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100 | 200 |
| Water | 68 | 136 |
| Salt | 2 | 4 |
| Instant yeast | 1.0 | 2.0 |
| **Total** | **171** | **342** |

**Total for the experiment:** 600 g flour, 408 g water, 12 g salt, 6 g instant yeast. Mix three separate doughs, not one divided dough, because each needs its own water temperature.
**Scale-up (×2.5 per variant = one R1-01-size loaf):** 500 / 340 / 10 / 5 g = 855 g.

**Water temperature per variant** (flour 21 °C, room 22 °C, hand friction 2 °C, three-factor method: water = 3 × DDT − flour − room − friction):

| Variant | Target dough °C | Water °C (example) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 18 | 54 − 45 = **9 °C** (fridge-cold water) |
| B | 24 | 72 − 45 = **27 °C** |
| C | 30 | 90 − 45 = **45 °C** — see warning |

<div class="callout warn">

**Watch out:** Water at 45 °C poured directly onto instant yeast can stress it. For variant C, mix the yeast into the flour first so the yeast meets the water already diluted by flour. The mixed dough is at 30 °C, which is well below the lethal range. If your calculated water exceeds 45 °C, instead mix C at ~26–27 °C and let it warm in the 30 °C chamber for 15 min before marking t = 0.

</div>

## Creating three temperatures at home

| Target | Options | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| ~18 °C | A cool room or cellar in winter. A wine fridge. An insulated cooler with a frozen gel pack wrapped in a towel (swap the pack every 1–2 h). **Fridge door on the warmest setting** in some fridges (measure first; many run 6–8 °C, which is too cold) | Thermometer beside the container. Aim for ±1.5 °C |
| ~24 °C | Most kitchens, or a cupboard | Same |
| ~30 °C | **Oven off with only the light on** (typically 28–35 °C: measure, and prop the door open a crack if too warm). A **cooler with a jug of ~40 °C water**, refreshed hourly. A proofing box | Same. Label the oven "IN USE" so nobody preheats it |

Leave a thermometer in each spot for an hour before the experiment and log what it reads.

## Procedure

1. **The day before:** test and log each location for 1–2 h.
2. **Stagger the mixes** so the long variant doesn't hold up the day. Mix **A first**, B about 1.5 h later, and C about 30 min after B. The loaves will then finish bulk within roughly an hour of each other.
3. **Mix each dough** identically: combine the flour and yeast, add the water minus 5 g, and mix to shaggy. Rest 15 min. Add the salt and the remaining 5 g water. Squeeze and fold for 4 min.
4. Measure the dough temperature (target ±1 °C). Place the dough in a **1 L straight-sided clear container**, level the top, and mark it. Also mark the **+75 % line**: measure the dough height h₀ and draw a line at 1.75 × h₀. Label the lid with the variant letter, temperature and time.
5. Put each container in its chamber. **t = 0.**
6. **Folds:** 2 sets. A at ~50 and ~100 min, B at ~25 and ~50 min, C at ~15 and ~30 min. Fold gently and relevel each time.
7. **Observe every 15 min** (every 30 min for A): height as a % of h₀, the dough temperature at the centre (a quick probe), and the smell.
8. When a dough touches the +75 % line, record the time. **Pre-shape** it into a round, bench-rest it 20 min at room temperature, then shape a small boule and place it in a floured bowl or small banneton.
9. **Proof all three at ~24 °C** until each shows a slow, partial poke-test spring-back (typically 35–60 min). Keeping proof conditions the same keeps the comparison about bulk.
10. **Bake** each boule when ready, in a preheated covered pot at 250 °C (480 °F) for 15 min, then uncovered at 230 °C (445 °F) for 10–15 min, to ≥96 °C internal. Give each loaf the same single cross-score. If the timings collide, the later dough can wait 10–15 min in the fridge. Record any such delay.
11. Cool for 2 h. Weigh each loaf and measure its height and width. Cut through the centre and photograph the three crumbs side by side with a ruler.
12. **Blind tasting:** have someone else label the slices 1, 2, 3. Rate each for aroma intensity, wheat/sweet, "yeasty", acidity, and overall preference.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot the time to +75 % against dough temperature. Does the "doubling per 8–10 °C" rule hold for your data? Calculate the factor between A and B, and between B and C.
2. How close were your predictions? Where were they off, and why? Consider how well each dough held its temperature.
3. Which dough was strongest and which slackest at the end of bulk? Link this to what enzymes and acids were doing over that time.
4. Did the crumb differ in openness or evenness? Did the crust colour differ?
5. Given your results, what bulk temperature and yeast % would you choose for flavour on a normal working day?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Timing.** With 1 % instant yeast, typical results are: **C (30 °C) ~60–80 min, B (24 °C) ~90–120 min, A (18 °C) ~3–4 h.** The B→C step (6 °C) usually gives a factor of ~1.4–1.6 and the A→B step (6 °C) ~1.6–2. That is consistent with roughly doubling per 8–10 °C, and slightly steeper at the cool end. Your numbers will differ with your flour and yeast. The *ratios* are the result.

**Dough.** C tends to feel slacker and stickier at +75 %: enzyme activity and the fast gas production stretch the network before the gluten has matured. A feels the strongest and most extensible, with a smooth, satiny surface.

**Crumb and volume.** The differences are usually modest, since all three were proofed and baked alike. C may show slightly larger, less regular cells and a lower, wider loaf. A may give a more even, glossy crumb.

**Crust.** A often browns slightly better. This is not about sugar consumed in total: all three made roughly the same gas. The longer time let amylases produce and keep more sugar, and allowed more protein breakdown for Maillard reactions.

**Flavour.** Most tasters rank A as the most aromatic and wheaty, sometimes with faint acidity. C tastes the blandest or yeastiest. B sits between. This is the main lesson: **gas is the same, flavour is not**. Time, not volume, builds flavour. It is the reason for low-yeast, cool fermentation and for preferments ([05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md)).

**If your results disagree.** Check the chamber logs. A cooler that warmed to 21 °C, or an oven that climbed to 36 °C, explains most surprises.

</details>
