# X1-37 · Choux paste: egg quantity and paste consistency

## Question

With the panade held identical, how does the amount of egg added (20 % under the V-test endpoint, at the endpoint, and 20 % over it) change the paste's consistency, and the height, shape, cavity and cracking of the baked choux?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

In your notebook, before you mix, write down:

- Which paste will spread most in the 10 minutes between piping and baking? By how much will a 3.5 cm blob widen?
- Rank the three variants by baked height, and separately by baked diameter. Are the two rankings the same?
- Which will have the largest cavity and the thinnest walls? Which will have the most irregular cracks?
- Which, if any, will lose height as it cools? Why?
- Is "more egg means more steam, so a bigger rise" true? Up to what point?

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Egg added to one third of a common panade: **A** stiff = 0.8 × E; **B** correct = E (the amount that just passes the V test); **C** slack = 1.2 × E |
| **Dependent** | Paste consistency (V-test tail length, trench closing time, gloss); slump of a piped blob before baking; baked height, diameter and height ÷ diameter; collapse after 30 min; bake loss; crack pattern; cavity and wall thickness; interior moistness; éclair shape |
| **Controlled** | One panade (same ingredients, boil, drying), split by weight; same beaten egg mix; paste temperature at egg addition (50–60 °C, recorded); mixing method and time per addition; piece weight (15 g balls, 35 g éclairs); piping tip; egg wash (none); tray, parchment, layout; one oven, one bake, one profile; all pieces removed at the same time; cooling on the same rack |

**Egg per variant** (E is found in the experiment; the R1-31 ratio predicts about 85–95 g per third):

| Variant | Egg | If E = 90 g | Egg as % of the portion's flour (if E = 90 g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A stiff | 0.8 × E | 72 g | 144 % |
| B correct | E | 90 g | 180 % |
| C slack | 1.2 × E | 108 g | 216 % |

Beat **6 large eggs** (about 300 g) together, strain, and weigh. All three variants use this one mix.

**Scale-up ×2.5** (375 g flour panade, each third 125 g flour, for full-size éclairs from each variant): 337.5 g water, 337.5 g milk, 300 g butter, 12.5 g salt, 12.5 g sugar, 375 g flour (1,375 g); eggs about 2.5 × 270 = 675 g (13–14 large eggs). Use a stand mixer for each portion in turn.

## Procedure

**Set-up (15 min)**

1. Preheat the oven to **200 °C (390 °F)** conventional (or **180 °C** fan), middle shelf, oven thermometer in place, for 30 min.
2. Prepare one tray (about 30 × 40 cm) with parchment. On the underside, draw 15 circles of 3.5 cm in 5 rows of 3, about 6 cm apart, and three 10 cm lines for éclairs along one long edge. Label each position with its variant so that each row holds A, B and C in a different order: row 1 A-B-C, row 2 B-C-A, row 3 C-A-B, row 4 A-B-C, row 5 B-C-A; éclairs A, B, C. This spreads each variant across the tray's hot and cool zones.
3. Label three bowls A, B, C and tare them. Have three piping bags with 12 mm plain tips, labelled.

**Panade (10 min)**

4. Make the panade as in [R1-31](recipes/R1-31-pate-a-choux.md) steps 1–4: butter fully melted at a rolling boil; flour all at once; dry 1.5–2 min on medium heat to a film and 75–80 °C. Weigh and record the loss.
5. **Divide** the panade by weight into the three bowls (total ÷ 3, ±2 g). Cover A and C with film in contact.

**Eggs (15 min, work quickly)**

6. **B first.** When B is at **50–60 °C**, add the egg in 4–5 additions, beating each in with a sturdy spatula (or hand mixer on low) until the paste comes back together. From about 70 g on, add 5 g at a time and do the V test after each. Stop at the first addition where the paste leaves a **3–5 cm V tail that holds** and a trench closes slowly. Record **E** (grams of egg in B) and the paste temperature.
7. **A:** calculate 0.8 × E, weigh it, and beat it into A in 3–4 additions the same way. Record the temperature at the first addition (aim 45–60 °C).
8. **C:** calculate 1.2 × E and beat it into C in 4–5 additions. Record the temperature.
9. **Describe each paste** (see Measurements): V-test tail, trench closing time, gloss. Photograph the three V tails side by side.

**Piping and baking (45 min)**

10. Bag each paste. Pipe **5 balls of 15 g** per variant on their labelled circles (weigh the first ball of each on the scale; aim ±1 g), and **one éclair of 10 cm** per variant (weigh it; aim 35 g). Do not egg wash. Do not tap down points: tails and points are part of the result. Note the time the last piece is piped.
11. **Slump.** Immediately after piping, measure the diameter of one ball per variant (the one in row 3). Measure again **10 min** later, just before baking.
12. **Bake** 10 min after the last piece was piped: **200 °C for 15 min, then 170 °C for 15–20 min** conventional, or **180 °C fan for 28–32 min**. **Do not open the door for 20 min.** Watch through the glass: note at what time each variant stops rising, and take a photo through the glass every 5 min if you can.
13. **Remove all pieces together** when **B** is done (deep golden, rigid, colour in the cracks). Record the total time.
14. **Measure straight away:** height and diameter of three balls per variant (rows 1, 2, 4). Weigh all five balls of each variant together (bake loss). Pierce nothing: collapse is a dependent variable here.
15. **After 30 min** on the rack: measure the same three balls again. Then cut two balls per variant vertically in half and the éclairs lengthwise. Photograph the sections side by side with a ruler.

## Analysis questions

1. What was E as a percentage of the portion's flour? Compare it with R1-31's 157–193 % range and with your panade loss. If you dried the panade longer next time, which way would E move?
2. Plot height, diameter and cavity against egg % of flour. Is any of them monotonic (always increasing or decreasing)? Where is the maximum volume?
3. Which variant slumped most before baking? How does slump relate to its baked height ÷ diameter?
4. Relate each variant's crack score to how extensible its paste was. Why does a stiff paste burst rather than crack finely?
5. Did any variant collapse after cooling? Was it wetter inside? Connect the two.
6. Your baker's instinct says "a bit more egg is safer than too little". Using your data, when is that true and when is it not?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Paste.** A is dull and short: it falls from the spatula in a lump or clings with a stub of a tail, the trench stays open (over 30–60 s), and it pipes with a rough, torn surface and visible tails. B is satiny and leaves a 3–5 cm V tail that holds; the trench closes in several seconds. C is wet-shiny, its tail stretches long and drips, and the trench closes almost at once.

**Slump.** A barely changes (0–5 %). B widens a little (typically 5–10 %). C spreads noticeably (often 15–30 %); a 35 mm blob can become 42–45 mm before it reaches the oven.

**Rise and shape.** B gives the greatest volume: tall, round balls (height ÷ diameter roughly 0.7–0.85) with fine, even cracks. A rises less in volume and often keeps a fairly tall profile, because it holds its piped shape, but with irregular, jagged cracks or a burst top, points and tails that brown early, and a rough, lopsided look; its cavity is smaller and the walls thicker. C rises outward rather than up: wider, lower pieces (height ÷ diameter often around 0.5 or less), with a smooth or lightly cracked top, sometimes a pale, soft base, and a greater tendency to sink on cooling. Its éclair is flat and wide, with bulbous ends. Ranking by height is usually B > A > C; by diameter C > B > A.

**Cavity and interior.** B: large cavity, walls 2–4 mm, dry membrane. A: smaller cavity, thicker walls, sometimes a web of dense paste in the middle. C: a cavity that may be large but flattened, with a moist layer or a wet centre if the pieces were removed when B was done, because C's walls are thicker at the base and it holds more water.

**Collapse and bake loss.** C collapses most (often 10–25 %), because the wetter, softer walls are still unset or undried when B is done. A usually shows a slightly lower bake loss than B (it had less water to lose) C may show a similar or higher bake loss and still be wetter inside, because it started with more water. B and A usually hold their height.

**Why.** Egg is both the source of extra water (steam) and protein (the set), and it controls the paste's extensibility. Too little and the paste cannot stretch: steam breaks through the stiffest point, giving bursts and a small, thick-walled cavity. The right amount gives a paste extensible enough to inflate evenly and firm enough to hold its shape until the proteins set. Too much and the paste flows before it sets; the extra water needs longer to evaporate, so at a fixed bake time the shell is under-dried and sinks. "More egg, more rise" is true only up to the V-test endpoint; past it, shape and set are lost faster than steam is gained.

**If your results differ.** If A rose as well as B, your panade was probably very dry and E is high; the 20 % reduction left A near the stiff edge of acceptable. If C did not spread, check that its paste temperature was not much lower than B's (cold paste is stiffer). If all three collapsed, the bake was too short for your oven; repeat with 5–10 min more at 170 °C.

</details>

## Going further

- **Finer series:** 0.9 E, E, 1.1 E. Is a 10 % error visible? This tells you how tight your tolerance on egg really is.
- **Panade drying × egg:** dry one panade 30 s and one 3 min, egg each to the V test, and compare E. This connects the two variables that set every choux paste.
- **Egg composition:** make B with whole egg, with whole egg plus 10 % extra yolk, and with whole egg plus 10 % extra white (each to the V test). How do E, colour and crispness change?
- **Stage 2** applies the same endpoint judgement to production pastes made in a mixer at 1–5 kg; **Stage 3** replaces the V test with a measured rheology specification (line-spread or a simple penetrometer reading) and explores egg replacement.
