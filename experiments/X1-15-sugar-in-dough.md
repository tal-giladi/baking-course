# X1-15 · Sugar in yeast dough: 0 / 5 / 10 / 20 %

## Question

How does the amount of sugar in an otherwise constant yeast dough change the fermentation rate, the crust colour, and the softness and tenderness of the crumb? At what sugar level does sugar stop helping the yeast and start slowing it, and how much of that slowdown does osmotolerant yeast recover?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

In your lab notebook, before mixing anything, write:

- your predicted time to +75 % rise for each variant, as a multiple of the 0 % time (for example "5 %: 0.9×");
- the sugar level at which you expect the crumb to become noticeably softer, and the level at which you expect the rolls to become noticeably darker;
- if you are running the fifth variant, how much faster you expect osmotolerant yeast to be at 20 % sugar than standard instant yeast.

Give a one-line reason for each prediction, using [06.1](stage-1/module-06/lesson-01.md).

## Procedure

**Preparation.**
1. Label four (or five) bowls, four (or five) identical straight-sided jars or clear cups (about 400–500 ml, with vertical walls), and a parchment-lined tray divided into labelled sections.
2. Compute the water temperature for a 26 °C dough ([01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)); use the same water temperature for all variants.
3. Weigh each variant's flour, sugar, salt and yeast into its bowl. Weigh the yeast on a 0.1 g scale. Stir the dry ingredients in each bowl so the yeast is not sitting in a pile of sugar.

**Staggered mixing (offset 10 min per variant).** Start variant 0 % at t = 0, 5 % at t = 10 min, 10 % at t = 20 min, 20 % at t = 30 min, 20 % gold at t = 40 min. All later times for each variant are measured from its own mixing start, so the variants can be compared directly.

For each variant:
1. Add 124 g water and mix by hand until no dry flour remains (1 min).
2. Knead on the bench with slap-and-fold for exactly **5 min**. Do not add flour. Record stickiness and softness (1 = stiff and dry, 5 = very soft and sticky).
3. Record the dough temperature. Accept 25–27 °C.
4. **Jar sample:** weigh **100 g** into the jar, press it flat with a wet finger, and mark the level on the outside with tape. Measure the starting height *h₀* with a ruler from the base. Mark **+75 %** at 1.75 × *h₀* and **+100 %** at 2 × *h₀*. Cover.
5. Round the rest of the dough and place it in its bowl, covered.
6. Put the jar and the bowl in the fermentation spot, with a thermometer.

**Rise readings.** Every **15 min**, read each jar's height and record it. Note the time when each jar reaches the +75 % mark and the +100 % mark. Stop recording a variant at +100 % or after 4 h, whichever is first.

**Rolls.** When a variant's jar reaches **+75 %**:
1. Divide that variant's bowl dough into **3 × 70 g**, round tightly, and place on its labelled section of the tray. (Cover the tray loosely.)
2. Proof until the rolls have grown about 75 % (judge against a 70 g ball you shaped at the same time and flattened as a reference, or by a slow, partial spring-back to a poke). Record the proof time.
3. Bake each set when it is ready. Preheat to **200 °C (390 °F)** and bake **14 min**, always in the centre of the same rack. This fixed time and temperature is what makes the colours comparable. Record the internal temperature of one roll per set at the end.

If two sets are ready together, bake them together, one on each side of the tray, and swap sides for a second comparison on another day if you can.

**Cooling and evaluation.** Cool all rolls for at least 1 h. Evaluate them side by side on the same day, under the same light.

**Safety:** 200 °C oven: use dry gloves. Don't taste raw dough (raw flour).

## Analysis questions

1. Plot % rise against time for all variants on one chart. Then plot time to +75 % against sugar %. Where is the minimum, and where does the curve start rising steeply?
2. Express each time to +75 % as a multiple of the 0 % time. How did your results compare with your hypotheses?
3. Compute the sugar concentration in the water phase for each variant: sugar ÷ (sugar + 124). Does fermentation time track that number more smoothly than sugar % of flour? (Here the two are proportional, so think about what would change if hydration differed.)
4. By how much did osmotolerant yeast shorten the 20 % time? What yeast type and dose would you now choose for a 15 % sugar bun dough that must be ready in 2 h?
5. The 20 % dough was probably the softest and stickiest even though all variants had the same water. Explain why.
6. All rolls were baked for 14 min at 200 °C. Rank the colours, and explain the ranking using [04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md). What bake settings would you use for the 20 % roll in practice?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Fermentation.** The 5 % dough usually rises as fast as the 0 % dough, or slightly faster early on, because the yeast's invertase gives it glucose and fructose immediately. The 10 % dough is somewhat slower, typically around 1.1–1.5× the 0 % time. The 20 % dough is much slower, often 2–3× the 0 % time with standard instant yeast, and it may struggle to reach +100 % within 4 h. The osmotolerant yeast at 20 % recovers a large part of the gap, but is usually still somewhat slower than the 0–5 % doughs. Your exact multiples depend on your yeast, flour and temperature. The shape of the curve (flat or slightly dipping to ~5 %, then rising increasingly steeply) is what should be reproducible. The explanation is osmotic stress: dissolved sugar lowers the water activity of the dough water, and yeast cells lose water and slow down. Glucose and fructose from inverted sucrose add to the load, which is one reason osmotolerant strains with low invertase activity do better.

**Dough feel.** Softness and stickiness increase with sugar at constant water. Sugar dissolves into the water and increases the volume of the liquid phase, and it competes with the gluten proteins for water, so the gluten develops less in the same 5 min kneading. At 20 % you can feel the dough is weaker.

**Colour.** At a fixed 14 min at 200 °C, the rolls range from pale gold (0 %) to deep brown (20 %). The 0 % roll has almost no residual sugar at the surface, because the yeast used what the flour offered; the sweet rolls carry unfermented sugar into the oven, which drives Maillard browning and caramelization. In practice you would bake the 20 % rolls at 175–185 °C for longer, to the internal temperature, rather than at 200 °C.

**Crumb and taste.** Softness and tenderness usually increase from 0 to 10 %, as sugar holds water and delays gelatinization and gluten setting. The 20 % roll is the most tender, but it may be smaller and slightly denser if proof and oven spring were weaker, and it is clearly sweet. The 5 % roll is barely sweet; most tasters notice sweetness from 10 %.

**If your results differ:** a 0 % dough that was slower than 5 % by a large margin, or a 20 % dough that rose as fast as 0 %, usually means a temperature difference between jars, a yeast weighing error (0.2 g on 2 g is 10 %), or a jar that wasn't straight-sided.

</details>

## Going further

- Repeat the 20 % variant with 2 % standard instant yeast, and compare with the 1 % osmotolerant result: which is the cheaper fix, dose or strain?
- Hold sugar at 20 % and move part of it into a sponge stage (add it after 60 min). Does the yeast get a head start?
- Replace sucrose with honey at equal sugar solids (about 1.2 × the weight), and compare rise and colour. Glucose and fructose are more osmotically active per gram than sucrose; predict first.
- Stage 3 direction: fit a simple model of rise time vs water-phase sugar concentration, and use it to predict a new sweet-dough formula before you bake it.
