# X1-14 · Levain: temperature and feeding ratio, tracked by rise and pH

## Question

How do feeding ratio (1:2:2 vs 1:5:5) and temperature (~21 °C vs ~28 °C) change your starter's time to peak, maximum rise, acidity at peak and smell, and do the two factors interact?

This is a **2 × 2 factorial design**: two factors, two levels each, four combinations run at the same time from the same starter. If you have only one warm spot, run only one factor (ratio at your room temperature, or temperature at 1:2:2) and say so in your notes.

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Before feeding anything, write in your lab notebook:

- Your predicted time to peak for each of the four jars, in hours.
- Which jar will rise highest, and why.
- Which jar will be most acidic at its peak, and why.
- How the smell will differ between 21 °C and 28 °C.
- Whether you expect the effect of ratio to be the same size at both temperatures (in hours, and as a ratio of times).

Base each prediction on [05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md).

## Variables

| Type | Variable | Levels / how controlled |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent** | Feeding ratio (starter : flour : water) | 1:2:2 · 1:5:5 |
| | Culture temperature | ~21 °C · ~28 °C (each ± 1 °C, measured next to the jars) |
| **Dependent** | Time to peak | Hours from feeding to maximum height |
| | Maximum rise | % height increase at peak |
| | Acidity at peak | pH (calibrated meter), optional |
| | Smell | Descriptors at 2 h, at peak, and 4 h after peak |
| **Controlled** | Seed starter | One ripe starter at its peak, same jar, stirred before sampling |
| | Flour | Same bag, same blend (90 % bread flour, 10 % wholemeal as in R1-05) |
| | Hydration | 100 % in all jars |
| | Water temperature | Adjusted so each mix starts at its target temperature |
| | Jars | Four identical straight-sided glass jars, 250–350 ml |
| | Mixing | Same spatula, same 30 s stir, surface levelled, sides scraped |
| | Start time | All fed within 10 min |

| B | 1:5:5 | 21 °C | 7 | 35 (31.5 bread + 3.5 wholemeal) | 35 | 77 |
| C | 1:2:2 | 28 °C | 15 | 30 (27 + 3) | 30 | 75 |
| D | 1:5:5 | 28 °C | 7 | 35 (31.5 + 3.5) | 35 | 77 |
| **Total** | | | **44** | **130** | **130** | **304** |

The totals are close (75 and 77 g) so that the paste starts at nearly the same height in every jar; rise % corrects for the small difference. Weigh on a 0.1 g scale if you have one; 7 g on a 1 g scale has a ±7 % error.

**Optional replication:** run two jars per condition (eight jars, 88 g starter). Differences between replicates tell you how much of any difference between conditions is real.

**Scale-up ×2.5:** the same ratios at 2.5× (1:2:2 = 37.5 + 75 + 75 g; 1:5:5 = 17.5 + 87.5 + 87.5 g) give a 190 g levain, enough for R1-06 with some to spare. Use your results to choose the build that peaks when you want to mix.

## Procedure

1. **Two days before:** refresh the starter twice a day at 1:2:2 and 24–26 °C so it is vigorous and on schedule ([R1-05](recipes/R1-05-levain.md)).
2. **Set up the two temperature zones and measure them for a few hours before starting.** ~21 °C: a quiet corner of the kitchen, or a cooler with a cool pack. ~28 °C: an oven with only the light on (door ajar if it overshoots), a proofing box, or a cooler with a jar of 35–40 °C water changed every few hours. Put a thermometer in each zone next to where the jars will sit.
3. **Label** four jars A–D and weigh each empty.
4. **At the starter's peak (07:00 is a good start time),** stir the starter and weigh the seeds into the jars. Add water at the right temperature (about 21 °C for A and B, about 30 °C for C and D), stir to disperse, add flour, and mix for 30 s until uniform. Scrape the sides and level the surface.
5. **Mark and measure the start height**: rubber band at the surface; with a ruler held against the outside of the glass, measure the paste height from the inside bottom of the jar to the top of the paste (h₀). Record the time.
6. **Place** A and B in the 21 °C zone and C and D in the 28 °C zone. Lids on loosely.
7. **Measure the height every hour.** When a jar has slowed down or domed, measure every 30 min. The **peak** is the maximum height before it starts to fall; record the time of the highest reading. A phone taking a photo every 15 min in front of the jars with a ruler in view makes this much easier.
8. **At each jar's peak:** if you have a meter, take the pH. Calibrate the meter first with pH 7 and pH 4 buffers at room temperature. Stir the jar, then insert the probe into the paste (or mix 5 g of culture with 20 g distilled water and measure the slurry, the same way every time). Rinse the probe between jars. Record smell descriptors.
9. **Smell** each jar at 2 h, at its peak, and 4 h after its peak. Use descriptors: flour, yogurt, milk, green apple, fruit, beer, wine, vinegar, cheese, acetone/nail polish, sharp, mild.
10. **Watch the fall.** Note roughly how long each jar holds near its peak before falling noticeably. That is its tolerance window.
11. **Discard** all four jars at the end. Do not return experiment jars to the mother starter.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Do not taste raw starter ([FDA-flour]). If an oven is your warm zone, put a note on the door and switch nothing on: someone preheating it will destroy your jars and possibly your thermometer. A heat mat must not be left on under a sealed cooler.

</div>

## Analysis questions

1. Plot rise % against time for all four jars on one chart. Describe the shape of each curve (lag, rise, peak, fall).
2. **Main effect of ratio** on time to peak: [(B − A) + (D − C)] ÷ 2. **Main effect of temperature**: [(C − A) + (D − B)] ÷ 2. Which factor had the larger effect in hours?
3. **Interaction:** compare (B − A) with (D − C). Is the ratio effect the same size at both temperatures? Now compare the *ratios* B/A and D/C. Which description, additive in hours or multiplicative, fits your data better?
4. From the temperature effect, calculate your culture's "doubling per °C": if the time at 21 °C is t₂₁ and at 28 °C is t₂₈, the rate doubles every 7 × ln 2 ÷ ln(t₂₁/t₂₈) °C. Compare this with the ~8–10 °C rule for baker's yeast from [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md).
5. Which jar was most acidic at peak? Explain using carried-over acid and dilution.
6. How did smell differ between the temperature zones, and between the ratios? Does this match the practitioner guidance in [05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md)?
7. Using your data, write a feeding instruction that makes your levain peak at 07:00 if fed at 22:00 in a 21 °C kitchen.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

Typical results for a vigorous white-flour starter at 100 % hydration; your culture will differ, and that is the point of measuring it.

**Time to peak.** Roughly: C (1:2:2, 28 °C) 3–5 h; D (1:5:5, 28 °C) 6–8 h; A (1:2:2, 21 °C) 6–9 h; B (1:5:5, 21 °C) 10–14 h. Both factors matter, and a 7 °C change is often about as powerful as a 2.5× change in dilution.

**Interaction.** In hours, the ratio effect is larger at 21 °C than at 28 °C (for example +5 h against +3 h), which looks like an interaction. As a *ratio of times*, it is often similar at both temperatures (about 1.5–2× longer for 1:5:5). That is what you expect from a growth process: temperature multiplies the growth rate, and dilution adds a roughly fixed number of population doublings. Engineers will recognise this as a system that is additive in log-time; analysing log(time) often removes most of the apparent interaction.

**Doubling per °C.** Many starters give a rate doubling somewhere around every 6–10 °C over this range, comparable to or a little steeper than baker's yeast.

**Maximum rise.** The 1:5:5 jars often rise higher (+150–250 %) than 1:2:2 jars (+100–150 %), because more fresh flour means more food and more fresh gluten to hold gas, and less acid weakening the structure at the start. The warm jars sometimes peak a little lower and fall faster than the cool ones.

**Acidity.** 1:2:2 jars usually read lower (more acidic) at peak, around pH 3.7–4.0, than 1:5:5 jars, around 3.9–4.3, because a higher inoculation carries more of the previous cycle's acid and starts at a lower pH. Temperature affects pH at peak less than expected, because the warm jars reach peak sooner; the difference shows more if you measure 4 h after peak.

**Smell.** Warm jars at peak typically smell milder, of yogurt, milk and fruit. Cool jars smell sharper and more vinegary, and the 1:2:2 jars sharper than the 1:5:5 jars. After the peak, all of them turn sharper; the warm 1:2:2 jar may reach an acetone or solvent note within a few hours, a sign of a starving culture. This pattern supports the practitioner guidance that warmer, more dilute cultures tend to be milder and more lactic, and cooler ones more acetic, but it is your nose, not a lab measurement.

**Tolerance window.** 1:5:5 cultures usually hold near peak longer than 1:2:2, which makes them easier to schedule overnight.

</details>
