# X1-23 · Fat temperature and piece size in scones

## Question

With the same scone formula and the same handling, how does the temperature and physical form of the butter — frozen and grated, cold and cubed, soft, or melted — change dough temperature, rise, spread, flakiness and tenderness?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Rank the four variants by baked height, tallest first.
- Which will be the flakiest? Which the most tender? Are they the same variant? Why or why not?
- Predict the dough temperature after mixing for each variant.
- What do you expect to see on a sheet of paper towel placed under each scone after baking?

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Butter state when added to flour: **G** frozen (−18 °C) and coarsely grated, tossed in, not rubbed · **C** cold 1 cm cubes (4–7 °C) rubbed to crumbs + pea-size pieces · **S** soft (20–22 °C), rubbed in completely to fine sand · **M** (optional) melted and cooled to 35–40 °C, stirred in with a fork |
| **Dependent** | Dough temperature after mixing; raw and baked height; rise ratio; width change (spread); layers when split; flakiness; tenderness; greasiness; crust colour |
| **Controlled** | Same formula and ingredients; same milk (from the fridge, 4–8 °C); same liquid mixing time (20 s); same 2 folds; same thickness (2.5 cm); same cutting (knife, straight down, 4 squares); same tray, rest and oven; same egg wash on tops only; one person does all handling |

## Batch and scale

**Per variant** (R1-14 formula at 160 g flour):

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|

| Unsalted butter (state per variant) | 22.5 | 36.0 |

| **Total** | **196.0** | **313.6** |

**Per-variant yield:** ~313 g dough, patted to a 10.5 × 10.5 cm square 2.5 cm thick and cut into **4 squares of ~75 g**. No scraps, no reroll.

**Total ingredients for 4 variants:** flour 640 g, butter 144 g (weigh four 36 g portions), sugar 80 g, baking powder 32.0 g, salt 6.4 g, milk 352 g. Egg wash: 1 egg + pinch of salt.

**Scale ×2.5:** 400 g flour per variant = the full [R1-14](recipes/R1-14-scones.md) batch (90 g butter, 50 g sugar, 20 g powder, 4 g salt, 220 g milk). Use it if you want to cut rounds and test rerolled scraps as a fifth variable.

## Procedure

**Preparation (the day before or ≥ 2 h ahead):**

1. **G:** wrap a 36 g piece of butter and freeze it ≥ 2 h.
2. **C:** cut 36 g butter into 1 cm cubes and refrigerate ≥ 30 min.
3. **S:** leave 36 g butter at room temperature until it probes **20–22 °C** (1–2 h, or slices for 30–45 min).
4. **M:** melt 36 g butter and cool it to **35–40 °C** just before use.
5. Weigh four identical dry mixes (flour, sugar, powder sifted in, salt) into four same-size bowls labelled G, C, S, M. Refrigerate the four portions of milk (88 g each) in labelled cups.
6. Preheat the oven to **220 °C (425 °F)** for ≥ 25 min, rack in the middle, oven thermometer in place. Line one light-coloured tray with parchment and draw four labelled zones.

**Mixing order: M → S → C → G.** Each variant is prepared start to finish before the next, about 5 min each. Doing the warm variants first means they chill slightly longer in step 13, which works *against* the difference you are looking for: any difference you still see is real.

7. **Fat in:**
   - **M:** pour the melted butter over the dry mix and stir with a fork for 30 s until evenly crumbly.
   - **S:** rub the soft butter in with fingertips until no butter is visible and the mix feels like damp sand (~2 min).
   - **C:** rub the cold cubes in to coarse crumbs with some pea-size pieces (~2 min), as R1-14 step 1.
   - **G:** grate the frozen butter on the coarse side of a box grater directly into the bowl, tossing the shreds with flour every few seconds. Toss to coat; **do not rub**.
   Photograph each mixture next to a ruler.
8. **Probe** the fat–flour mixture temperature.
9. **Liquid:** add the 88 g cold milk; mix with a table knife for **20 s** exactly.
10. **Probe the dough temperature** (centre of the mass). Record it.
11. **Fold:** tip onto a lightly floured bench; pat to ~2 cm, fold in half; pat, fold again (**2 folds**). Pat to a **10.5 × 10.5 cm square, 2.5 cm thick** (use a ruler).
12. **Cut** into 4 squares with a floured knife, pressing straight down. Measure the raw height of each to the nearest mm. Place in the variant's zone, ~2 cm apart, cut sides facing outward the same way for all variants.
13. **Rest:** when all four variants are on the tray, refrigerate the tray **15 min**. Brush the tops (not the sides) with egg wash.
14. **Bake** together at **220 °C for 13–15 min**, rotating the tray at 8 min. All scones should reach 93–97 °C inside; probe one per variant.
15. **Cool** 10 min on a rack, one variant per sheet of paper towel (for greasiness), then measure.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot rise ratio against dough temperature after mixing. Describe the relationship.
2. Which variant was flakiest and which was most tender? Explain why these are different variants using the two fat populations in [08.4](stage-1/module-08/lesson-04.md).
3. The S and M variants were chilled for the same 15 min as G and C. Why didn't chilling restore their rise?
4. Which variant left the largest grease mark? What does that tell you about where its fat was during baking?
5. How did the doughs feel different at the fold stage? Which was hardest to handle and why?
6. If your kitchen were 28 °C, which method would you choose for production and why?
7. Predict what the same four variants would do in a flaky pie crust. Check your prediction later in [X1-31](experiments/X1-31-pie-fat-size.md).

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Dough temperature.** G coldest (often ~6–10 °C), then C (~10–14 °C), S (~15–19 °C), M warmest (~18–22 °C, the milk cools the melted butter but the fat sets as fine flecks and films).

**Rise.** Typically G ≥ C > S > M. Expect rise ratios roughly 2.1–2.6× for G, 1.9–2.3× for C, 1.6–1.9× for S and 1.4–1.7× for M; your oven and flour will shift the numbers, but the order is robust. G and C often keep straight sides; S and M spread more (width ratio up to ~1.1–1.2) and have rounded, slumped edges.

**Flakiness vs tenderness.** G is the flakiest: visible layers on the side, splits cleanly along a plane, irregular open crumb with some larger voids where shreds melted. C is close to G, with a more even crumb. S is the most "cakey": fine, even, tender and short, with few or no layers. M is fine-textured and dense; it may feel tender and short but also compact and slightly bready or crumbly, and it is the greasiest. **Flakiness needs solid pieces; tenderness needs coating**: G maximizes pieces, S maximizes coating ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)).

**Why chilling did not rescue S and M.** Chilling re-solidifies the fat but cannot re-form discrete pieces. Once the fat has been smeared into a film or dispersed as liquid, it re-sets in place as a coating; there are no pockets to melt and flash to steam.

**Greasiness.** M, then S, leave the largest marks: their fat is spread thinly through the dough and near the surface, and melts out at the base during baking. G and C leave little, because their fat is held inside the pieces and the dough sets around them before much can leak.

**Handling.** G and C doughs are firm, cool and slightly shaggy; M is soft and greasy and sticks; S is soft and sticky and tears when folded.

**The lesson.** Butter temperature and piece size, not butter quantity, decided texture here: the same 22.5 % butter gave a flaky, tall scone or a flat, cakey one. That is the principle you apply to pie dough ([11.3](stage-1/module-11/lesson-03.md)) and, as a continuous sheet, to lamination ([15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)). In a 28 °C kitchen, grated frozen butter (G) is the most robust method.

</details>

## Going further

- Add a fifth variant: C with 5 folds instead of 2. More layers or more toughness?
- Use cold cubed lard or shortening in C: 29 g fat (the fat in 36 g butter) plus 6 g extra milk (the butter's water). Wider plastic range, no water in the fat. Compare flake and flavour.
- Hold cut G and C scones frozen for a week and bake from frozen: does freezing change rise?
- **Stage 2/3** version: measure piece-size distribution by sieving the rubbed mixture through 2 mm and 5 mm sieves (weigh each fraction) and relate it to flakiness score: a quantitative specification for rubbing-in.
