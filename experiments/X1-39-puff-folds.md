# X1-39 · Number of folds vs puff pastry lift

## Question

In one puff pastry block, how do lift, evenness and layer definition change after 2, 4, 6 and 8 single turns? Where is the optimum, and does the layer-thickness calculation from [15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md) predict it?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write down before you begin:

- The nominal number of fat layers for each sample (use F = 3^s).
- The nominal thickness of each butter layer at 3 mm for each sample (the butter is about 37 % of the volume in this formula).
- Your predicted ranking by mean height, and by evenness (difference between the highest and lowest edge).
- Which sample you expect to leak the most butter, and which to have the finest, most uniform interior.
- At what number of turns you expect the maximum lift, and why.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Number of single turns after lock-in: **F2, F4, F6, F8** |
| **Dependent** | Baked height (mm) at centre and edges; evenness (max − min edge height); distinct leaves counted at the cut edge; butter leakage (grease ring width); bake loss (%); shrinkage (%); panel: flakiness, crispness, tenderness |
| **Controlled** | One détrempe and one plaque for all samples; envelope lock-in; block temperature 10–15 °C at every turn (measured); 30 min rest after each pair of turns; rolled dimensions per turn; final thickness 3 mm with guides; square size 7 × 7 cm; edges trimmed; 30 min rest after cutting; no egg wash; same tray, positions balanced; oven profile and bake time |

**Known confound:** the samples are cut from the block at different times, so F2 waits in the refrigerator about 1.5 h longer than F8 before the final rolling. A long cold rest only relaxes the dough further, which if anything helps F2; note it and discuss it in the analysis.

## Batch and scale

One block, divided in four as the turns progress.

Check: 120 g at about 1.1 g/cm³ ≈ 109 cm³; at 3 mm that is about 360 cm² of sheet, e.g. 17 × 21 cm. Three 7 × 7 cm squares use 147 cm², leaving room to trim all edges.

**Scale ×2.5** (for 6 squares per sample or a 5th sample at 10 turns): flour 500 g, water 250 g, salt 10 g, détrempe butter 62.5 g, beurrage 375 g (1,197.5 g). Plaque 20 × 20 cm (≈ 1 cm); détrempe square about 29 cm.

## Procedure

**Day 1 (or at least 1 hour before)**

1. Make the détrempe (R1-33 step 1), cut a cross, wrap, refrigerate at least 1 h, ideally overnight.
2. Plaque: 150 g butter to **14 × 14 cm** (163 cm³ ÷ 196 cm² ≈ 8 mm). Refrigerate.

**Day 2: lamination and sampling (about 3 h)**

3. Bring the plaque to 12–15 °C. Roll the détrempe to a **20 × 20 cm** square (14 × √2 ≈ 19.8 cm). Envelope lock-in. Block about 14 × 14 cm.
4. **Turns 1 and 2.** Roll to **14 × 42 cm**, fold in three, rotate 90°; repeat. Measure and record the block temperature before and after each turn.
5. **Sample F2.** With a sharp knife, cut straight down through the block to remove **a quarter of its length** (about 3.5 cm of the 14 cm). Weigh it (≈ 120 g). Wrap, label **F2**, write the time, refrigerate. Refrigerate the main block 30 min.
6. **Turns 3 and 4** on the remaining block (it is now narrower: roll it to about 3 times its length, keeping the width). Cut off **one-third** of the block (≈ 120 g). Label **F4**. Rest the main block 30 min.
7. **Turns 5 and 6.** Cut the block **in half**. Label one half **F6**. Rest the other 30 min.
8. **Turns 7 and 8.** Label the remainder **F8**. Rest all samples at least **1 hour after F8's last turn**.
   - As the block gets smaller, rolling gets easier, and it warms faster. Keep measuring; do not skip rests.
9. **Final rolling (all four in one session, F2 first).** Roll each sample in both directions with **3 mm guides** to about 17 × 21 cm. Rest 10 minutes on the bench-top tray in the refrigerator, re-roll to 3 mm if it shrank.
10. **Cut.** Trim all four edges of each sheet, then cut **3 squares of 7 × 7 cm** per sample with a sharp knife, straight down. Weigh each sample's three squares together (raw weight). Place on one parchment-lined tray in a **3 × 4 grid**, each row containing all four samples in a different order (for example row 1: F2 F4 F6 F8; row 2: F6 F8 F2 F4; row 3: F4 F2 F8 F6). Write the map on the parchment edge.
11. **Rest 30 minutes** in the refrigerator. No egg wash, no docking.
12. **Bake.** Oven preheated to **220 °C (430 °F)**, rack in the middle. Bake at **210 °C (410 °F) for 15 minutes**, then **185 °C (365 °F) for 10 minutes**. Do not open the door in the first 12 minutes. Remove all at the same time.
13. Leave on the parchment 10 minutes (to measure the grease ring), then cool on a rack 30 minutes.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot mean height (with the range) against the number of turns. Where is the maximum? Is the curve sharp or flat around it?
2. Plot evenness against turns. Does the most even sample coincide with the tallest?
3. Compare your leaf count with the nominal fat-layer count. By what factor do they differ at F2 and at F6? What does that tell you about how many nominal layers survive?
4. Which sample leaked the most butter? Relate it to the nominal butter-layer thickness.
5. Using the nominal thickness at F8 (about 0.17 µm), explain what you see in F8's cross-section.
6. If you were designing a vol-au-vent (tall, straight) and cheese straws (maximum shatter, shape irrelevant), would you choose the same number of turns?
7. How might the F2 confound (longer rest) have affected its result? How would you remove it in a second run?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Nominal numbers.** F2: 9 fat layers, each about 124 µm; F4: 81 (≈ 14 µm); F6: 729 (≈ 1.5 µm); F8: 6,561 (≈ 0.17 µm).

**Height and evenness.** The usual pattern is a rise to a broad maximum around **F4–F6**, then a clear fall at **F8**. F2 often rises high in places but very unevenly: large blisters, domed or tilted squares, thick, bready leaves, and the largest evenness value. F4 is tall with coarse but distinct leaves; F6 is similar in height or slightly lower and noticeably the most even, with fine, regular leaves and straight sides. F8 rises clearly less (often something like half to two-thirds of F6), with a finer, more uniform, cracker-like interior. Absolute heights depend on your flour, butter, rolling and oven: a lift ratio of roughly 5–9× for F4–F6 and 3–5× for F8 is typical, but the ranking matters more than the numbers.

**Leaf count.** You will count tens of distinct leaves at best, never hundreds. The count usually peaks at F4–F6 and falls at F8. Nominal counts overstate real layers enormously beyond F4: at F6 the nominal butter film is thinner than a starch granule, so many adjacent layers must have merged; at F8 so many have merged that the pastry behaves like a single, fat-rich dough with some residual lamination.

**Leakage.** F2 usually leaks the most: its butter is in thick layers (about 124 µm) that melt into pools and run out of the edges before the leaves set. F8 leaks least, because its fat is dispersed through the dough.

**Why.** Lift requires continuous fat films between dough leaves at the moment steam forms ([15.1](stage-1/module-15/lesson-01.md)). Too few turns gives few, thick barriers and too much fat per gap: high but irregular lift and leakage. Too many gives films too thin to stay continuous: layers fuse, less steam is trapped, the rise falls. The classic 6 single turns (or 4 doubles) sit at the point where lift is still near its maximum and evenness is best, which is why they are the professional standard for tall, straight products. For cheese straws, 4–5 turns are perfectly adequate.

**If your results differ.** If all samples are low and bready, the butter smeared (check block temperatures: the small F6–F8 blocks warm quickly). If F2 and F4 show shards and holes, the butter was too cold. If one row of the tray is consistently taller, your oven has a gradient: the balanced layout lets you see it and average it out.

</details>

## Going further

- Run F3, F5 and F7 as well (cut a fifth and sixth sample from a ×2.5 block) to locate the maximum more precisely.
- Repeat with 4 doubles vs 6 singles (256 vs 729 nominal layers) at the same final thickness.
- Repeat with the English lock-in to test whether doubling the nominal layers shifts the curve by about "half a turn".
