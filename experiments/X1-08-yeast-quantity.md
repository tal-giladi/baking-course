# X1-08 · Yeast quantity vs fermentation time

## Question

At a constant temperature, how does the time a lean dough takes to rise 75 % depend on the instant-yeast dose (0.25 / 0.5 / 1 / 2 % of flour)? Is halving the yeast the same as doubling the time?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write down:

- Your predicted time to +75 % for each dose at 24 °C.
- Whether you expect time to be exactly inversely proportional to yeast, and your reasoning.
- Which dough you expect to taste best, and why.

## Batch and scale

Weighing 0.5 g of yeast on a 1 g kitchen scale is meaningless, so this experiment doses yeast as a **10 % yeast suspension**: 1 g of yeast per 10 g of liquid. Even on a 1 g scale this gives ±5–10 % accuracy on the smallest dose.

**Yeast suspension:** 10 g instant yeast + 90 g water at ~30 °C. Stir, wait 5 min, stir again. Use it within 15 min and stir before each dose.

Per variant:

| Ingredient | A 0.25 % | B 0.5 % | C 1 % | D 2 % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 200 | 200 | 200 | 200 |
| Salt (2 %) | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| Yeast suspension (contains yeast / water) | 5 (0.5 / 4.5) | 10 (1 / 9) | 20 (2 / 18) | 40 (4 / 36) |
| Additional water | 131.5 | 127 | 118 | 100 |
| **Total water** (68 %) | 136 | 136 | 136 | 136 |
| **Total dough** | 340.5 | 341 | 342 | 344 |

Check: total water = suspension water + additional water. For example, A: 4.5 + 131.5 = 136 g = 68 % of 200 g.

**Total for the experiment:** 800 g flour, 16 g salt, 75 g suspension (from the 100 g made), 476.5 g additional water.
**Scale-up (×2.5 = one R1-01-size loaf):** 500 g flour, 340 g total water, 10 g salt, yeast 1.25 / 2.5 / 5 / 10 g for A–D. At that size you can weigh the yeast directly on a 0.1 g scale.

If you have a 0.01 g scale, you can weigh the yeast directly instead (0.5 / 1 / 2 / 4 g) and use 136 g of water.

## Procedure

1. Calculate the water temperature for a **DDT of 24 °C**. The additional water and the suspension should both be at that temperature.
2. Label four containers A–D with the dose. Prepare four bowls, each with 200 g flour and 4 g salt, whisked.
3. **Mix at 5-minute offsets** (A at 0, B at 5, C at 10, D at 15 min) so every observation round has the same offsets. Stir the suspension, weigh the dose into the additional water, pour it onto the flour, and mix by hand for 3 min to a uniform dough, then 3 min of squeeze-and-fold. Record the dough temperature.
4. Transfer each dough to its container, level it, mark h₀ and the **+75 % line** (1.75 × h₀), and put the lid on. Put all four in the same 24 °C spot, side by side.
5. **Observe every 15 min** for the first 2 h and every 30 min after that: rise %, surface (dome, bubbles), smell. Take a photo of the row of containers each time.
6. Record the time each dough reaches +75 %. Carry on observing the fast doughs (C, D) to +100 % and beyond: note when the top flattens or sinks (the peak).
7. **Optional bake:** when each dough reaches +75 %, pre-shape, rest 15 min, shape a small boule, proof at 24 °C to a slow partial spring-back, and bake as in X1-07 (covered pot, 250 → 230 °C (480 → 445 °F), ≥96 °C). Blind-taste the next day or once cool.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot time to +75 % against yeast % on log–log axes. What is the slope? A slope of −1 would mean time is exactly inversely proportional to dose.
2. Compute the ratio of times for each halving: t(A)/t(B), t(B)/t(C), t(C)/t(D). Are they equal? Explain any trend.
3. What is the shape of each rise curve: is there a lag at the start? How long is the lag at each dose?
4. Which dough reached a peak and started to fall? How long after +75 %? What does this mean for the "window" you have to shape?
5. Use your curve to choose a yeast % that would give a 3 h bulk at 24 °C. Check it against the rules in [03.3](stage-1/module-03/lesson-03.md).

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Typical times at 24 °C** (without folds, 68 % hydration, 2 % salt): **D (2 %) ~50–70 min, C (1 %) ~1.5–2 h, B (0.5 %) ~2.5–3.5 h, A (0.25 %) ~4–6 h.**

**Not a straight inverse.** Each halving multiplies the time by about **1.5–1.9**, not 2. The log–log slope is typically around −0.7 to −0.9. There are two reasons. First, the yeast multiplies during the longer fermentations, so the low-dose doughs partly catch up. Second, part of the time is a lag before any rise shows (the water must saturate with CO₂ and the yeast must hydrate and adapt). That lag doesn't scale in proportion to the dose. Engineers will see a system with growth plus dead time.

**Lag.** D starts rising almost immediately (visible in 15–20 min). A may show almost nothing for the first 60–90 min.

**Peak and window.** D typically peaks within 30–45 min after +75 %: the top flattens and the dough smells boozy. A stays near +75 % with a stable dome for an hour or more. **Low yeast gives a wide working window, high yeast a narrow one.** This is why low-yeast doughs are more forgiving in a busy kitchen.

**Flavour (if baked).** D tastes distinctly yeasty and sometimes slightly bitter. A and B taste the most wheaty, with more aroma. C is the neutral reference. Crust colour is often a little deeper on the long ferments.

**Practical rule confirmed:** to double bulk time, cut the yeast to roughly 0.35–0.4× of the original, not 0.5×. To halve it, raise the yeast about 2.5×. Or change the temperature, which is often the easier lever ([X1-07](experiments/X1-07-fermentation-temperature.md)).

</details>
