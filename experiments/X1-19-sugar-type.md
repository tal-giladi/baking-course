# X1-19 · Sugar type: white vs brown vs part invert/honey

## Question

At the same total sugar solids, how does the type of sugar (all white, all brown, a 50/50 mix, or a mix with 15 % of the sugar replaced by honey or invert syrup) change cookie spread, colour, chewiness and how the texture holds up over two days?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Rank the four variants from widest to narrowest spread.
- Rank them from palest to darkest.
- Predict which will snap and which will bend on day 0 and on day 2.
- For the honey/invert variant, predict one effect of the extra water it brings and one effect of its sugar composition.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Sugar type at 100 % sugar solids on flour: **W** all granulated; **Br** all light brown; **M** 50 % brown + 50 % granulated (control); **Hy** 42.5 % brown + 42.5 % granulated + honey supplying 15 % sugar solids (or invert syrup, variant **Iv**) |
| **Dependent** | Diameter, thickness, spread ratio; edge, centre and base colour; top surface (crackled, smooth, glossy); bend test and bite texture on day 0 and day 2; weight change in storage |
| **Controlled** | Flour, butter, egg, soda, salt; total sugar solids; butter temperature; creaming time; mixing time; rest time and temperature; portion weight; tray and lining; oven temperature and bake time (fixed); storage |

**Design choice: substitute on solids, not weight.** Honey is ~82 % sugar and ~18 % water. To replace 15 g of sugar you need 15 ÷ 0.82 ≈ **18 g honey**, which brings ~3 g extra water: 3 % of flour weight, or about 7 % more water in a dough that holds only ~42 g water per 100 g flour. Keeping sugar solids equal means you are testing the *type* of sugar; the small extra water is a known, recorded side effect. Brown sugar is treated as sugar by weight (its 2–4 % moisture is part of what "brown sugar" is).

**Invert option:** if you use invert syrup instead of honey, read the solids on the label (typically 70–80 %) and use 15 ÷ solids fraction; e.g. at 75 % solids, **20 g invert syrup**. You can run both as a fifth variant; add one ball of it to each tray.

## Batch and scale

Four doughs, each on 100 g flour.

| Ingredient | W (g) | Br (g) | M (g) | Hy (g) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour | 100.0 | 100.0 | 100.0 | 100.0 |
| Unsalted butter, 18–20 °C | 76.0 | 76.0 | 76.0 | 76.0 |
| Granulated sugar | 100.0 | — | 50.0 | 42.5 |
| Light brown sugar | — | 100.0 | 50.0 | 42.5 |
| Honey (~82 % solids) | — | — | — | 18.0 |
| Whole egg, beaten | 38.0 | 38.0 | 38.0 | 38.0 |
| Baking soda | 0.8 | 0.8 | 0.8 | 0.8 |
| Fine salt | 1.6 | 1.6 | 1.6 | 1.6 |
| **Total** | **316.4** | **316.4** | **316.4** | **319.4** |

Baker's percentages equal the grams, because each dough is on 100 g flour.

**Total ingredients:** flour 400 g, butter 304 g, granulated sugar 192.5 g, light brown sugar 192.5 g, honey 18 g, beaten egg 152 g (beat 4 eggs together), baking soda 3.2 g, salt 6.4 g. Total dough 1268.6 g.

**Portions:** 6 × 45 g per variant (24 cookies); bake 4 per variant, keep 2 spare balls per variant.

**Scale ×2.5:** each variant on 250 g flour (butter 190 g, egg 95 g, soda 2 g, salt 4 g; sugars W 250 g white; Br 250 g brown; M 125 + 125 g; Hy 106.25 + 106.25 g + 45 g honey). Bake 12 per variant.

## Procedure

**Day 1: mix and rest** (about 1 h 15 min)

1. Label four bowls and containers **W**, **Br**, **M**, **Hy**. Butter at 18–20 °C, eggs at 18–22 °C; beat the eggs and weigh 38 g per variant.
2. Mix in the order **M, W, Br, Hy**, each exactly the same way:
   - cream butter + sugar(s), medium speed, **2 min**; for Hy, add the honey with the butter and sugars;
   - egg in two parts, 30 s each;
   - dry ingredients (pre-whisked flour, soda, salt) on low for **20 s**, finish with a spatula until no dry flour shows.
3. Record each dough's temperature and appearance (colour, stiffness, stickiness). Note which dough is softest.
4. Portion 6 × 45 g ± 0.5 g per variant, shape slightly taller than wide, cover, and refrigerate at 3–5 °C for **18–24 h**.

**Day 2: bake** (about 1.5 h)

5. Preheat to **180 °C (355 °F)**, no fan, rack in the middle, 30 min; check with an oven thermometer.
6. Four trays with parchment, **one ball of each variant per tray** in a square, spaced at least 10 cm apart. Rotate positions so that each variant occupies each corner once (a Latin square):

| Tray | Front left | Front right | Back left | Back right |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | W | Br | M | Hy |
| 2 | Br | M | Hy | W |
| 3 | M | Hy | W | Br |
| 4 | Hy | W | Br | M |

Write the variant letter on the parchment.

7. Bake one tray at a time straight from the refrigerator for **11 min**, fixed. Look at the oven window at 6 and 9 min and note which variant starts browning first.
8. 5 min on the tray, then onto a rack on their labelled parchment. Let the oven recover 5 min between trays; use a cool tray each time.

**Evaluation**

9. After 30 min, measure every cookie and photograph all 16 grouped by variant on white paper with a ruler. Photograph the **bases** too.
10. Day 0: bend test and tasting on 2 cookies per variant.
11. Weigh the other 2 of each variant and store them in **separate** airtight containers (one per variant, so moisture cannot move between variants).
12. Day 2 after baking: weigh again, bend test and tasting.

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the variants by spread ratio and compare with your hypothesis. Which difference surprised you?
2. Br and W have the same sugar solids. List every property of brown sugar that differs from white sugar, and say which result each one explains.
3. Hy has only 15 % of its sugar changed, yet may look very different from M. Which two properties of honey are responsible?
4. Which variant changed most in texture between day 0 and day 2? Relate the answer to hygroscopicity and crystallization ([07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md)).
5. Look at the base colour. Why can the bases differ even though the tray and time were the same?
6. Design a follow-up that separates the effect of honey's water from the effect of its sugars.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**W (all white):** the widest, flattest cookies with the most even, pale-golden colour, a shiny, crackled or rippled top and crisp edges. On cooling they become crisp, often throughout; on day 2 they snap. Pure sucrose recrystallizes readily as the cookie dries, and without the acids in molasses the soda raises the pH more but has little acid to react with, so gas production is small: the cookie spreads without puffing. Some bakers taste a faint soapy or metallic note from unreacted soda.

**Br (all brown):** thicker and puffier, often with a smaller diameter, darker (deep golden to brown), with a softer, chewier and moister texture and a strong butterscotch/molasses flavour. The molasses brings invert sugar (reducing, hygroscopic) and acids that react with the soda, giving CO₂ lift and lowering the pH somewhat. On day 2 they still bend. Diameter differences between Br and W vary with the flour and bake; the thickness and texture differences are consistent.

**M (50/50):** between the two in every respect: crisp edges, chewy centre. This is why most chocolate chip cookie formulas mix the two sugars.

**Hy (honey) or Iv (invert):** the darkest cookies, browning visibly first (fructose caramelizes from ~110 °C and glucose and fructose are reducing sugars for Maillard). Usually more spread than M (a liquid, hygroscopic sugar and a little extra water make a softer dough), with a softer, more cake-like or bendy texture and the smallest texture change by day 2: fructose and glucose hold water and interfere with sucrose crystallization. The dough is noticeably stickier. With invert syrup the flavour is neutral; with honey you taste it. The weight loss in storage is typically smallest for Hy and Br and largest for W.

**Bases:** Hy and Br bases are usually darker because the reducing sugars brown faster in contact with the hot tray.

**If your results differ.** If Br spread more than W, check your brown sugar: very moist or dark brown sugar adds water and acid; clumps of brown sugar that did not cream in give uneven spread. If Hy was not darker, check the honey (some "honey" products are diluted syrups) or that it was weighed. Large scatter within a variant usually comes from uneven ball shapes or tray position; compare corner positions in the Latin square.

</details>

## Going further

- Add a variant with dark brown or muscovado sugar and compare with light brown.
- Separate honey's water from its sugars: run M + 3 g water against Hy.
- Replace 15 % of the sugar with glucose syrup (42 DE, on solids) and compare it with invert: glucose syrup is less sweet and less hygroscopic, with less fructose.
- Stage 2: apply the same logic to sablés and tuiles, where the sugar type sets the snap.
