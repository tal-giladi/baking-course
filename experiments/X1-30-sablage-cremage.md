# X1-30 · Sablage vs crémage in pâte sucrée

## Question

With the formula held constant, does the mixing method (rubbing cold butter into the dry ingredients first, versus creaming soft butter with sugar and egg before adding flour) change how a pâte sucrée handles, how much it shrinks, and how tender it is?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

In your notebook, before mixing, write down:

- Which dough will be easier to roll and line? Which will crack more at the edges?
- Which will shrink more in diameter when a 10 cm disc is baked, and by roughly how many millimetres?
- Which will break under a smaller load (more tender)? Guess the ratio between the two.
- Which will you prefer to eat, and which would you choose for lining 30 tartlets? Why might those answers differ?

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Mixing method: **S** sablage (butter 4–8 °C rubbed into dry ingredients, then egg) vs **C** crémage (butter 16–18 °C creamed with sugar, then egg, then dry ingredients) |
| **Dependent** | Handling at rolling (cracking, stickiness, tearing); disc diameter shrinkage (%); bar break load (g); texture panel (tenderness, sandiness, crispness); surface appearance; colour |
| **Controlled** | Formula and ingredient lots; dough temperature at wrapping (≤ 20 °C); rest time (2 h from wrapping); rolling thickness (3 mm guides); cutter and bar template; chill before baking (20 min freezer); tray, oven position, temperature and bake time; cooling time before measuring |

## Procedure

**Mixing (about 45 minutes)**

1. Take 90 g of butter out 45–60 minutes ahead to reach **16–18 °C** (variant C). Keep the other 90 g, cubed at 1 cm, in the refrigerator (**4–8 °C**, variant S). Record both temperatures.
2. **Variant S (sablage), mix first.** Combine flour, salt, icing sugar and almond flour. Rub in the cold butter by fingertips until no butter pieces remain and the mixture looks like fine damp sand (about 3–5 minutes). Add the 30 g egg and mix with a scraper just until it clumps. Fraisage once. Record the mixing time after the egg and the dough temperature. Flatten to a 2 cm square, wrap, **label S** and note the time.
3. **Variant C (crémage).** Mix soft butter with icing sugar and almond flour by spatula (or paddle on low) for 1 minute until smooth. Add the egg in 3 additions, mixing until smooth. Add flour and salt; mix only until no dry flour shows (about 30 s by paddle on lowest speed, or 40–60 s by spatula). Record mixing time after flour and dough temperature. Flatten, wrap, **label C**, note the time.
4. Refrigerate both. **Each rests exactly 2 hours from its own wrapping time**; C will be about 15 minutes behind S.

**Rolling and cutting (after 2 h)**

5. Roll S between two sheets of parchment with **3 mm guides**. Score handling as you go (see observation sheet). Cut:
   - **3 discs** with a 10 cm round cutter (or around a 10 cm template with a knife);
   - **4 bars** of 12 × 3 cm using a ruler.
   - Optional: line one 8–10 cm tartlet ring, trimmed flush, and dock the base.
   Place on a parchment-lined tray marked **S**; freeze **20 minutes**.
6. Repeat for C 15 minutes later, on a tray marked **C** (or on the other half of a shared tray, marked clearly).
7. Preheat the oven to **165 °C (330 °F)** still, 155 °C fan, rack in the middle.
8. **Bake both trays together** (on one tray, or on the same rack side by side), once both have had at least 20 minutes in the freezer. Bake **14–18 minutes**, rotating the tray at 8 minutes, until the discs are evenly golden. Both variants come out at the same time.
9. Cool on the tray 10 minutes, then on a rack for at least 30 minutes before measuring.

## Analysis questions

1. Calculate shrinkage % for each variant. Is the difference larger than the spread between the three discs of the same variant? If not, what can you conclude?
2. Compare median break loads. Express the difference as a percentage of the higher value. Does the panel's tenderness ranking agree with the snap test?
3. Which dough scored worse on handling? Relate that to what the fat was doing to the flour before the egg arrived.
4. How long did you mix C after adding the flour? Predict how your shrinkage and break-load results would change if you had mixed it for 3 minutes.
5. Which method would you choose for (a) a single delicate tart for a tasting, (b) 50 tartlets lined in a production morning? Justify with your data.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Handling.** S is shorter and more fragile: more cracking at the edges, more tearing when discs are lifted, especially if the dough is rolled cold. C is smoother, more cohesive and more plastic; it lines the tartlet ring more easily but may be slightly stickier if it warms.

**Shrinkage.** S usually shrinks less. With C mixed briefly the difference is often small, of the order of 1–3 mm on a 10 cm disc (1–3 %); it grows markedly if C is overmixed after the flour. If your difference is smaller than the disc-to-disc spread, you have shown that a well-executed crémage is nearly as stable as sablage, which is a useful result: the method matters less than the mixing time after the flour goes in.

**Tenderness.** S bars usually break at a lower load (often noticeably lower, for example 20–40 % less) and are rated more tender and sandier by the panel. C bars snap more cleanly and are rated crisper, with a finer, more even crumb.

**Surface.** C gives a smoother, more uniform surface and colour; S can show a slightly rougher, mottled surface.

**Why.** In sablage the flour particles are coated with fat before any water arrives, so the proteins that later meet the egg water are partly shielded and less gluten forms: more tender, less elastic recovery, but less cohesion. In crémage the egg water sits in a butter–sugar emulsion that the flour then meets directly; any mixing after that point develops gluten, which improves cohesion and handling and increases firmness and shrinkage ([11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md)).

**If your results differ.** If S shrank more, check its dough temperature: butter that warmed and smeared during rubbing releases its water and the flour hydrates anyway. If C was more tender, it may have been mixed very briefly while S was worked too much after the egg or given several fraisage passes.

</details>

## Going further

- **Mixing-time series** in crémage: 20 s, 1 min, 3 min after the flour. Plot shrinkage and break load against mixing time.
- **Rest time:** repeat with a 20-minute rest instead of 2 hours for both methods. Which method is more sensitive to a short rest?
- **Snap test rig:** fix the span and loading point precisely (two dowels, a hook), and repeat with 8 bars per variant to estimate variability ([SC-14](stage-1/science/sc-14-measurement-sensory.md)).
- Stage 2 applies the choice to production (mixer-made sucrée, sheeted dough); Stage 3 replaces the rice cup with a texture analyser three-point bend test and treats break force as a specification.
