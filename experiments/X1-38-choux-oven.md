# X1-38 · Choux: oven temperature and venting

## Question

With the paste and piece size held identical, how does the oven profile (constant hot, hot then falling, constant moderate, and hot-then-falling with the door opened early) change the height, collapse after cooling, colour and interior dryness of choux?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

In your notebook, before you bake, write down:

- Rank the four profiles by height at the end of the bake. Then rank them by height 30 minutes later. Will the rankings be the same?
- Which profile will give the darkest shells after the same total time? Will it also be the driest?
- What will you see through the glass in profile (d) in the minute after you close the door again?
- Which profile do you expect to be best, and what would "best" mean in numbers?

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Oven profile, conventional (no fan), same total time of 38 min: **(a)** 200 °C constant; **(b)** 200 °C for 15 min, then 170 °C; **(c)** 170 °C constant; **(d)** as (b), with the door opened fully for 30 s at 10 min |
| **Dependent** | Time rise stops; height and diameter at pull; height after 30 min (collapse %); bake loss %; colour (outside and in the cracks); crack pattern; side rigidity; cavity and interior dryness when cut |
| **Controlled** | One batch of paste; identical pieces (15 g balls, 40 g éclairs) piped the same way; all pieces frozen raw and baked from frozen; same tray, parchment and layout; shelf position; preheat time (30 min for the first bake, 20 min recovery at the start temperature between bakes); total bake time; no egg wash; all pieces cooled on the same rack at the same room temperature |

Why bake from frozen: a home oven can bake only one profile at a time. Fresh paste waiting 40–120 min for its turn would dry and skin, so the wait would become a second variable. Freezing all pieces at once, then baking each tray straight from the freezer, makes the wait identical for every variant. Baking from frozen is also standard professional practice for choux ([14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md)).

## Batch and scale

**Paste:** one batch of [R1-31 Pâte à choux](recipes/R1-31-pate-a-choux.md) (140 g flour, about 740 g paste), made to the V test. Record the panade loss and egg weight.

**Per variant:**

| Piece | Number | Raw weight | Paste |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ball, 3.5 cm, 12 mm plain tip | 6 | 15 g | 90 g |
| Éclair, 12 cm, 16–18 mm French star tip | 2 | 40 g | 80 g |
| **Per variant** | | | **170 g** |
| **Four variants** | | | **680 g** (of ~740 g) |

**Scale ×2.5:** make two batches of R1-31 (or one ×2 batch plus a half batch) to pipe 15 balls and 5 éclairs per variant; bake all of a variant on one tray.

## Procedure

**Day 1: pipe and freeze (about 1.5 h)**

1. Cut four sheets of parchment to fit your tray and label them **a**, **b**, **c**, **d** in pencil on the underside. Draw 6 circles of 3.5 cm and two 12 cm lines on each, in the same positions on every sheet.
2. Make R1-31 to the V test.
3. Pipe each sheet: 6 balls of **15 g** and 2 éclairs of **40 g**, weighing the first ball and the first éclair of every sheet (±1 g for balls, ±2 g for éclairs). Tap down points with a wet finger on every piece, the same way. No egg wash. Pipe the leftover paste (about 60 g) as 4 balls of 15 g on a fifth sheet, labelled **ctrl**.
4. Slide each sheet onto a tray or board and freeze flat for at least **2 h**, until hard. Then stack the sheets with a sheet of parchment between them in a large container or bag. They can wait up to 1 week.

**Day 1 or 2: bake (about 3.5 h for four bakes)**

5. Place an oven thermometer on the shelf next to where the tray will go. Preheat to **200 °C (390 °F)** conventional (no fan) for **30 min**.
6. **Bake (a).** Slide sheet a from the freezer onto the tray and straight into the oven. Start a timer. **38 min at 200 °C**, door closed throughout. Watch through the glass and note: time the rise visibly stops; time colour first appears; a photo every 5 min if possible.
7. At 38 min, remove the tray. **Measure immediately** (step 11). Reset the oven to 200 °C for 20 min before the next bake.
8. **Bake (b).** Same, but at **15 min** turn the thermostat down to **170 °C (340 °F)** without opening the door. Bake to 38 min total. Note the oven-thermometer readings at 15, 20, 25 and 38 min (through the glass): the oven takes several minutes to fall.
9. **Bake (c).** Set the oven to **170 °C** and let it stabilize for **20 min** (it must fall and settle). Bake **38 min at 170 °C**, door closed.
10. **Bake (d).** Preheat to **200 °C** for 20 min. Bake as (b), but at **exactly 10 min** open the door fully for **30 s**, then close it. Watch the pieces through the glass for the next 2 min and note what happens. Turn down to 170 °C at 15 min; bake to 38 min.
11. **For each variant, straight out of the oven:** weigh the 6 balls together; measure height and diameter of 3 balls and both éclairs; press the side of one ball gently and score its rigidity; photograph the tray with a white card and ruler. Do not pierce any pieces.
12. **After 30 min** on the rack: measure the same 3 balls and 2 éclairs again. Cut 2 balls and 1 éclair open vertically; score the interior and measure wall thickness. Photograph the sections.
13. **Optional control:** if (c) is clearly underbaked at 38 min, bake the ctrl sheet at 170 °C until done (rigid, colour in the cracks), and record the time needed. This tells you how much longer a moderate oven needs.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Opening a hot oven releases a burst of hot air and steam: stand to the side and keep your face away. Use dry oven gloves; wet gloves conduct heat instantly. Allergens: gluten, egg, milk.

</div>

## Analysis questions

1. Plot height at pull and height after 30 min for each profile. Which profile lost the most height on cooling, and does its interior score explain why?
2. Compare bake loss with colour. Is the darkest profile also the one with the highest bake loss? What does that tell you about using colour alone to judge doneness?
3. What did (d) do when the door opened, and did it recover? Compare (d) with (b), which is identical except for the door.
4. Look at the oven-thermometer readings for (b). How long did your oven take to fall from 200 to 170 °C? How would you change the schedule if your oven falls very slowly?
5. Which profile would you standardize for your oven, and what bake loss and colour would you write into your recipe as the doneness specification?
6. Your oven is a fan oven. Using your results, how would you translate the best profile, and what extra risk would you watch for?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**(a) 200 °C constant.** Fast, strong rise that stops early (often around 12–15 min). Surface sets quickly, so cracks are larger and more irregular, sometimes with one burst. At 38 min the shells are **dark**, sometimes too dark on the points and bases, and dry, with the highest or close to the highest bake loss. They hold their height. At this piece size the risk is over-colour and a slightly bitter crust, not collapse; with larger pieces, or if you had pulled them when they "looked done" at 20–25 min, they would have been wet inside and collapsed.

**(b) 200 → 170 °C.** Rise as vigorous as (a) at the start, with finer, more even cracks, then a steady drying phase. At 38 min: **even golden-brown including in the cracks, rigid, dry membrane inside, bake loss typically 35–45 %,** little or no collapse (0–5 %). Usually the best overall.

**(c) 170 °C constant.** Slower, smaller rise that continues longer; smoother, rounder tops with small cracks; paler (often 2–3 on the colour scale), and pale in the cracks. The interior is moister, the walls thicker; bake loss lower than (b). On cooling they often wrinkle and lose height (commonly 5–20 %), especially the éclairs. They are underbaked at this time, not ruined: the optional control shows they need 10–15 min longer at 170 °C. Pieces baked from frozen show this more strongly, because they reach 100 °C inside more slowly.

**(d) Door opened at 10 min.** At 10 min the pieces are still expanding and the walls are unset. When the door opens, the oven air temperature drops by tens of degrees within seconds; the surface cools, the steam pressure inside drops, and you often see the pieces **sink visibly** within a minute. They recover only partly, because the surface skin has cooled and begun to set in the smaller shape. Final height is noticeably lower than (b) (often 15–30 % lower), with wrinkled or dimpled tops, thicker walls and a smaller cavity, and more collapse after cooling. The exact size of the effect depends on how far the rise had progressed at 10 min.

**The principle.** Choux needs strong early heat to turn water into steam fast enough to inflate the paste before its surface sets, and then a long, gentler phase to dry the walls. Colour and dryness are separate processes: high heat gives colour before dryness, low heat gives neither in the time available, a falling profile lets them arrive together. The door must stay closed until the proteins have set the shape.

**If your results differ.** If (a) came out best, your oven probably runs cool (check the thermometer readings): its "200 °C" may be closer to 180 °C. If (c) did not collapse, your oven may run hot or your pieces were small; extend the comparison to larger éclairs. If (d) barely changed, the door was probably opened late (after the walls set) or for only a few seconds.

</details>

## Going further

- **Venting:** add a fifth variant, (e), as (b) with the door propped open 1–2 cm for the last 8 min. Compare bake loss, rigidity and crispness 2 h after baking with (b).
- **Fan vs conventional:** repeat (b) in fan mode at 180 °C constant and at 160 °C constant. Watch for lopsided pieces nearest the fan.
- **Fresh vs frozen:** bake (b) with fresh paste and with frozen pieces from the same batch on two days. Is freezing neutral, better or worse in your oven?
- **Hot-oven-off method:** a variant some professionals use: load into a 230–250 °C oven, switch it off for 10–15 min, then bake at 160–170 °C to dry. Compare it with (b).
- **Stage 2** transfers this to deck and rack ovens with dampers (controlled venting) and to large pieces such as Paris-Brest; **Stage 3** logs product core temperature and weight loss over time to design a bake profile as a specification.
