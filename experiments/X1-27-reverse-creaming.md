# X1-27 · Creaming vs reverse creaming

## Question

For the same butter-cake formula, how does the mixing method (conventional creaming vs reverse creaming) change batter specific gravity, cake volume and top shape, crumb fineness and tenderness?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Which batter will have the lower SG? Why?
- Which cake will be taller? Which will have the flatter top?
- Which crumb will be finer, and which more tender? Explain each prediction with a mechanism from [10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md).

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Mixing method: **CR** (conventional creaming) vs **RC** (reverse creaming) |
| **Dependent** | Batter temperature and SG; cake height at centre and edge; dome (centre − edge); bake loss; crumb cell size and uniformity; tenderness (compression test); eating quality |
| **Controlled** | Formula and ingredient batches; butter temperature (18–20 °C); egg and milk temperature (18–22 °C); mixer, beater, bowl, speeds; batter weight per pan; pans; oven temperature and position; doneness criterion; cooling |

## Batch and scale

An educational butter-cake formula with milk, balanced by the regular-cake rules of [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md) (sugar = flour; eggs 60 ≥ fat 50; liquids 110 ≈ sugar 100).

**Per variant:**

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Cake flour or soft plain flour | 100.0 | 100 |
| Caster sugar | 100.0 | 100 |
| Baking powder (double-acting) | 3.0 | 3 |
| Fine salt | 1.0 | 1 |
| Unsalted butter, 18–20 °C | 50.0 | 50 |
| Whole egg, beaten, 18–22 °C | 60.0 | 60 |
| Whole milk, 18–22 °C | 50.0 | 50 |
| Vanilla extract | 1.0 | 1 |
| **Total** | **365.0** | **365** |

Each variant fills one **15 cm round pan** (at least 5 cm deep, base lined with parchment, sides greased) with **330 g** of batter. Alternative: 6 paper-lined cupcake cups × 55 g per variant, both variants in one 12-cup tin, alternating CR and RC cups so neither method occupies the hot edge.

**Total ingredients for two variants:** flour 200 g, sugar 200 g, baking powder 6 g, salt 2 g, butter 100 g, egg 120 g, milk 100 g, vanilla 2 g.

**Scale-up:** ×1.78 per variant for a 20 cm round pan (same depth; about 650 g batter). ×2.5 (250 g flour, 912.5 g batter) for a 23 cm round pan, bake 35–45 min. Replicate the whole experiment once if you can: a single pair can be confounded by oven position.

## Procedure

1. **Prepare.** Weigh both sets. For each variant sift flour + baking powder + salt together. Whisk egg, milk and vanilla together for each variant (111 g per variant). Temper butter to **18–20 °C** (probe). Label pans **CR** and **RC**. Weigh your SG cup's water fill.
2. **Oven.** Preheat to **175 °C (345 °F)**, conventional, rack in the centre, 20 min. Both pans on the same rack, side by side, 5 cm apart; record positions. Check with an oven thermometer.
3. **CR — conventional creaming** (make first; about 9 min):
   - Cream butter 30 s, add sugar, cream **5 min** at medium, scraping at 2 and 4 min.
   - For fairness both methods use the same combined liquid (egg + milk + vanilla). Add **half** of it in 4 small additions, 30 s each at medium, until smooth (this half carries the egg into the creamed fat, as in a classic creaming method).
   - Add the dry ingredients and the remaining liquid alternately: dry ⅓ – liquid ½ – dry ⅓ – liquid ½ – dry ⅓, each **10–15 s at low speed**, then 3 spatula strokes.
4. **Measure** CR batter temperature and SG. Return the cup of batter with 2 folds. Pan 330 g, level, note the time.
5. **RC — reverse creaming** (immediately after; about 5 min):
   - Put the sifted dry ingredients and the sugar in the bowl; mix **30 s** at low.
   - Add the butter in pieces; mix at low **1–2 min** until no pieces larger than a pea remain and the mixture looks like damp sand.
   - Add **half** the liquid; mix 15 s at low, then beat at **medium for 60 s**. The batter lightens and thickens. Scrape.
   - Add the rest of the liquid in **2 additions**, **15 s at low** each, then 3 spatula strokes.
6. **Measure** RC batter temperature and SG. Return the cup with 2 folds. Pan 330 g, level.
7. **Bake both together**, within 10 min of the CR pan being filled (baking powder is working while they wait; keep the gap short and record it). Start checking at 22 min. Pull **each when its centre reads 96 °C** and it springs back; record the time.
8. **Cool** 10 min in the pan, unmould, cool 1.5 h on a rack. Weigh.
9. **Cut** each vertically through the centre; photograph both faces together with a ruler.
10. **Compression test.** Cut a 3 cm cube from the centre of each cake. Measure its height, place a small flat plate on it and a 500 g weight on the plate for 10 s, and measure the compressed height. Compression % = (3 cm − compressed height) ÷ 3 cm × 100. Remove the weight and note recovery after 30 s.
11. **Taste** blind if possible (ask someone to label the plates A/B).

## Analysis questions

1. Which batter had the lower SG? Did the lower-SG batter give the taller cake?
2. Compare dome and top shape. Which would waste less when levelled for a layer cake?
3. Compare cell counts and photographs. Which is finer and more uniform?
4. Which cake compressed more? Is that "more tender", or "weaker"? How would you tell the difference?
5. Explain each difference using gluten formation and the source of aeration in each method.
6. Which method would you choose for (a) a pound cake eaten plain, (b) a three-layer cake cut into thin, even layers? Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Batter.** The creamed batter is usually the lighter of the two (SG around 0.8–0.9), because sugar crystals cut many cells into the butter. The reverse-creamed batter is often similar or slightly heavier (around 0.85–0.95): its aeration comes from the minute of beating after the first liquid, and it is a smoother, more fluid, glossier batter. Your values will vary; the direction is the point.

**Cake.** CR is typically a little taller with a more rounded or domed top and a slightly more open, springy crumb. RC is flatter-topped, sometimes slightly lower, with a finer, more uniform crumb that feels soft and "velvety" and compresses more easily in the cube test while still recovering well.

**Why.** In reverse creaming the flour is coated with fat before any water arrives, so much less gluten can form during mixing, however long you beat; the crumb is more tender, and the batter stays fluid longer in the oven, levelling the top. Creaming gives more, and more varied, air cells from the sugar crystals, so more volume, but the flour meets water with little fat protection during the alternating additions, so a bit more gluten forms and the cake is springier and more domed.

**Choice.** For a pound cake eaten plain, creaming gives the classic texture and good volume. For thin, even layers, reverse creaming gives a flat top and fine crumb that cuts cleanly and wastes less.

</details>
