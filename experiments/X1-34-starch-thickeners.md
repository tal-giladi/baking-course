# X1-34 · Starch thickeners and the amylase trap

## Question

Two questions, sharing one control batch:

1. At roughly equal thickening power, how do **cornstarch**, **wheat flour** and **potato (or tapioca) starch** change the texture, clarity, flavour and stability of a pastry cream?
2. Does a cornstarch pastry cream pulled at **85 °C** (thickened but not boiled) behave differently from one **boiled for 1 minute**, immediately and after **24 hours**?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before cooking:

- Rank the three starches by firmness after 2 h of chilling, and by clarity/gloss.
- Which starch will make the cream most "stringy" when stirred? Which will taste most of cereal?
- Will the 85 °C batch be thinner than the boiled batch at 2 h? At 24 h? By how much?
- What mechanism do you expect to act between 2 h and 24 h, and in which batch?

**Equivalent thickening levels** (standard professional practice, approximate): 1 part cornstarch ≈ 2 parts flour ≈ about 0.9–1 part potato starch. Tapioca starch is used at about 1–1.25× cornstarch; if you use tapioca, use **25 g** instead of 20 g.

## Procedure

Make the four batches one after another using the same method. Order: **D, A, B, C** (D first, while you are fresh, because its endpoint must be exact). Allow about 15 min per batch, plus washing.

1. **Set up.** Four identical containers (shallow, about 12 × 15 cm), lined with film, labelled **A, B, C, D** with the time each is poured. Same shelf in the refrigerator. A small heavy saucepan (16–18 cm), a whisk, a probe thermometer, a timer.
2. **Heat the milk** for the batch with **15 g** of its sugar to a **boil**.
3. **Mix** the yolks with the remaining **40 g** sugar, then the starch for that batch, until completely smooth.
4. **Temper** with about one third of the hot milk, whisking; return all to the pan.
5. **Cook, whisking constantly** at medium heat, with the probe in the centre. Record the **temperature at first thickening** (the moment the cream suddenly resists the whisk and leaves visible trails on the base).
6. **Endpoint:**
   - **A, B, C:** bring to a full boil (large, slow bubbles breaking in the centre) and boil **exactly 1 min**, whisking. Note any change in gloss or thickness during the minute.
   - **D:** take off the heat the moment the probe reads **85 °C** in the centre (it will already be thick). Do not boil.
7. **Pour** immediately into the labelled container, spread to **2 cm**, press film onto the surface, and refrigerate. Record the time.
8. **Wash and cool the pan** under cold water and dry it before the next batch.
9. **Test at 2 h** (all batches at ≤ 5 °C; probe one to check) and again at **24 h**.

**Safety:** Boiling pastry cream spits: long sleeves, high-sided pan. D reaches 85 °C, well above the 71 °C recommended for egg dishes ([FDA-eggs]), so it is safe to taste; all batches should be kept at ≤ 5 °C and discarded after the 24 h test (or eaten within 2–3 days). ## Analysis questions

1. Which starch thickened at the lowest temperature? Why would a root starch swell earlier than cornstarch?
2. Rank A, B and C for firmness, clean cut and gloss. Which would you choose for (a) a fruit tart filling that must hold a clean slice, (b) éclairs, (c) a glossy fruit filling that is not a pastry cream?
3. Did B taste of flour after 1 min of boiling? What would you change in the process?
4. Compare A and D at 2 h. Was D already thinner? If so, which of the two mechanisms (incomplete gelatinization or amylase) can explain a difference already at 2 h?
5. Compare the change from 2 h to 24 h in A and in D. Which mechanism explains the difference that appears overnight?
6. Write the critical control point for pastry cream in one line, with a target and a reason.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Thickening temperature.** C (potato) thickens first, often around 65–75 °C, well before the boil; A and D (cornstarch) thicken suddenly at about 78–85 °C; B (flour) thickens more gradually over a similar range. Root-starch granules are large and swell early and extensively; cornstarch granules are smaller and more resistant, and the sugar raises their gelatinization temperature further ([SC-02](stage-1/science/sc-02-carbohydrates.md)).

**A (cornstarch, boiled)** is the reference: the firmest, shortest gel, holding a clean trench (3) and the smallest line-spread; opaque but glossy; neutral taste. At 24 h it is as firm or slightly firmer (amylose gel strengthening); at most a few drops of liquid on the surface.

**B (flour, boiled)** is softer and pastier at 2× the weight, with a duller, cloudier, slightly grey-cream appearance and a larger line-spread than A. After only 1 min of boiling, most tasters find a faint cereal or floury note (1–2). It changes little overnight. Flour creams need 2 min or more of boiling.

**C (potato, boiled)** is the glossiest and most translucent. It is elastic and pulls into strings when lifted, especially after the 10 stirring strokes; it looks thick hot but sets less firmly cold, so its line-spread is usually larger than A's and the trench closes softly. Over 24 h it may soften a little further. It is a good fruit-filling starch and a poor pastry-cream starch.

**D (cornstarch, 85 °C)** at 2 h is usually already softer than A: many granules were not fully swollen and less amylose leached out, so the gel is weaker. At 24 h the difference is dramatic: D has thinned substantially, often to a thick sauce that flows back into the trench (0–1), with a much larger line-spread and sometimes liquid on the surface. A has not changed. The overnight thinning is the work of **egg-yolk α-amylase**, which survived 85 °C and cut the gelatinized starch during storage ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)). The boil in A both finished gelatinization and inactivated the enzyme.

**The lesson.** CCP for pastry cream: *full boil, held for 1–2 min while whisking (2 min with flour), to complete starch gelatinization and inactivate yolk amylase*. Cornstarch is the pastry-cream starch because it gives the firmest, cleanest set; flour gives body but must be cooked longer; root starches are clear and glossy but stringy and soft.

</details>

## Going further

- **Boil-time series:** cornstarch batches boiled 0, 30 s, 1, 2 and 5 min; line-spread at 2 h and 48 h. Where is the minimum safe boil, and does 5 min start to thin the cream through breakdown?
- **Acid:** make two A batches and stir 30 g lemon juice into one at the boil and one after cooling to 60 °C.
- **Blend of starches:** 50:50 cornstarch:flour by thickening power, compared with A.
- **Stage 2/3:** repeat with a waxy maize starch and a modified (cross-linked) starch, freeze one portion of each and thaw it: this is how bake-stable and freeze-stable industrial creams are designed.
