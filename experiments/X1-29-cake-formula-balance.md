# X1-29 · Formula balance: pushing sugar and liquid

## Question

Starting from a butter cake that obeys the regular-cake balance rules, what happens when you add 25 % more sugar, 25 % more liquid, or both, without changing anything else? Can you predict each result from the builder/tenderizer and moistener/drier balances and the setting-time model of [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md) before you bake?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

This experiment is about prediction. For each of the four variants, write down **before mixing**:

- Which balance rules it breaks (sugar vs flour; eggs vs fat; liquids vs sugar).
- Whether you expect it to set earlier or later than the baseline, and why.
- A predicted profile: top shape (flat / domed / peaked / dipped / sunken), crumb (fine / coarse / gummy), crust colour, moistness, sweetness, bake time relative to baseline.

Score your predictions against the results afterwards.

Each variant fills one **15 cm round pan** (at least 5 cm deep, base lined with parchment, sides greased) with **330 g** of batter. Equal batter weight means each pan holds slightly less flour in S, L and SL; that is part of what you are testing (the structure available per gram of batter).

**Total ingredients for four variants:** flour 400 g, sugar 450 g, baking powder 12 g, salt 4 g, butter 200 g, egg 240 g, milk 250 g, vanilla 4 g.

**Scale-up:** ×1.78 per variant for 20 cm round pans. ×2.5 per variant (250 g flour) for 23 cm pans; a larger cake exaggerates the set-timing effects because the centre takes longer to heat.

## Procedure

1. **Prepare** four sets, labelled **B, S, L, SL**. Sift flour + baking powder + salt for each. Combine egg + milk + vanilla for each (B and S: 111 g; L and SL: 136 g). Temper butter to 18–20 °C. Weigh the SG cup's water fill.
2. **Oven.** Preheat to **175 °C (345 °F)**, conventional, rack in the centre, 20 min. All four pans on one rack in a 2 × 2 layout, 4 cm apart; record positions. Oven thermometer in the middle.
3. **Mix** each variant by the creaming method: butter + sugar **5 min** at medium; half the liquid in 4 additions (30 s each); dry/liquid alternately D-L-D-L-D, 10–15 s at low each; 3 spatula strokes. Note any curdling (S and SL have more sugar to dissolve; L and SL more liquid to hold).
4. **Measure** batter temperature and SG; describe consistency (ribbon time off a spatula, s).
5. **Pan** 330 g; level. Mix variants in the order **B, S, L, SL**; keep the whole mixing session under 40 min and bake together. When you replicate, reverse the order.
6. **Bake** together. Look through the window (do not open) at 15, 20 and 25 min and sketch each profile: record which rises fastest, which domes, and whether any rises and then falls.
7. **Doneness.** Start probing at 25 min. Pull each when its centre reads **96 °C** and it springs back; record the time. Note how the centre behaves in the 5 min after removal (does it fall?).
8. **Cool** 10 min in the pans, unmould, cool 1.5 h. Weigh.
9. **Cut** each vertically through the centre. Photograph all four together with a ruler.
10. **Compression test** on a 3 cm centre cube (plate + 500 g for 10 s). Taste all four, noting moistness, tenderness and sweetness.

## Analysis questions

1. Which variants set later than the baseline (longer bake to 96 °C, more fluid profile in the oven)? Does that match the direction you predicted from the balance model?
2. What did extra sugar do to the top, the crust and the crumb? Explain each with a mechanism.
3. What did extra liquid do? Was the effect of liquid simply "moister"?
4. SL follows the high-ratio pattern of the balance rules. Why did it (or did it not) work with your flour and butter?
5. Pick the variant closest to failure that you would still sell. What single change would bring it back into balance?
6. How many of your written predictions were right? Which were wrong, and what did you misjudge?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**B (baseline)** is level or gently rounded, fine and even, moderately golden: your reference.

**S (+25 sugar).** The batter is slightly more fluid (dissolved sugar). In the oven it often rises as high as or higher than B, then the centre flattens or dips as it approaches doneness, because extra sugar delays starch gelatinization and egg coagulation, so the cells over-expand before the walls set. Expect a darker, shiny, sometimes crackly and sugary crust (more sugar at the surface for caramelization and Maillard), a slightly coarser crumb, more tenderness (higher compression), noticeably sweeter, moister, and a slightly longer time to 96 °C. With a chlorinated cake flour the dip is smaller or absent.

**L (+25 liquid).** The batter is thinner. The effect is less dramatic than you might expect: the extra water gives the starch more water to gelatinize, so structure is not simply weakened. Typical results: similar or slightly lower height, a paler top, a longer bake, a moister but denser and sometimes slightly gummy or "wet" crumb, especially towards the base, with a tendency to a tighter texture. It may taste less sweet (the sugar is diluted).

**SL (both).** The most likely to fail with ordinary flour and butter: a thin batter, a long bake, a pronounced sunken centre (often visible falling in the last minutes or on removal), a coarse crumb with a gummy band beneath the dip, and a dark sugary crust. It follows the high-ratio pattern on paper, but that pattern assumes chlorinated or heat-treated flour and emulsified shortening ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)); without them, the starch cannot set a batter this rich in sugar and water in time.

**The lesson.** Sugar is a tenderizer that also shifts the setting temperature upward; liquid needs to be balanced against sugar (the sugar must dissolve) and against flour (the starch must have enough water, but not so much that the batter is too thin to hold its cells). Every rule of thumb in [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md) is a statement about when the batter sets relative to how far it expands.

</details>

## Going further

- Rebalance S by adding egg (+15 %) and milk (+15 %): does it recover?
- Repeat SL with a chlorinated or heat-treated cake flour, if you can obtain one, and with a high-ratio shortening instead of butter.
- Repeat the series at 165 °C and 190 °C: oven temperature moves setting time too.
- Stage 3 turns this into a designed experiment (a 2 × 2 factorial, with replicates) and maps the boundary between "sets too early" and "sets too late" as a response surface for a new formula.
