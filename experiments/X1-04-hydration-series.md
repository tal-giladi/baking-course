# X1-04 · Hydration series: 60 / 65 / 70 / 75 / 80 %

## Question

Holding flour, salt, yeast, mixing and schedule constant, how does hydration change dough handling, extensibility, stickiness, fermentation, loaf volume and shape, crumb and crust?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Which hydration you think will give the tallest loaf, and which the largest volume (they may not be the same).
- Which will have the most open crumb, and which the crispest crust.
- At what hydration your flour will become too sticky to handle comfortably.
- Whether fermentation (rise in the container) will be faster, slower or the same as hydration rises.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Hydration: 60, 65, 70, 75, 80 % (water as % of flour) |
| Dependent | Stickiness; extensibility and elasticity; container rise at fixed times; loaf weight, height, width, volume; crumb openness; crust colour, thickness and crispness |
| Controlled | Same flour (one bag, your house bread flour), salt 2 %, instant yeast 1 %; dough temperature 25 ± 1 °C; mixing procedure and time; number and timing of folds; bulk time and temperature; shaping; proof time; oven temperature, steam and bake time; container shape; cooling time before cutting |

## Equipment

0.1 g scale; five identical straight-sided containers of about 1 L, marked with tape at the starting level (a clear deli container or measuring jug is ideal); probe thermometer; bench scraper; timer; baking stone, steel or heavy tray; tray for steam or an oven-safe cover; parchment; ruler; optional: five identical small loaf pans about 15 × 8 cm; rapeseed, millet or small dry beans plus a container for volume by displacement.

## Procedure

**Timing plan.** Most home ovens bake 2–3 small boules at once. Run two groups: **Group 1: 60, 70, 80 %**; **Group 2: 65, 75 %**, started 40 minutes later. Within a group, start each variant 5 minutes apart and keep that offset through every step, so each dough has an identical timeline.

1. **Label** containers, parchment and bowls with the hydration.
2. **Water temperature:** compute for a dough temperature of 25 °C with the method from [01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md). Hand mixing adds little friction (take 1–2 °C).
3. **Mix (t = 0):** whisk flour, salt and yeast in the bowl. Add all the water. Mix with a spatula or one hand for 1 minute until no dry flour remains.
4. **Rest** 10 minutes covered.
5. **Develop — identical for all:** 3 minutes of in-bowl pinch-and-fold: squeeze the dough through your fingers, then lift and fold it over itself, rotating the bowl; wet your hand for the wetter doughs, never add flour. This method works across the whole range; kneading on the bench does not.
6. **Measure dough temperature.** Record. Transfer each dough to its container, flatten the top level, and mark the starting height.
7. **Bulk: 2 h at 24–26 °C** with three sets of stretch-and-fold at 30, 60 and 90 minutes (4 folds per set, [02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md)). Mark the dough height before each fold and at 120 minutes.
8. **At each fold, score stickiness and handling** (see sheet). At the 90-minute fold, take a 20 g piece and do a windowpane test, then return it.
9. **Shape (t = 2:00):** turn out onto an unfloured bench (lightly flour only if unavoidable, and note it). Shape each into a tight round boule with the same number of movements. Place seam-down on labelled parchment. Measure diameter immediately.
10. **Proof 45 minutes**, covered, at 24–26 °C. Preheat the oven to 250 °C (480 °F) with stone/steel and steam tray for at least 45 minutes — for Group 2 the oven is already hot.
11. **Score** each boule with one 1 cm-deep cross (same length) and bake at **230 °C (450 °F)**: steam for the first 12 minutes (pour ~100 g boiling water into the preheated tray, or cover the boules with an inverted metal bowl), then vent and bake a further 13–18 minutes to a deep golden-brown crust and **internal temperature ≥ 96 °C**. Bake all variants of a group together for the same time; if one is clearly darker, note it rather than removing it early.
12. **Weigh** each loaf as it comes out; cool 1 hour on a rack. Record crust crispness at 1 hour (press and listen).
13. **Measure** height and width. Optional volume: fill a container with seeds, level, remove seeds, place the loaf, refill with seeds, level; the seeds left over = loaf volume. Specific volume = volume (mL) ÷ weight (g).
14. **Cut** each loaf vertically through the centre. Photograph crumb and crust side by side with a ruler in frame.

**Pan option.** For a volume comparison without spread, bake the shaped doughs in identical small loaf pans at 220 °C for 25–30 minutes. You lose the height/width information but gain a clean rise comparison. Choose one method and use it for all five variants.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot container rise at 120 minutes against hydration. Is the relationship linear?
2. Plot height/width ratio and specific volume against hydration. Where is each maximum? Why can the tallest loaf and the largest-volume loaf be different variants?
3. At which hydration did handling become the limiting factor for you? How would a stronger flour, more folds or an autolyse move that limit?
4. Relate crumb openness and crust thickness to the water available in the dough ([02.3](stage-1/module-02/lesson-03.md)).
5. Which hydration would you choose for a pan sandwich loaf, a baguette and a ciabatta with *this* flour, and why?
6. What would you change in the design to separate the effect of hydration from the effect of fermentation speed?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

Exact positions depend on your flour's absorption and strength (a 13 % flour shifts everything about 2–4 points toward higher hydration compared with an 11.5 % flour).

| Hydration | Handling | Rise in container | Loaf | Crumb | Crust |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 % | Firm, smooth, easy; may feel tight and tear when folded | Slowest | Compact, round, tallest relative to width, lowest volume | Fine, even, dense, cottony | Thicker, duller, softens quickly |
| 65 % | Soft, smooth, very easy | Slightly faster | Good height, good volume | Fine to medium, even | Moderate |
| 70 % | Soft, slightly tacky; needs wet hands for folds | Faster | Often the best volume–shape compromise for a bread flour at home | Medium, some irregular cells | Thinner, crisper |
| 75 % | Sticky, extensible, needs confident folding; shaping harder | Fast | Spreads more; volume can be high if strength was built | Open, irregular, glossy walls | Thin, crisp, blistered |
| 80 % | Very sticky, flows, hard to shape without a technique change | Fastest | Flattest; volume may drop; risk of gummy base | Very open but uneven; may be gummy in centre | Thin, crisp; pale where the dough spread thin, more bake loss |

**Why.** More water lubricates the gluten network (more extensible, less elastic), so the dough expands with less resistance: fermentation appears faster and cells grow larger and merge, opening the crumb. The same softness means the dough cannot hold its shape unless strength is built by folds and shaping; above a point, spread wins over rise and height falls. More water also means more steam in the oven and a longer time to dry the crust and set the crumb — hence thinner, crisper crust and, if the bake is not extended, a moister or gummy crumb. Low-hydration doughs are stiff; their gluten network is fully capable but the dough resists expansion, giving small, fine-crumbed loaves.

**Design limitation.** On a fixed schedule, higher-hydration doughs are further along in fermentation at shaping, so part of their extra openness is extra proof. Question 6's answer: proof each variant to the same container rise (e.g. +75 %) rather than the same time, and record time as a dependent variable.

</details>
