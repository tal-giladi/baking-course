# X1-22 · Overmixing muffin batter

## Question

How does the amount of mixing after wet and dry ingredients meet change a muffin's shape, internal structure and tenderness? At what point does "mixed" become "overmixed" for your batter?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Sketch the side profile you expect for 10, 20 and 60 folds and for 1 minute in a mixer.
- At which level do you expect the first tunnels? How many in the mixer variant?
- Will the most-mixed muffin be the tallest or the shortest? Will batter specific gravity go up or down with mixing? Explain your reasoning.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Mixing after combining wet and dry: **F10** 10 folds · **F20** 20 folds · **F60** 60 folds · **M60** 60 s with a hand mixer or stand mixer paddle on medium speed |
| **Dependent** | Batter appearance and specific gravity; muffin height at centre and shoulder; peak index; symmetry; number of tunnels; crumb structure; tenderness (compression); crust colour and texture |
| **Controlled** | One master wet mix; identical dry mixes; same flour; same fold technique (one person folds all variants); same portion (85 g); same tin, pattern and oven profile; time from mixing to oven recorded; same cooling time |

## Batch and scale

The batter is [R1-12](recipes/R1-12-muffins.md) without zest and berries, at 100 g flour per variant.

**Per variant:**

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Plain (all-purpose) flour | 100 | 100.0 |
| Caster sugar | 45 | 45.0 |
| Baking powder (double-acting or SAPP-based) | 4 | 4.0 |
| Fine salt | 1 | 1.0 |
| Master wet mix (below) | 133 | 133.0 |
| — of which whole egg | 33 | 33.0 |
| — of which whole milk | 70 | 70.0 |
| — of which unsalted butter, melted | 30 | 30.0 |
| **Total** | **283** | **283.0** |

**Master wet mix (4 variants plus bowl losses):** 150 g whole egg (3 eggs, beaten and weighed) + 315 g whole milk at 20 °C + 135 g melted butter at 35–45 °C = 600 g. Whisk the egg, then the milk, then the butter in a stream. Temperature 20–26 °C.

**Total dry ingredients for 4 variants:** flour 400 g, sugar 180 g, baking powder 16.0 g, salt 4.0 g.

**Per-variant yield:** 283 g batter → 3 muffins of 85 g (~28 g left for the specific-gravity check and bowl losses).

**Scale ×2.5:** 250 g flour, 112.5 g sugar, 10 g powder, 2.5 g salt and 332.5 g wet mix per variant → 8 muffins per variant. At this size the fold counts should be raised by about half (15 / 30 / 90) because a larger mass needs more folds to combine; the mixer time stays 60 s.

## Procedure

1. **Oven** rack in the middle, **220 °C (425 °F)**, preheated ≥20 min and checked with an oven thermometer. Line a 12-cup tin (3 rows × 4 columns). **One variant per column**: F10, F20, F60, M60 from left to right.
2. **Dry bowls.** Weigh four identical dry mixes into four same-size bowls labelled F10, F20, F60, M60. Sift the baking powder with part of the flour; whisk each bowl 20 s.
3. **Define a fold.** One fold = scrape the spatula across the bottom of the bowl from the far side to the near side, lift, fold the batter over the top, then turn the bowl a quarter turn. Practise the rhythm on an empty bowl: about **1 fold per second**. The same person folds all variants.
4. **Mix F10.** Pour in 133 g wet mix. Fold exactly 10 times. Some dry flour streaks may still show; stop anyway. Record appearance (lumpy / streaky / smooth, matt / glossy, strands when lifted).
5. **Specific gravity.** Fill your small SG cup level, weigh, compute SG ([08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md)), then scrape the batter back with 1 gentle fold. Do this identically for every variant.
6. **Portion** 3 × 85 g into the F10 column. Record the clock time.
7. **Repeat** for F20 (20 folds) and F60 (60 folds, about 1 min), then M60: add the wet mix and run a hand mixer (or stand mixer with paddle) at **medium speed for 60 s**, moving it around the bowl. Photograph each batter lifted on the spatula.
8. **Bake all together:** 220 °C for 5 min, then 190 °C (375 °F) for 13–16 min, until the tops spring back and the centres read 93–97 °C.
9. **Cool** 5 min in the tin, then 30 min on a rack.
10. **Measure** heights, then cut each muffin vertically through the exact centre (a serrated knife, gentle sawing) and photograph all 12 cut faces together with a ruler.

## Analysis questions

1. At which mixing level did the batter first look smooth and glossy? At which did the first tunnels appear?
2. Did the peak index rise steadily with mixing, or jump at one point?
3. How did specific gravity change with mixing? Did batter density predict height?
4. Which variant was tallest? Is tallest the same as best? Justify with at least two other measurements.
5. Explain the tunnels in terms of gluten, gas and the order in which the muffin sets.
6. F10 may have shown a few dry specks in the batter. Were there any flour pockets in the baked muffin? What happened to them?
7. Write a mixing specification for R1-12 that a new baker could follow without having seen this experiment.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Batter.** F10 is thick, matt and lumpy, with a few streaks of flour. F20 is smoother with small lumps. F60 is smooth, slightly glossy, and pulls into short strands from the spatula. M60 is glossy, elastic and stringy: visibly developed gluten.

**Specific gravity.** Usually changes only a little (a few hundredths), often rising slightly with heavy mixing as gas formed by the fast acid is beaten out, though vigorous mixer action can also beat some air in. Batter SG is a weak predictor here; gluten development is the main variable, and SG cannot see it.

**Shape.** F10 and F20 give broad, rounded domes with small peak indices. F20 is often as tall as or slightly taller than F10. F60 is narrower and more pointed. M60 has a pronounced peak, sometimes off-centre or cracked, and a smoother, paler, shinier crust. The most-mixed muffin can have the highest *centre* but less overall volume and a poor shape.

**Tunnels.** F10: none. F20: none or one short one. F60: several. M60: many long tunnels running from near the base toward the peak. As the muffin heats, the sides and base set first. In a developed batter, gas in the fluid centre merges and the elastic gluten network guides it upward in channels; in an undeveloped batter, bubbles expand where they are ([08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md)).

**Tenderness.** Compression falls steadily with mixing: F10 most tender, M60 chewy to rubbery and bread-like. The difference is large and easy to feel in the bite.

**Dry specks.** Small flour pockets in F10 batter hydrate during the rest and bake and disappear; there are no raw flour pockets in the baked crumb unless whole lumps of dry flour were left.

**A good specification:** "For a 300 g-flour batch, fold 10–15 times with a wide spatula, scraping the bottom each time, until no dry pockets of flour remain; the batter must stay lumpy and matt. Never use a mixer after the wet ingredients are added."

</details>

## Going further

- Repeat with bread flour (12–13 % protein) and with cake flour (7–8 %) at F20: flour protein and mixing interact.
- Replace melted butter with oil at F60: does the more complete fat coating reduce the effect of overmixing?
- Rest the F60 batter 1 h in the fridge before baking: gluten relaxes. Does it reduce tunnels?
- **Stage 3** version: measure batter viscosity with a simple line-spread test or a flow cup as a proxy for gluten development, and relate it to tunnel count, as a quality-control specification.
