# X1-17 · Butter in brioche: 20 / 40 / 60 %

## Question

With the base dough held constant, how does the butter level (20, 40 or 60 % of flour) change how the dough handles, how long the butter takes to incorporate, how warm the dough gets, how fast it rises, and the volume, crumb, tenderness and colour of the baked brioche?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Before mixing, write down for each butter level:

- the time you expect it to take to incorporate the butter by hand, and the final dough temperature you expect;
- the order you expect for rise speed (fastest to slowest) and for baked height (tallest to shortest);
- how you expect the crumb to differ (cell size, tenderness, "bread-like" vs "cake-like");
- at which level you think the dough is most likely to turn greasy.

Justify each prediction from [06.4](stage-1/module-06/lesson-04.md).

## Procedure

**Day 1**

1. **Butter.** Beat the butter between parchment to a plastic slab at **13–16 °C** ([R1-09](recipes/R1-09-brioche.md), Mise en place). Weigh it into three labelled portions (30, 60 and 90 g), each cut into three equal pieces. Keep them on a plate at about 15 °C (fridge for a few minutes if needed, then check).
2. **Master base dough.** Mix as in R1-09 Steps 1–2 (cold eggs, speed 1 for 4 min, speed 2 for 8–12 min) to a **good windowpane**. Record the temperature (target ≤22 °C, so the hand work that follows has room). Weigh and divide into three equal portions. Cover all three and put two of them in the fridge while you work on the first.
3. **Incorporate the butter by hand, one variant at a time, in the order 20 %, 40 %, 60 %.** Start a timer when the first butter piece goes in. For each of the three pieces: spread it over the dough, fold the dough over it, squeeze it through your fingers, then slap-and-fold until the dough comes back together. Add the next piece only when the previous one has disappeared. Stop the timer when the dough is smooth, homogeneous and forms a windowpane. Record the time, the dough temperature, and scores for stickiness, greasiness and extensibility (1–5). Photograph a windowpane.
   *If a dough reaches 26 °C, stop, chill it for 10 min in the fridge, and continue; record the chill.*
4. **Rate jar.** From each variant, weigh **50 g** into a small straight-sided jar or cup (about 200–250 ml), press flat, mark *h₀* and 2 × *h₀*. Leave all three jars together at **24–25 °C**. Read the height every 15 min until each reaches 2 × *h₀*, or for 4 h.
5. **Bulk and retard.** Place the rest of each variant in its own labelled container for **1 h at 22–24 °C**. Then flatten each into a slab 2 cm thick on parchment, wrap, and refrigerate at 3–5 °C for **12–16 h** on the same shelf. The time from the end of butter incorporation to the fridge should be the same (±10 min) for all three.

**Day 2**

6. **Shape** each variant, straight from the fridge, into **4 × 50 g** tight balls. Put all 12 into one buttered 12-cup muffin tin, 4 cups per variant, and label the tin. Record the dough temperature at shaping.
7. **Proof** at **25–26 °C** (not above 27 °C). Wash all balls once, identically, at the start. Record the time at which each variant reaches about 2× its shaped volume (a light, wobbly feel and a very slow spring-back). Look for grease pooling in the cups.
8. **Bake.** If all three are ready within 20 min of each other, wash again and bake together at **190 °C (375 °F)** for 15 min. If not, move the ready balls to the fridge until the last is ready (record this), then bake all together. Record the internal temperature of one ball per variant (target 88–93 °C).
9. **Cool** for 1 h. Unmould, weigh each ball, and measure its height and diameter.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Raw egg dough: wash hands and surfaces after the hand incorporation, and keep dough refrigerated whenever it is not deliberately fermenting. Don't taste the raw dough.

</div>

## Analysis questions

1. Plot incorporation time and dough temperature after butter against butter %. Which is the practical limit for hand mixing in your kitchen, and why?
2. Rank the variants by jar rise time and by baked height. Do the two rankings agree? If not, what does that tell you about the difference between gas *production* and gas *retention*?
3. Describe the crumb of each variant in two sentences. Relate cell size and wall thickness to what fat does to the gluten network.
4. True hydration rose from 48 % to 55 % as butter increased. Could that alone explain the differences in tenderness? Design a control variant that would separate the effect of butter fat from the effect of butter water.
5. Which variant would you choose for (a) a burger bun, (b) a breakfast brioche à tête, (c) a bread-and-butter pudding? Justify each with your data.
6. The variants were mixed in the order 20, 40, 60 %, so the 60 % base dough waited longest. How might this order have biased your results, and how would you remove the bias in a repeat?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Incorporation and temperature.** Incorporation time rises steeply with butter: the 20 % dough takes a few minutes by hand; the 60 % dough takes much longer and goes through a long, slippery phase where it seems it will never come together. Each butter addition lubricates the network so the dough loses grip; more butter means more of that phase. Your hands add heat all the time, so the 60 % dough usually ends warmest, and it is the one most likely to need chilling and to turn greasy. This is why stand mixers and cold ingredients are the norm for rich brioche.

**Handling.** The 20 % dough is the least sticky and most elastic, closer to a soft bread dough. The 40 % dough is soft, silky and extensible. The 60 % dough is the softest, stickiest and most fragile, and may look shiny. When cold on day 2, the order reverses in firmness: the 60 % dough is the firmest out of the fridge, because it contains the most solid butterfat, and it is easy to shape cold but softens fast.

**Rise.** Gas production slows as butter increases, because there is proportionally less water phase for the yeast and the fat coats and weighs down the dough, so the jar times usually increase from 20 to 60 %. Proof times on day 2 follow the same order, often with a bigger spread, because the 60 % dough is also the coldest to start with (most solid fat).

**Volume.** Height and specific volume are usually highest for 20 % or 40 % and lowest for 60 %. More fat means more shortening of the gluten network and less gas retention. With very good development, 40 % can match or beat 20 %, because moderate fat lubricates expansion. Many bakers find 40–50 % the best balance of volume and richness, which is why R1-09 sits at 50 %.

**Crumb and eating quality.** 20 %: more bread-like, slightly chewy, more open and irregular, springy. 40 %: fine, even, tender, pulls into strands. 60 %: finest and most cake-like, very tender, melts in the mouth, richest; if the dough warmed during mixing, it can be slightly greasy and dense at the base. Compression under the same load usually increases (softer, more tender) with butter.

**Colour.** Differences are modest because the egg wash and sugar are identical; the higher-butter variants are often slightly darker, from butter's milk solids, and brown more evenly.

**Hydration confound.** The rise in true hydration from 48 to 55 % contributes some softness, but a lean dough 7 points wetter is not dramatically more tender, whereas the 60 % brioche clearly is. The fat is the dominant effect. The control that separates them is a 20 % variant with 9.6 g of extra water, which matches the true hydration of the 60 % variant.

</details>

## Going further

- Add a 60 % variant with the butter added at 20 °C instead of 13–16 °C, and compare greasiness, dough temperature and crumb. This isolates butter plasticity.
- Repeat at ×2.5 in the stand mixer and compare incorporation time and dough temperature with your hand results.
- Add a 100 % variant (rich brioche) in the mixer only. Where does volume collapse?
- Stage 3 direction: replace 25 % of the butter with a neutral oil at equal fat content, and measure the effect on shaping and crumb; this is the first step into formulating fat blends.
