# X1-20 · Butter state and dough rest vs cookie spread

## Question

How do the physical state of the butter at mixing (melted vs softened and creamed) and a 24 h refrigerated rest (baked immediately vs rested) change cookie spread, and does the effect of resting depend on the butter state?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Rank the four combinations (melted/immediate, melted/rested, creamed/immediate, creamed/rested) from most to least spread.
- Predict whether resting reduces spread by the same amount for both butter states, or more for one of them. Explain why.
- Predict the colour and flavour difference between rested and unrested doughs.

## Why a 2×2 design

The brief for this module lists three butter states (melted, creamed, cold-cubed) and two rest conditions. A full 3 × 2 design is six doughs, which is too much to mix, portion and bake consistently in a home kitchen in one session. Two separate one-variable experiments (butter state at no rest; rest at creamed butter) would take four doughs but could not answer the most interesting question: **does resting matter more for one butter state than the other?** That is an **interaction**, and only a factorial design, where every level of one factor is combined with every level of the other, can show it.

So this experiment uses a **2 × 2 factorial**: two butter states × two rest conditions = four cells. Melted and creamed are chosen because they are the two extremes most used in practice; cold-cubed is an optional fifth cell (Going further).

| | Baked immediately (0 h) | Rested 24 h at 3–5 °C |
|---|---|---|
| **Melted butter** | M0 | M24 |
| **Creamed butter** | C0 | C24 |

**Main effect of butter** = average of (M0, M24) − average of (C0, C24).
**Main effect of rest** = average of (M24, C24) − average of (M0, C0).
**Interaction** = (M24 − M0) − (C24 − C0): if it is near zero, resting has the same effect whatever the butter state; if not, the factors interact.

**Scheduling so that the oven is not a confound.** If you bake the immediate doughs on day 1 and the rested doughs on day 2, "rest" is mixed up with "day" (oven, room temperature, your own consistency). Instead, **mix the rested doughs on day 1 and the immediate doughs on day 2**, then bake all four cells together, on the same trays, on day 2.

**A known confound inside "rest".** The rested doughs go into the oven at 4–8 °C; the immediate doughs at 20–28 °C. So "rest" here means *hydration + enzyme time + cold dough*, which is what "chill your dough" means in practice. The optional cell R24-T in Going further separates temperature from time.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Factor A, butter state: **melted** (melted, cooled to 30–35 °C) vs **creamed** (18–20 °C, creamed 2 min). Factor B, rest: **0 h** (portioned and baked within 15 min of mixing) vs **24 h** at 3–5 °C |
| **Dependent** | Diameter, thickness, spread ratio; dough temperature at oven entry; edge and centre colour; surface; texture; flavour |
| **Controlled** | Formula (identical for all cells); sugar and egg temperatures; mixing times; portion weight (45 g) and shape; tray and lining; oven temperature, rack and bake time (fixed 11 min); evaluation time after baking |

## Batch and scale

Four doughs, one per cell, each on 100 g flour; same formula as the X1-18 baseline (R1-11 without chocolate and vanilla).

**Butter loss on melting:** melting in a small pan or microwave can evaporate 1–3 g of water. Melt ~80 g, then weigh 76.0 g of the melted butter (stirred, so water and fat are both included).

**Portions:** 6 × 45 g per cell; bake 4 per cell, keep 2 spare.

**Scale ×2.5:** each cell on 250 g flour (butter 190, brown 140, white 110, egg 95, soda 2, salt 4 g) = the full R1-11 batch without chocolate. Bake 12 per cell.

## Procedure

**Day 1, evening: rested doughs** (about 40 min)

1. Weigh ingredients for M24 and C24. Eggs at 18–22 °C.
2. **C24 (creamed):** butter at 18–20 °C (probe). Cream butter + both sugars, medium speed, **2 min**. Egg in two parts, 30 s each. Dry mix on low, **20 s**, finish with a spatula. Record dough temperature.
3. **M24 (melted):** melt the butter and cool it to **30–35 °C**. Whisk the melted butter with both sugars by hand for **1 min**, add the egg and whisk **1 min** until glossy and slightly thickened. Stir in the dry mix with a spatula until no dry flour shows (about 30 s). Record dough temperature.
4. Portion each into 6 × 45 g ± 0.5 g balls, shape slightly taller than wide, cover, and refrigerate at 3–5 °C. Record the time. (The melted dough is soft; scoop it and let it firm in the refrigerator before shaping neatly if needed.)

**Day 2: immediate doughs and bake** (about 2 h)

5. At **23–24 h** after the day-1 mix: preheat to **180 °C (355 °F)**, no fan, rack in the middle, 30 min; check with an oven thermometer.
6. Mix **C0** then **M0** exactly as in steps 2–3. Portion and shape immediately. Record dough temperature (this is also their temperature at oven entry, as they are baked within 15 min).
7. Four parchment-lined trays, **one ball of each cell per tray**, at least 10 cm apart, positions rotated:

| Tray | Front left | Front right | Back left | Back right |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | M0 | M24 | C0 | C24 |
| 2 | M24 | C0 | C24 | M0 |
| 3 | C0 | C24 | M0 | M24 |
| 4 | C24 | M0 | M24 | C0 |

Take the rested balls out of the refrigerator only as you load each tray, and probe one of them for its temperature.

8. Bake each tray for **11 min**, fixed. Watch at 3, 6 and 9 min and note which cookie starts spreading first and which stops first.
9. 5 min on the tray, then onto a rack on labelled parchment. Recover the oven 5 min between trays; use a cool tray each time.
10. After 30 min, measure every cookie and photograph all 16 grouped by cell.

## Analysis questions

1. Which factor had the larger main effect on spread, butter state or rest?
2. Is there an interaction? Describe it in one sentence a baker would understand.
3. Using the race model from [07.3](stage-1/module-07/lesson-03.md), explain why M0 behaves as it does, starting from its dough temperature.
4. Your rested doughs were also colder at oven entry. What part of the rest effect might be temperature alone? How would you find out?
5. Compare the colour and flavour of rested and unrested doughs. What happened during the rest besides chilling?
6. How large was the scatter within each cell compared with the differences between cells? Would you trust a difference of 0.5 in spread ratio?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Order of spread.** The most common outcome is **M0 > C0 > M24 ≈ C24**, with M0 clearly the widest and thinnest. M0's dough goes into the oven at 25–30 °C with its fat already liquid and a lot of sugar already dissolved in the warm dough; it starts flowing almost as soon as it goes in and gives a thin cookie with a glossy, crackled top, chewy centre and sometimes a greasy, lacy edge. C0 goes in at ~20–22 °C with plastic fat and air cells: moderate spread, a lighter, more even, slightly cakier cookie.

**Resting reduces spread in both,** typically by a clear margin, and the rested cookies are thicker, with more even golden-brown colour, a deeper toffee/butterscotch flavour and a less "raw sugar" taste. During the rest the flour absorbs the free water, the butter crystallizes again (even melted butter re-solidifies in the cold dough), and enzymes from flour and egg release small amounts of sugars and amino acids that feed browning. The surface also dries slightly.

**Interaction.** The rest effect is usually **larger for the melted-butter dough**: M24 − M0 is a bigger drop than C24 − C0. Resting undoes most of what melting did: the liquid fat solidifies and the excess free water is absorbed, so M24 ends up much closer to C24 than M0 was to C0. The lines on the interaction plot converge. In baker's language: "if you melt the butter, you must rest the dough; if you cream it, resting helps less for spread but still improves flavour and colour."

**Temperature vs time.** Part of the rest effect is only the cold: a cold ball needs longer to warm to the point where the fat melts, so the edges set first. The R24-T cell below separates that part. Typically the tempered, rested dough spreads more than the cold rested dough but still less than the unrested one: both hydration and temperature matter.

**If your results differ.** If C0 spread as much as M0, check the butter temperature: butter above ~22 °C creams into a greasy mass that behaves like melted butter. If M24 spread more than C0, the melted butter may have been too hot (above ~40 °C), dissolving more sugar and partly cooking the egg. If the scatter within cells is as large as the differences, look at ball shape and tray position.

</details>

## Going further

- **R24-T (temperature vs hydration):** a fifth cell of creamed dough rested 24 h, then left at room temperature for 60–90 min until the balls reach 18–20 °C before baking. Compare with C24 (same rest, cold) and C0 (no rest, similar temperature).
- **Cold-cubed butter (third butter level):** butter at 4–6 °C in 1 cm cubes, beaten with the sugars on low for 2 min until just combined (lumps of butter remain), then egg and flour as usual. Expect the least spread, thick and uneven cookies with a tender, short bite.
- Extend the rest to 48 and 72 h and track colour, flavour and spread as a time series.
- Stage 3: use the same 2 × 2 structure with replicated batches and analyse it statistically (analysis of variance), so you can say how confident you are that an interaction is real.
