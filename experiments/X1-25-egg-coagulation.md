# X1-25 · Egg coagulation temperature series

## Question

At what temperatures do egg white, egg yolk, beaten whole egg and a diluted custard mixture change from liquid to thickened, to soft-set, to firm, to rubbery, when each is held at a fixed temperature for a fixed time? How much does dilution with milk and sugar shift the setting temperature?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before heating anything:

- For each of the four samples, predict the lowest temperature at which it will no longer flow when the cup is tipped.
- Predict at which temperature the white will be tender and just set, and at which it will be rubbery.
- Will the yolk or the white be more set at 65 °C?
- How many degrees later do you expect the custard mixture to set than the beaten whole egg?

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Holding temperature: **60, 63, 65, 68, 70, 75, 80 °C** (and the four sample types, compared within each temperature) |
| **Dependent** | Texture score (0–5, defined below); flow when tipped; opacity; colour; weeping (liquid released when cut); photos |
| **Controlled** | Hold time at temperature (15 min, timed from when a dummy sample reaches bath temperature); sample mass and depth (~1 cm); cup type; eggs (one carton, same age); custard mixture (one batch); starting temperature of samples (refrigerator, 4–6 °C); cooling (ice water 2 min); observer and scoring scale |

## Batch and scale

Each temperature uses four samples in small heatproof glass cups (espresso cups, 60–100 ml glass jars or small ramekins). All cups should be the same type, so that heating time is the same.

**Per temperature:**

| Sample | Contents | Grams per cup |
|---|---|---|
| W — egg white | White, stirred gently to break up the thick white | 20 |
| Y — egg yolk | One whole yolk, intact, membrane unbroken (weigh and record) | ~18–20 |
| E — whole egg | Whole egg beaten smooth with a fork | 25 |
| C — custard mixture | Mixture below, stirred | 22 |
| D — dummy (temperature reference) | Water, with the probe in it | 20 |

**Custard mixture (make once, 170 g):** 50 g whole egg (beaten), 100 g whole milk, 20 g sugar. Whisk until the sugar dissolves; strain. This is roughly 1 part egg to 2 parts milk with 12 % sugar, close to a baked custard ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md)). You use 7 × 22 = 154 g.

**Total for 7 temperatures:**

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Egg whites | 140 g (from the 7 eggs you separate for the yolks) |
| Egg yolks | 7 whole yolks |
| Whole egg, beaten | 175 g for E + 50 g for the custard = 225 g (about 5 eggs) |
| Whole milk | 100 g |
| Sugar | 20 g |
| **Eggs needed** | **about 12** (7 separated + 5 beaten) |

**Scale ×2.5:** not useful; larger samples take longer to heat and blur the result. To extend the experiment, add temperatures (62, 72, 85 °C) or a second hold time (see Going further), not mass.

**Equipment:**
- A large pot (4–6 L) filled with 3–4 L of water: more water holds its temperature better. Or a sous-vide circulator in a pot, which makes this experiment much easier.
- A trivet, small wire rack or folded cloth on the base of the pot so cups do not sit on the hot bottom.
- Two probe thermometers if possible (one in the bath, one in the dummy); at least one, calibrated ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)).
- Kettle of boiling water and a jug of cold water for adjustments.
- 29 identical small cups (or 5 and wash between rounds), foil or a lid for the pot, tongs or a jar lifter.
- Bowl of ice water; a teaspoon for the texture test; labels; camera.

## Procedure

**Holding a steady temperature without a circulator.** Put the pot on the smallest burner at a low setting. Clip the probe so its tip is at the depth of the samples, not touching the pot. Stir the bath every minute or two. Adjust by adding boiling water (to raise) or cold water (to lower) in 50–100 g amounts, not by turning the burner up: the burner overshoots. With 3–4 L of water and a lid or foil over most of the pot, you can hold **±1 °C** with attention. A circulator holds ±0.1 °C.

1. **Prepare samples.** Separate 7 eggs (three-bowl method, [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md)). Weigh the white samples (20 g) and put one intact yolk in each Y cup. Beat 5 eggs; weigh E samples (25 g); use 50 g for the custard mixture and weigh C samples (22 g). Cover all cups with film and refrigerate, so every sample starts at 4–6 °C. Label the cups by temperature and sample: e.g. **60-W, 60-Y, 60-E, 60-C**.
2. **Run temperatures from lowest to highest,** one round at a time: 60, 63, 65, 68, 70, 75, 80 °C. Stepping up is faster than cooling the bath down. (With two pots or a circulator and a second pot, run two temperatures at once and halve the total time.)
3. **For each round:**
   1. Bring the bath to the target temperature and hold it for 2 min.
   2. Take that round's four cups and the dummy (D) from the refrigerator, remove the film, and stand them on the trivet so that the water comes 1 cm above the level of the samples but cannot slop in. Cover the pot loosely with foil.
   3. The dummy's probe shows when the samples are at temperature (typically 4–7 min). **Start the 15-min hold when D is within 1 °C of the target.** Record the come-up time.
   4. Keep the bath within ±1 °C for 15 min, recording its temperature every 3 min.
   5. At 15 min, lift the cups out with tongs and stand them in ice water for **2 min** to stop the cooking.
   6. **Observe immediately** (next section), photograph the four cups side by side with the label, then move on to the next temperature.
4. **Total time:** about 25 min per round (bath adjustment, come-up, 15 min hold, observation), so **about 3 h** for seven temperatures with one pot.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Samples held below 71 °C for 15 min are **not** reliably safe to eat unless you used pasteurized eggs; treat them as raw egg and discard them ([FDA-eggs]). You may taste the 75 and 80 °C samples if the eggs were handled hygienically. The bath water scalds at 60 °C and above: lift cups with tongs, keep the pot on a back burner with the handle turned in, and do not leave it unattended. Wash hands and surfaces after handling raw egg.

</div>

## Measurements

**Texture score** (use the same scale for all samples):

| Score | Description | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Unchanged: liquid, raw appearance | Flows freely when the cup is tipped |
| 1 | Thickened or milky; still flows | Flows slowly, like syrup or honey |
| 2 | Soft, fragile gel; trembles; barely holds | Holds when tipped 45°; teaspoon breaks it into a soft, wet mass |
| 3 | Tender set; holds shape; moist | Teaspoon cuts a clean, moist piece that holds its shape |
| 4 | Firm; springy or pasty | Needs pressure to cut; bounces back (white) or is pasty-firm (yolk) |
| 5 | Rubbery or dry-crumbly; may weep | Tough, squeaky (white) or dry, crumbling (yolk); liquid appears when cut |

| What | How | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Bath temperature | Every 3 min during hold; record min and max | Probe |
| Come-up time | Time until dummy is within 1 °C of target | Probe in dummy, timer |
| Texture | Score 0–5 as above | Cup tilt, teaspoon |
| Opacity / colour | Clear / translucent / milky / opaque; yolk colour (deep orange / yellow / pale) | Eyes, photo against a white card |
| Weeping | Liquid visible in the cup after cutting: none / trace / pool | Eyes |

## Analysis questions

1. For each sample, what is the lowest temperature at which it scored 2 (holds when tipped)? And 3 (tender set)?
2. At 65 °C, which was more set: white or yolk? At 80 °C? Explain using the different proteins in each.
3. How many degrees later did the custard mixture reach score 2–3 than the beaten whole egg? Which two ingredients cause the shift, and how?
4. At which temperature did weeping first appear in any sample? What is happening to the protein network?
5. Your come-up time was several minutes. How would the results change if you held each sample for 5 minutes instead of 15? For 60 minutes?
6. Using your graph, choose a holding temperature for (a) an egg with a set but tender white and a thick, jammy yolk; (b) a custard that is just set; (c) a sabayon endpoint. Compare (c) with [R1-16](recipes/R1-16-sabayon.md).

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

Typical results after a 15-min hold (scores vary by ±1 with sample depth, egg age and bath accuracy):

| Temperature | White (W) | Yolk (Y) | Whole egg (E) | Custard (C) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 °C | 1: slightly milky, flows | 0–1: liquid, perhaps slightly thicker | 0–1 | 0 |
| 63 °C | 2: milky, trembling, very soft gel | 1–2: thick, syrupy, honey-like | 1: thickened | 0 |
| 65 °C | 2–3: opaque, delicate gel | 2: thick, fudgy, holds shape when tipped slowly | 2: soft, custard-like | 0–1 |
| 68 °C | 3: tender, opaque, softly set | 3: fudgy paste, spreadable, holds shape | 2–3 | 1: slightly thickened |
| 70 °C | 3: tender set | 3–4: set, pasty, starting to crumble | 3: set, moist, tender | 1–2: thick, coats the spoon |
| 75 °C | 4: firm, springy | 4: firm, crumbly | 4: firm | 2: soft set, wobbly |
| 80 °C | 4–5: firm to rubbery, possibly a trace of liquid | 5: dry, crumbly, paler | 4–5: firm, may weep | 3: set, smooth, wobbles when shaken |

**White.** It changes over a wide range because it is a mixture of proteins: ovotransferrin starts to set around 62 °C, giving the milky, fragile gel; ovalbumin, the main protein, sets fully only around 80 °C, which is when the white becomes firm and then rubbery.

**Yolk.** It thickens from about 65 °C and sets by about 70 °C, over a narrower range; past 75 °C it turns crumbly and dry. At 63–65 °C you see the "jammy" yolk of sous-vide eggs. At 65 °C the white is a soft gel and the yolk a thick paste: similar degrees of set, different textures. At 80 °C the white is rubbery and the yolk crumbly.

**Whole egg** sets between the two, at about **70–73 °C**, and starts to weep near 80 °C.

**Custard mixture** thickens only around 70 °C and sets at roughly **78–82 °C**, about **8–12 °C later** than whole egg. The milk dilutes the proteins (they meet less often) and the sugar gets between them and holds water, so bonding needs more heat and the gel is softer. That is why custards are cooked to 80–85 °C ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md), [X1-33](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md)).

**Weeping** appears first in the whole egg and white at 75–80 °C: the protein network over-coagulates, contracts and squeezes out water (syneresis).

**Time.** Coagulation is a rate process. With only 5 minutes at temperature (much of which is come-up time), every sample would score lower; with 60 minutes, higher, especially at 63–68 °C, where the reaction is slow. A 63 °C egg held for an hour has a noticeably firmer yolk than one held for 15 minutes.

**Choices.** (a) About 63–65 °C for 45–60 min in the shell. (b) About 78–82 °C for a diluted custard. (c) About 72–78 °C: a sabayon is yolk diluted with sugar and wine, so it tolerates more heat than pure yolk (which is set by 70 °C) but less than a milk custard, and it is heated fast and whisked, so it can be pulled at a lower temperature than a custard held for 15 minutes.

</details>

## Going further

- **Time series:** hold whole-egg samples at 65 °C for 5, 15, 30 and 60 min.
- **Acid and salt:** at 65 and 70 °C, compare beaten whole egg with 1 % lemon juice or 1 % salt added. Both should set sooner and firmer ([09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md)).
- **Sugar dose:** custard mixtures with 0, 10 and 20 % sugar at 75 and 80 °C.
- **In the shell:** eggs in the shell held for 45 min at 63, 65 and 70 °C (the shell and size make the centre heat slowly: 15 min is not enough). Compare white and yolk with your cup results.
- **Stage 2:** sous-vide textures for plated desserts and precise set creams; **Stage 3:** measure gel firmness with a penetrometer or a simple weight-drop test and fit a time–temperature model.
