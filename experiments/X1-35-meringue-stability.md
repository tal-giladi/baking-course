# X1-35 · Meringue method vs stability

## Question

With the same egg white and the same sugar in the same proportions, how does the method (French, Swiss or Italian) change the meringue's volume, gloss, piping definition, stability over 2 hours and behaviour when baked at 100 °C?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before you whip anything:

- Rank the three meringues by specific gravity (lightest to densest).
- Rank them by how much liquid will drain out in 2 hours, and by how well a piped rosette will keep its height and ridges.
- Predict which will bake lightest and crispest, and which will bake densest.
- Predict whether the three will lose the same percentage of weight when fully dried, and why or why not.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Meringue method: F French (raw sugar added after soft peak), S Swiss (whites + sugar heated to 70 °C, then whipped), I Italian (syrup at 119 °C poured into whipping whites) |
| **Dependent** | Whip time; final temperature; specific gravity and foam volume; gloss; peak; piped rosette height and ridge definition at 0, 30, 60, 120 min; drainage (g) at 30, 60, 120 min; baked weight loss, colour, shape, texture |
| **Controlled** | Egg whites from one pooled batch (mixed, then weighed out); white temperature (20 ± 2 °C at the start); white : sugar ratio 1 : 2; sugar type (caster for all); no cream of tartar; same hand mixer, same whisk beaters, same bowl size; same speed settings; endpoint "stiff, glossy peak" judged the same way; same piping tip and rosette size; same room (record temperature and humidity); same oven, tray and position |

## Batch and scale

Per variant: **60 g egg white and 120 g caster sugar.** The Italian meringue needs water to make the syrup; that water is a process aid, and most of it boils off.

| Ingredient | F French | S Swiss | I Italian |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg whites (pooled) | 60 | 60 | 60 |
| Caster sugar, whipped into whites | 120 | — | 12 |
| Caster sugar, heated with whites | — | 120 | — |
| Caster sugar, in syrup | — | — | 108 |
| Water, in syrup | — | — | 36 |
| **Total before process** | **180** | **180** | **216** |
| Expected after process | 180 | ~178 (a little evaporates) | ~196 (syrup 108 ÷ 0.87 ≈ 124 g) |

**Total ingredients:** 180 g egg white (about 6 large eggs; separate 7 to be safe) and 360 g caster sugar, 36 g water.

**Scale ×2.5:** 150 g white and 300 g sugar per variant (Italian: 30 g sugar in the whites, 270 g in a syrup with 90 g water). At this size a stand mixer works well, and you can pipe a full tray from each.

## Procedure

**Set-up (30 min before)**

1. Separate the eggs, pool the whites in one bowl, and stir gently with a fork to mix without foaming. Weigh three 60 g portions into three identical clean, grease-free bowls. Cover and leave to reach 20 ± 2 °C.
2. Preheat the oven to **100 °C (212 °F)**, conventional, with an oven thermometer on the middle rack. Line one tray with parchment; draw three columns labelled F, S, I.
3. Prepare: a 100 ml cup for SG; three drainage rigs (a small tea strainer or funnel with a mesh insert, resting on a small tared cup); a piping bag with a medium open-star tip (reuse by washing and drying between variants, or use three bags with identical tips); a plate lined with parchment for the rosettes at room temperature, marked F, S, I; a timer; a ruler.
4. Record room temperature and, if you can, relative humidity.

**Order of making.** Make **I first** (it takes longest and needs your full attention), then **S**, then **F**. Each variant has its own t = 0, the moment whipping stops. All stability readings are timed from that variant's own t = 0.

**For each variant**

5. **Make the meringue:**
   - **I Italian:** Syrup: 108 g sugar + 36 g water in a 12–14 cm pan, dissolved, walls washed, boiled without stirring (tilt the pan to cover the probe). At 110 °C, start whipping the whites on medium; at soft peak add the 12 g sugar gradually and whip to firm peak. At **119 °C** pour the syrup in a thin stream down the side of the bowl while whipping on high. Whip on high until stiff and glossy and the bowl is barely warm (target **30 °C**).
   - **S Swiss:** Whites + 120 g sugar over a bain-marie, whisking constantly, to **70 °C**, sugar fully dissolved (no grit). Whip on high until stiff and glossy and **30 °C**.
   - **F French:** Whip whites on medium to foamy, then high to soft peak. Add the 120 g sugar about 10 g every 15–20 seconds while whipping on high. Whip until stiff and glossy.
6. Record **whip time** (from first switching on the mixer to the endpoint) and **final temperature**.
7. **SG:** fill the 100 ml cup level, weigh, divide by 100. Calculate foam volume = final meringue weight ÷ SG (ml).
8. **Gloss and peak:** score gloss (1 dull – 5 mirror) and describe the peak.
9. **Pipe:** three rosettes of about **10 g** each onto the room-temperature plate, and three rosettes of about 10 g onto the oven tray in the variant's column. Weigh the oven-tray rosettes as a group (tare the tray and parchment first, or weigh the parchment strip).
10. **Bake:** put the tray in the oven as soon as the first variant is piped, and add the others as they are piped, opening the door briefly. Bake each for **90 min from its own loading time**, then turn the oven off after the last one and leave all three in with the door ajar for 1 h. (Remove earlier ones at 90 min + 1 h if you prefer; record what you did.)
11. **Drainage:** spoon **50 g** of meringue into the strainer over its tared cup. Cover loosely with an upturned bowl (not touching) to limit drying.
12. **Readings at 0, 30, 60 and 120 min:** rosette height (ruler, from plate to tip, average of three), ridge definition (1 lost – 5 sharp), any liquid at the base or droplets on top; drainage liquid weight (g).
13. **Baked samples:** when cool, weigh (weight loss %), note colour, shape (spread, cracks, collapse), break one open and score texture (crisp/dense/chewy/hollow), sweetness and melt.

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the three by SG. Did the ranking match your hypothesis? What in each method explains its density?
2. Which meringue drained first, and how much had each drained at 120 min? Relate the result to how completely the sugar was dissolved and how much heat each foam received.
3. Plot rosette height against time for all three. Which one would you choose for a piped decoration that must stand for 6 hours at a buffet?
4. Calculate the theoretical water content of each meringue (egg white is about 88 % water; add the water left in the Italian syrup, about 16 g). Compare with your measured baked weight loss. Were all three fully dried?
5. Which baked meringue would you choose for a light, crisp pavlova shell, and which for a meringue that must not shatter when cut?
6. What is the largest source of variation in this experiment that you could not control? How would you reduce it?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Density and volume.** French is usually the lightest (lowest SG, roughly 0.15–0.22 with a hand mixer) and has the most volume per gram of white. Swiss is the densest (roughly 0.22–0.32). Italian sits between them or close to Swiss (roughly 0.20–0.28). French is lightest because raw, unheated whites foam most easily; heat in the Swiss and Italian methods partly unfolds and sets proteins before or while air goes in, so they form smaller bubbles with thicker walls. Swiss and Italian also take longer to whip, because you whip until they cool.

**Gloss and piping.** Swiss and Italian are glossier and hold sharper ridges from a star tip. French is glossy when fresh but softer-edged.

**Stability over 2 hours.** French drains first, often visibly within 30–60 minutes (a few grams from 50 g by 2 hours is typical), and its rosettes lose some height and ridge sharpness; beads of syrup may appear. Swiss and Italian typically drain little or nothing in 2 hours, and their rosettes are almost unchanged. Italian usually keeps the sharpest ridges. The ranking follows the model in [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md): fully dissolved sugar and partial heat-setting of the proteins make the films thicker and the syrup between bubbles less mobile.

**Baked at 100 °C.** French bakes lightest and most fragile: crisp, white, melting, sometimes with small cracks or slight spread. Swiss bakes denser and harder, with a smooth, slightly shiny surface and a crunchier, candy-like bite. Italian holds its piped shape most precisely, dries to a fine, dense texture, and may stay very slightly chewy in the centre after 90 minutes because it is the densest foam, even though its surface sets quickly.

**Weight loss.** French and Swiss contain about 29 % water (53 g in about 180 g); Italian about 35 % (53 g + ~16 g from the syrup in about 196 g). A fully dried French or Swiss meringue loses about 25–28 %. If your Italian lost less than about 30 %, it is not fully dry. Comparing measured loss with theoretical water tells you which were under-dried, which is why the loss must be judged against each meringue's own water content.

**If your results differ.** A French meringue that did not drain may have been whipped with more care than usual (slow sugar addition) in a dry room; a Swiss that drained probably did not reach 70 °C and still had undissolved sugar (check for grit). Large differences in whip time between variants often come from the mixer: a hand mixer tires and slows, so keep the speed setting constant and note it.

</details>
