# X1-26 · Creaming time

## Question

In a pound cake with no chemical leavener, how does the time spent creaming butter and sugar (1, 3, 6 and 10 minutes) change batter specific gravity, cake height and crumb? Is there a point beyond which more creaming stops helping, or starts to hurt?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Predict the batter SG for each creaming time, and sketch SG (y) against creaming time (x). Will the curve keep falling, flatten, or turn back up?
- Predict which cake will be tallest and which will have the finest crumb. Are those the same cake?
- Predict what happens to the butter's temperature during 10 minutes of creaming, and what that might do.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Creaming time of butter + sugar at medium speed: **1, 3, 6, 10 min** (labels C1, C3, C6, C10) |
| **Dependent** | Mixture temperature after creaming; batter temperature and SG after eggs and flour; cake height at centre and edge; weight and bake loss; crumb cell size and uniformity; tenderness; any curdling |
| **Controlled** | Formula and ingredient batches; butter start temperature (18–20 °C, probed); egg temperature (18–22 °C); egg addition (6 additions, 30 s each); flour mixing (2 additions, 15 s each + 3 spatula strokes); mixer, beater, bowl and speed; batter weight per pan; pans; oven temperature and position; doneness criterion (internal temperature); cooling time |

**No baking powder** in this experiment: all lift must come from creamed air and steam, so the effect of creaming is not masked.

## Batch and scale

The quarter-scale quatre-quarts of [R1-17](recipes/R1-17-pound-cake.md), without baking powder. Each variant is mixed separately.

**Per variant:**

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Cake flour or soft plain flour | 100.0 | 100 |
| Caster sugar | 100.0 | 100 |
| Unsalted butter, 18–20 °C | 100.0 | 100 |
| Whole egg, beaten, 18–22 °C | 100.0 | 100 |
| Fine salt | 0.75 | 0.75 |
| Vanilla extract | 2.0 | 2 |
| **Total** | **402.75** | **402.75** |

Each variant fills one pan with **350 g** of batter; the rest is used for the SG measurement and discarded (or baked as a cupcake for tasting).

**Total ingredients for four variants:** flour 400 g, sugar 400 g, butter 400 g, eggs 400 g (about 8 large, beaten together and then divided into 4 × 100 g), salt 3 g, vanilla 8 g.

**Pans:** four identical pans: mini loaf pans about 15 × 8 cm at the top and 5–6 cm deep, or 15 cm round pans at least 5 cm deep. Greased and floured, or lined with parchment.

**Scale-up:** ×2 = the full [R1-17](recipes/R1-17-pound-cake.md) formula (without baking powder) in 22 × 11 cm loaf pans, one per variant, baked 50–65 min. ×2.5 (250 g of each main ingredient, about 1,007 g of batter) fills a 20 cm round pan per variant. Larger batches cream more efficiently in a stand mixer, so expect lower SG at each time.

**Mixer:** 100 g of butter is too little for most stand-mixer bowls (the paddle barely reaches it). Use a **hand mixer in a medium bowl (1.5–2 L)** at a fixed speed, or a stand mixer with a small bowl insert. Use the same mixer, beaters, bowl and speed for all four.

## Procedure

1. **Prepare everything first.** Weigh four sets of flour + salt (sifted together), four sets of sugar and four 100 g portions of butter cut in cubes. Beat all the eggs with the vanilla together and divide into 4 × 102 g. Label pans **C1, C3, C6, C10**. Weigh your SG cup's water fill once (see [10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)).
2. **Oven.** Preheat to **165 °C (330 °F)**, conventional, rack in the centre, for 20 min. Arrange pans so all four fit on one rack with at least 3 cm between them; record each position (e.g. front-left). Check with an oven thermometer.
3. **Temperatures.** Before each variant, probe the butter: start only when it is **18–20 °C**. Record kitchen temperature.
4. **Cream** the butter for 30 s, add the sugar, and start the timer. Cream at your fixed medium speed for exactly the variant's time (**1, 3, 6 or 10 min**), scraping down at every 2 min mark (for C1, once at the end). At the end: record the mixture's temperature and describe it (colour, volume, texture).
5. **Eggs.** Add the 102 g egg in **6 additions**, beating **30 s** after each. Note if and when it curdles; do not attempt a rescue (it is data).
6. **Flour.** Add the flour + salt in **2 additions**, **15 s** at low speed each, then 3 spatula strokes.
7. **Measure** batter temperature and SG immediately. Return the batter in the cup to the bowl with 2 gentle folds.
8. **Pan** 350 g of batter into its pan; level; cover loosely with film. Note the clock time.
9. **Repeat** for the next variant. Any order is fine; record it. Hold the filled pans at room temperature (about 20–22 °C) and **bake all four together within 45 min** of the first. Without baking powder, the batters change little while standing; the SG you measured at step 7 is the main aeration record.
10. **Bake** all four at 165 °C. Start checking at 30 min. Pull **each pan when its centre reads 96 °C** (write down its time). If one pan is clearly behind, rotate positions at 25 min, not before.
11. **Cool** 10 min in the pans, unmould, cool on a rack for 1.5 h. Weigh each cake.
12. **Cut** each cake vertically through the centre. Photograph the four cut faces side by side with a ruler and the labels.

## Analysis questions

1. How did SG change with creaming time? Where did the curve flatten or turn?
2. How closely did cake height follow SG? Plot height against SG: is it roughly a straight line?
3. What happened to the mixture temperature over 10 min? Did the C10 batter behave differently at the egg stage?
4. Which cake had the finest crumb, and which the most even? Were they the tallest?
5. Recommend a creaming time for this batch size with your mixer, with a tolerance, and justify it with numbers.
6. How would you expect the results to change if the formula contained 1 % baking powder?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**SG falls steeply, then flattens.** Typical values with a hand mixer: C1 around 0.95–1.05 (almost no aeration), C3 around 0.88–0.95, C6 around 0.8–0.88, C10 similar to C6 or slightly higher. Your exact numbers depend on mixer, bowl and butter, but the shape of the curve is the result.

**Height follows SG.** C1 is clearly the lowest (often 20–30 % lower than C6), dense, with a tight, slightly greasy, pudding-like crumb and a pale, flat top that may not split. C3 is better but still close-textured. C6 is the tallest, with a fine, even crumb and a proper split. C10 is similar to C6 or slightly worse.

**Temperature is why C10 does not keep improving.** Friction warms the creamed mixture by several degrees over 10 min; as the butter approaches 23–25 °C its crystal network softens and can no longer hold more cells. Cells coalesce into fewer, larger ones, so C10 can be slightly coarser than C6, and it is more likely to curdle at the egg stage.

**The lesson.** With no leavener, the crumb and volume are set entirely in the mixer: the oven can only enlarge the cells creaming made ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md), [10.2](stage-1/module-10/lesson-02.md)). There is an optimum window (here around 4–6 min), bounded below by too few cells and above by the butter warming out of its plastic range. With 1 % baking powder, all four cakes would be taller and the differences smaller in height, but C1 would still have a visibly coarser, less even crumb, because the extra gas goes into the few large cells it had.

</details>

## Going further

- Repeat C6 with butter at 14, 18 and 24 °C to separate the effect of temperature from time.
- Repeat with caster sugar vs coarse granulated vs icing sugar at 6 min.
- Replace half the butter with shortening and compare the SG curve (shortening holds more air over a wider temperature range).
- Stage 3 treats creaming as a process parameter: SG targets and tolerances by fat type and mixer, used as a specification in production.
