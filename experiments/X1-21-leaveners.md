# X1-21 · Baking soda vs baking powder vs both

## Question

In the same buttermilk muffin batter, how do no leavener, baking soda only, baking powder only, and soda plus powder differ in rise, crumb, crust colour, crumb colour, taste and batter pH? And what does an excess of soda do?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Rank the four variants (none, soda, powder, both) from tallest to flattest. Why?
- Which variant will have the darkest crust? The palest? Which will taste tangiest?
- Predict the batter pH of each variant relative to the others (lowest to highest).
- If you run the optional excess-soda variant, list three things you expect to see or taste.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Leavening system: **A** none · **B** soda 0.7 g (≈ neutralizes the buttermilk acid) · **C** baking powder 4.0 g · **D** soda 0.7 g + baking powder 2.0 g · optional **E** soda 1.5 g (about double the neutral amount) |
| **Dependent** | Muffin height; dome shape; crumb cell size and evenness; crust colour; crumb colour; taste (tang, soapy/metallic, bitterness); tenderness; batter pH |
| **Controlled** | One master wet mix (same buttermilk, egg, oil); same flour, sugar and salt per variant; same number of folds (12); same portion (85 g); same tin, oven, position pattern and bake; time from mixing to oven recorded; same cooling time before evaluation; same tasters |

**Why 0.7 g soda:** 90 g buttermilk at ~0.8 % lactic acid contains ~0.72 g acid, which neutralizes 0.72 × 0.93 ≈ 0.67 g soda ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)). B and C release roughly similar amounts of CO₂ (0.7 g soda ≈ 0.37 g CO₂; 4 g powder ≈ 0.48 g). D has more total gas; that is how buttermilk formulas are usually built.

**Master wet mix (enough for 5 variants plus bowl losses):** 495 g cultured buttermilk + 137.5 g whole egg (beat 3 eggs, then weigh) + 165 g neutral oil = 797.5 g, the same 90 : 25 : 30 ratio. Whisk until uniform, at 18–22 °C. Make the full amount even if you run only four variants.

**Total ingredients used for A–D** (with E in brackets): flour 400 g (500), sugar 160 g (200), salt 4 g (5), master wet mix 580 g (725), soda 1.4 g (2.9), baking powder 6.0 g.

**Per-variant yield:** ~286–290 g batter → 3 muffins of 85 g, plus ~30 g for the pH slurry.

**Scale ×2.5:** 250 g flour per variant, 100 g sugar, 2.5 g salt, 362.5 g wet mix (225 buttermilk, 62.5 egg, 75 oil); soda 1.75 g (B, D), powder 10 g (C) / 5 g (D). Each variant then fills 8 cups; bake one variant per tin or two per tin in alternating columns.

## Procedure

1. **Test your powder** first: 4 g in 50 g water at 60 °C must foam vigorously ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)). Note the brand and acid(s) on the label.
2. **Oven:** rack in the middle, **200 °C (390 °F)** constant, preheated ≥20 min, confirmed with an oven thermometer. A constant temperature is used here so that the leavener, not the oven profile, drives the dome.
3. **Tin:** line a 12-cup tin (3 rows × 4 columns) with paper liners. Label the liners or use a map: **one variant per column** (A, B, C, D from left to right), so each variant gets one front, one middle and one back cup. For E, use 3 cups of a second tin or 3 ramekins on the same rack.
4. **Dry bowls:** weigh flour, sugar and salt into 4 (or 5) bowls labelled A–E. Weigh each leavener on a 0.1 g scale and **sift it with 20 g of that bowl's flour**, then whisk each bowl for 20 s.
5. **Mix in sequence, one variant at a time.** Start a timer at the first variant. For each: add 145 g master wet mix, fold **12 times** (stroke as in [08.3](stage-1/module-08/lesson-03.md)), take **10 g** batter into a labelled cup for pH, then portion **85 g** into each of the variant's 3 cups. Record the clock time when each variant is mixed. Aim for **≤ 90 s per variant**, so the last variant is filled within ~6 min of the first. Mixing order A → B → C → D (→ E).
6. **Bake all together** at 200 °C for **18–21 min**, until the leavened variants spring back and read 93–97 °C inside. A will look underbaked and pale: probe it too and bake until it reaches 93 °C (it may need 2–3 min more; if so, record it and remove the others on time).
7. **pH (during the bake):** add 90 g distilled water to each 10 g batter sample, stir 30 s, wait 5 min, stir again, and measure with a calibrated meter or narrow-range strips ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)).
8. **Cool** 5 min in the tin, then 30 min on a rack. Keep the three cups of each variant together with their labels.
9. **Measure and cut** (below). Taste blind: a helper codes the muffins with numbers so tasters do not know which is which.
10. **Replicate if you can:** on a second day, mix in the reverse order (D → C → B → A) to check that mixing order was not the cause of any difference.

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the variants by mean height. Did the ranking match your hypothesis? Where not, propose a mechanism.
2. How much did the no-leavener muffin rise at all? What produced that rise?
3. Compare B and C, which release roughly similar amounts of gas. Which rose more, and why might the timing of gas release explain it?
4. Order the variants by batter pH, then by crust colour. Is there a relationship? Explain it with Maillard chemistry.
5. Which variant tasted tangiest? Which tasted soapy or metallic? Relate each to unneutralized acid or base.
6. If you ran E, list every difference between B and E. Which would a customer notice first?
7. Which variant would you choose for a production muffin that must wait 30 min between mixing and baking? Why?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Height.** Typically D ≥ C > B >> A. D has the most total gas; C and D both have a slow acid that releases gas in the oven when the batter can use it. B releases all its gas on mixing, so some escapes before and during portioning; it usually rises reasonably but less than C if there is any delay. A rises only from air folded in, thermal expansion and steam: expect about half the height of C, a flat or slightly sunken top, and a dense, compact, sometimes gummy crumb.

**Batter pH (approximate; your buttermilk and meter will shift the numbers).** A is the most acidic (roughly pH 5–5.5, the buttermilk acid unneutralized and buffered by flour and egg). C is slightly higher (baking powder is roughly neutral overall, so the buttermilk acid is still mostly present). D and B are higher again (the soda has neutralized much of the lactic acid), around pH 6.5–7. E is alkaline, around pH 7.5–8 or above.

**Colour.** Crust colour tracks pH: A and C palest, B and D more golden, E dark brown. E's crumb is noticeably **yellowish** (flour flavonoids at high pH) and may show darker specks if the soda was not fully dispersed.

**Taste.** A and C are tangiest (free lactic acid). B and D are mild and rounded. E tastes **soapy, metallic or bitter**, with a lingering alkaline aftertaste: sodium carbonate from soda that found no acid ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)).

**Crumb.** C and D: fine to medium, even cells, tender. B: similar or slightly coarser. E: coarse, open, sometimes crumbly, with larger irregular cells, because high pH weakens the gluten and egg proteins and the cells over-expand. A: dense, tight, gummy near the base.

**The lessons.** (1) Soda is a partner, not a leavener: without acid it gives colour and off-flavours, not lift. (2) Gas timing matters as much as gas amount: slow-acid powder keeps working after the wait. (3) Leavener choice sets pH, and pH sets colour, flavour and crumb, so you can tune a buttermilk formula between tangy and pale (more powder) and mellow and golden (more soda) while keeping the lift constant. For a batter that must wait, choose C or D.

</details>

## Going further

- Hold a second set of B and C batters for 30 min at room temperature before baking and compare with the immediate bake: bench tolerance made visible.
- Replace the buttermilk with milk in B only: soda with no acid source. Compare with E.
- Add 30 g blueberries to each variant: watch the berry colour change from red-purple (A, C) to blue-green-grey (E) ([SC-05](stage-1/science/sc-05-acids-bases-ph.md)).
- **Stage 3** version: build your own leavening system from soda plus individual acids (cream of tartar, citric acid, SAPP if you can source it) using neutralizing values, and measure the gas-release curve by timing the rise of batter in a graduated cylinder at 25 °C and 60 °C.
