# X1-13 · Preferment comparison: straight vs poolish vs pâte fermentée vs biga

## Question

With the same overall formula (flour, water and salt identical, and the same final-dough yeast), how does fermenting 30 % of the flour in advance as a poolish, a pâte fermentée or a biga change fermentation time, dough extensibility, crumb, crust colour, flavour and staling after 2 days, compared with a straight dough?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

In your lab notebook, before mixing anything, write down for each of the four variants:

- Will its bulk be faster or slower than the straight dough, and by roughly how much?
- Rank the four by expected extensibility at shaping.
- Rank them by expected crust colour.
- Which will taste most different from the straight dough, and how?
- Which will be least stale on day 2?

Give a one-line mechanism for each prediction, using [05.1](stage-1/module-05/lesson-01.md).

## Batch and scale

**Yeast solution:** dissolve 1.0 g instant yeast in 99 g water at ~25 °C (1 % solution; 1 g of solution contains 0.01 g yeast). Make it fresh the evening you mix the preferments, and count it as water.

### Preferments (mix the evening before, 12–16 h ahead)

| Preferment | Flour | Plain water | 1 % yeast solution | Yeast contained | Salt | Total | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poolish (100 %, 0.1 %) | 60 | 54 | 6 | 0.06 | — | 120 | 12–16 h at 20–22 °C |
| Biga (60 %, 0.2 %) | 60 | 24 | 12 | 0.12 | — | 96 | 12–16 h at 16–20 °C if you have a cooler spot; otherwise alongside the poolish |
| Pâte fermentée (70 %, 0.5 %, 2 % salt) | 60 | 12 | 30 | 0.30 | 1.2 | 103.2 | 1 h at room temperature, then 11–15 h at 3–5 °C; take out 1 h before use |

Weights in grams. Hydration and yeast % are on preferment flour.

| Instant yeast | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 |

Check for every column: flour 200, water 140 (70 %), salt 4.0 (2 %). Every dough totals 345.0 g; the preferment yeast is inside the solution weight, which is counted as water. Weigh 1.0 g yeast on a 0.01–0.1 g scale.

**Scale-up ×2.5:** 500 g flour per variant (one full-size loaf each, ~862 g): multiply every number above by 2.5 (for example, poolish 150 g flour, 135 g water, 15 g solution; final dough 350 g flour, 200 g water, 10 g salt, 2.5 g yeast). Bulk and bake times then match R1-01 rather than the small-loaf times below.

## Procedure

**Evening (day 0).**
1. Make the yeast solution. Mix the three preferments in labelled, marked, straight-sided containers (poolish P, pâte fermentée F, biga B). Stir the poolish to a batter. Knead the biga briefly until no dry flour remains: shaggy and stiff is correct. Knead the pâte fermentée 2–3 min to a smooth dough.
2. Record each preferment's temperature and the time. Put P and B at 20–22 °C (B cooler if possible), and F in the fridge after 1 h.

**Morning (day 1).** Stagger the mixing by **20 min** per variant so they don't all need attention at once. Suggested order: S (straight), P, F, B. Take F out of the fridge 1 h before its mix.

3. Photograph and describe each preferment: volume vs mark, surface, smell.
4. For each variant: combine flour and yeast; add water (and preferment, broken up in the water); mix to shaggy (1–2 min); rest 20 min; add salt; slap-and-fold 4 min by hand, the same for all. Record the dough temperature (target 24 °C: adjust water temperature per variant using the four-factor DDT from [05.2](stage-1/module-05/lesson-02.md); the pâte fermentée is cold).
5. Place each dough in a 500–750 ml straight-sided container, flatten, mark the level, label, and put all four in the same spot at 24–25 °C.
6. Fold each at 30 and 60 min after its own mix.
7. Check every 15 min and record the time each reaches **+75 %**. That is its end of bulk. **Each variant is fermented to the same endpoint, not for the same time.**
8. **Stretch test at end of bulk:** cut a 20 g piece, roll it into an 8 cm log, cover for 10 min, then hold both ends over a ruler and pull apart slowly and steadily (about 1 cm per second) until it tears. Record the length at tear. Also score the feel of the main dough: extensibility 1 (tears easily) to 5 (stretches a long way); elasticity 1 (no spring-back) to 5 (strong spring-back). Put the 20 g back with its dough.
9. Pre-shape into a round, rest 20 min, shape into a tight boule, and proof seam-up in a floured bowl or small banneton at 24–25 °C until the poke test is ready (typically 30–50 min).
10. Bake each variant as it becomes ready, **in the same position** on a stone preheated to 250 °C (480 °F), with ~100 g boiling water for steam: 12 min with steam, then vent and bake at 230 °C (445 °F) for 13–16 min, to ≥96 °C internal. Use the same total bake time for all (choose it on the first loaf and keep it). Allow the oven 10 min to recover between loaves. If two are ready together, bake them together side by side and note it.
11. Cool on a rack for 2 h. Measure height and width. Cut one half through the centre and photograph crumb and crust with a ruler and your R1-01 colour reference in shot.
12. Blind tasting on day 0 (see below). Then put the uncut halves in identical paper bags on the same shelf.
13. Day 2 (48 h after baking): compression test and blind tasting again.

### Blind tasting protocol

1. A helper assigns each loaf a random three-digit code (e.g. 418, 732, 095, 561) and records the key in a sealed note. You must not know the key.
2. The helper cuts 1 cm slices from the centre, cuts each into cubes, and serves them on plates labelled only with codes, **in a different random order for each taster**.
3. Tasters rinse with water between samples and taste each sample for aroma, crust and crumb. They record:
   - flavour intensity rank (1 = most intense);
   - preference rank (1 = most liked);
   - up to three descriptors per sample from: sweet, wheaty, nutty, yeasty, bland, buttery, tangy, winey, beery, toasty, cardboard, stale.
4. Optional **triangle test**: to check whether poolish and straight are distinguishable at all, serve each taster three samples, two of one bread and one of the other, and ask which is the odd one out. With 1 in 3 correct by chance, you need many tasters for a firm conclusion; treat a small panel as a pointer.
5. On day 2, repeat with a firmness rank (1 = softest) added.

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the variants by bulk time. Is the ranking explained by total yeast alone? If not, what else contributes?
2. Did extensibility (stretch test) follow the hydration of the preferment? Where did the pâte fermentée fall, and why?
3. Which variants were darkest? Connect this to amylase activity and residual sugar.
4. Were the flavour differences detectable blind? Which descriptors separated the variants?
5. Did staling on day 2 differ measurably? Was the difference bigger or smaller than the day-0 flavour difference?
6. Which result surprised you relative to your hypothesis, and what is your best explanation?
7. If you repeated this, which single variable would you control more tightly, and how?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

These are typical outcomes; your flour, kitchen and ripeness will shift them.

**Bulk time.** All three preferment doughs usually reach +75 % faster than the straight dough, often by 15–40 min at this scale, even though the extra yeast they carry is tiny. The ripe preferment brings a large, already active and grown yeast population, some sugar and some acidity. The pâte fermentée, which carried the most yeast, is often fastest; the biga, stiff and cool, is often slowest of the three.

**Extensibility.** Poolish dough is clearly the most extensible (longest stretch before tearing, lowest elasticity), because 30 % of its flour spent the night fully hydrated and relaxed without mechanical development. The biga dough is the most elastic and "strongest" feeling of the preferment doughs, sometimes firmer than the straight dough, reflecting the stiff preferment and its acidity. Pâte fermentée sits between them and close to the straight dough, because it is itself a developed dough.

**Crumb and volume.** Poolish often gives the most open, irregular crumb and a slightly lower, wider loaf; biga a tall loaf with a fairly open crumb; straight dough the most regular crumb. Differences at 345 g are smaller than in full-size loaves.

**Crust colour.** Preferment loaves usually colour more deeply than the straight dough, often with reddish tones, especially the poolish, from sugars released by amylases during the long preferment. A preferment that was over-ripe reverses this and bakes paler.

**Flavour.** Most panels find the straight dough blandest and most "yeasty". Poolish tends to be described as sweet, wheaty or nutty; biga as fuller, slightly tangy or winey; pâte fermentée as similar to the straight dough but rounder. At 30 % PFF the differences are real but moderate; not every taster will separate every pair, and a triangle test with a few tasters may not reach a firm result.

**Staling on day 2.** Preferment loaves are usually slightly softer and taste fresher than the straight dough, but the effect is modest, much smaller than the difference you will see with sourdough or with tangzhong ([X1-16](experiments/X1-16-tangzhong.md)). The compression test may show only a few millimetres of difference, within the scatter of the method; the blind firmness rank is often more sensitive.

**Why.** The preferments change *where and when* the same ingredients spend their time: fermentation by-products and sugars accumulate overnight, and gluten is either relaxed (poolish) or tightened (biga) before the final mix. None of this appears in the overall formula, which is why two breads with identical baker's percentages can be clearly different products.

</details>
