# X1-05 · Mixing time and gluten development

## Question

How does mixing time change gluten development (windowpane, elasticity), dough temperature, loaf volume and crumb — and how much of that development can fermentation time provide on its own?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Before mixing, write down:

- Which mixing time will give the largest loaf, and whether you expect the curve to keep rising or level off.
- How the 0-minute dough will look after 90 minutes of bulk compared with right after mixing.
- Which loaf will have the whitest crumb, and which the best flavour.
- Whether any variant will be overmixed in your equipment.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Development mixing time after incorporation: **0 / 3 / 6 / 10 minutes** at medium-low speed in a stand mixer, or **0 / 5 / 10 / 15 minutes** of hand kneading |
| Dependent | Windowpane grade; elasticity and extensibility; surface; dough temperature rise; container rise; loaf volume/height; crumb structure and colour; flavour |
| Controlled | Flour (one bag), hydration 65 %, salt 2 %, instant yeast 1 %; incorporation step (1 min low speed or 1 min hand stir); **final dough temperature 25 ± 1 °C** (by adjusting water temperature); **no stretch-and-folds during bulk** (they would add development); bulk time and temperature; shaping; pan size; proof time; bake |

<div class="callout warn">

**The confounding variable is heat.** Longer mixing warms the dough, and warmer dough ferments faster. If you do not compensate with colder water, you will be testing mixing *and* temperature. Measure and record the dough temperature of every variant.

</div>

## Batch and scale

Per variant (200 g flour):

|---|---|
| Bread flour | 800 |

**Stand mixer note:** many 4.5–5 L planetary mixers cannot knead 200 g flour — the dough rides on the hook without being worked. If that happens, use **×1.5** (300 g flour, 195 g water, 6 g salt, 3 g yeast; 504 g dough) for all four variants, or run the hand version.

**Scale ×2.5:** per variant 500 g flour, 325 g water, 10 g salt, 5 g instant yeast (840 g dough); totals 2000 g flour, 1300 g water, 40 g salt, 20 g yeast. Mixer times stay the same at ×1.5–×2.5 for a home mixer; bake time rises to 35–40 minutes in a 1 kg loaf pan.

## Equipment

Stand mixer with dough hook (or bench for hand version); 0.1 g scale; probe thermometer; four identical 1 L straight-sided containers with tape marks; four identical small loaf pans about 15 × 8 cm (the pan turns loaf volume into loaf height); timer; ruler; seeds for volume (optional).

## Procedure

**Pilot (15 minutes, the day before or first thing):** mix one extra 200 g test dough for 6 minutes at medium-low and measure the temperature rise from start to end. Divide by 6 to get your mixer's rise per minute (typically 0.5–1 °C/min for a small home batch). Use it to set water temperatures:

| Variant | Development time | Expected friction rise (example at 0.8 °C/min, + 1 °C for incorporation) | Water temperature (compute yours with the DDT method in [01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| M0 | 0 min | ~1 °C | warmest |
| M3 | 3 min | ~3.5 °C | |
| M6 | 6 min | ~6 °C | |
| M10 | 10 min | ~9 °C | coldest (may need ice water) |

Start variants **20 minutes apart** (mixing M10 takes about 12 minutes including handling), so each one has an identical timeline after mixing.

1. **Label** containers and pans M0, M3, M6, M10.
2. **Incorporate:** flour, salt and yeast in the bowl; add water at the computed temperature; mix **1 minute on the lowest speed** (or stir by hand for 1 minute) until no dry flour remains. For M0, stop here.
3. **Develop:** for M3, M6, M10, mix at **medium-low** (the speed your manual allows for dough — the same setting for all) for 3, 6 or 10 minutes. Hand version: push-and-fold kneading on an unfloured bench for 5, 10 or 15 minutes at a steady rhythm (about one push per second).
4. **Immediately:** measure dough temperature; do a windowpane test on a 20 g piece and photograph it against a window; score surface (rough / smooth / silky / wet-shiny) and elasticity. Return the piece.
5. **Bulk: 90 minutes at 24–26 °C** in the marked container, covered. **No folds.** Mark height at 45 and 90 minutes.
6. **At 90 minutes,** before shaping, repeat the windowpane test on a 20 g piece and photograph again. This measures what time added.
7. **Shape** into a log with the same number of movements, place seam-down in the greased pan. Measure dough height at the centre.
8. **Proof 50 minutes** at 24–26 °C, covered. Preheat oven to 220 °C (430 °F).
9. **Bake** all pans together (or in two pairs with the same offsets) at 220 °C for 25–30 minutes to an internal temperature of ≥ 96 °C. Unmould immediately, weigh.
10. **Cool 1 hour.** Measure height at the centre; measure volume by seed displacement if possible. Cut lengthwise; photograph the crumb side by side under the same light. Taste in a blind order.

## Analysis questions

1. Plot loaf specific volume (or height) against mixing time. Is the curve linear, levelling off, or peaking?
2. Compare windowpane grades after mixing and after bulk. How much development did 90 minutes of fermentation add to M0 compared with M10?
3. Did your dough temperatures stay within 25 ± 1 °C? If not, which results might be temperature effects rather than mixing effects?
4. Relate crumb colour and flavour to mixing time. What mechanism explains it?
5. Given these results, what mixing time would you choose for a pan sandwich loaf, and what would you do instead for an open-crumb hearth loaf?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

| | M0 | M3 | M6 | M10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| After mixing | Shaggy, rough, tears at once; pane 0 | Cohesive, rough; pane 0–1 | Smooth, some sheen; pane 1–2 | Smooth, silky, elastic, clears the bowl; pane 2–3 |
| After 90 min bulk | Much smoother than at mixing; pane 1 | pane 1–2 | pane 2 | pane 2–3 |
| Loaf (pan) | Lowest; uneven crumb with some large holes and dense zones | Better | Close to maximum | Highest or equal to M6; fine, even, soft crumb |
| Crumb colour | Creamiest | | | Whitest |
| Flavour | Most wheaty | | | Blandest |

**Why.** Mixing hydrates and aligns the gluten network and incorporates oxygen, which favours disulfide linking; a better-developed network holds gas more evenly, giving more volume and a finer, more regular crumb ([02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md)). The effect shows **diminishing returns**: most of the volume gain comes in the first minutes of real development; beyond that you gain evenness and a whiter crumb more than height.

**Time develops gluten too.** The M0 dough, left alone, becomes noticeably smoother and more extensible over 90 minutes as hydration completes and the network aligns passively, and expanding gas stretches it. Without folds this is rarely enough for a tall loaf, but it explains why no-knead and short-mix-plus-folds methods work: they trade mixer energy for time.

**Colour and flavour.** Intensive mixing incorporates more oxygen, which oxidizes yellow carotenoid pigments (whiter crumb) and some flavour-bearing lipids. This is the classical argument, developed by Calvel, for shorter mixing in French bread ([Calvel]).

**Overmixing:** at medium-low speed in a home mixer, 10 minutes rarely overmixes a 65 % bread-flour dough. If your M10 was wet, shiny and stringy, check the speed setting and whether the dough overheated (above ~28 °C).

**If temperatures drifted:** a warmer M10 would have fermented faster, exaggerating its volume advantage; a cooler one would have understated it.

</details>

## Going further

- Add a fifth variant: M0 **with** three stretch-and-folds at 30-minute intervals during bulk. Compare it with M6 — this is the core of the short-mix method ([02.4](stage-1/module-02/lesson-04.md)).
- Repeat with a 30-minute autolyse before M3 and compare with M6 without autolyse.
- Push to 15 and 20 minutes to find your mixer's overmixing point (watch dough temperature).
