# X1-33 · Custard endpoint temperature: 78 / 82 / 86 °C

## Question

How do the thickness, smoothness and flavour of a crème anglaise change when the same base is cooked to 78, 82 and 86 °C? Where exactly is the window between "thin" and "grainy" for this formula, and can the nappe test find it without a thermometer?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before cooking:

- Rank the three variants by thickness after chilling. Will the 86 °C variant be thicker or thinner than the 82 °C one? Why?
- At which temperature do you expect the nappe line to first hold clean?
- Which variant will taste most "eggy", and which will feel grainy on the tongue?
- Sketch a curve of line-spread (y) against endpoint temperature (x).

## Batch and scale

One base, mixed cold, divided into three portions. The cold-start method (all ingredients combined cold, then heated together) replaces the tempering method of [R1-24](recipes/R1-24-creme-anglaise.md) here, because it gives every portion the same heating history.

**Per variant (one 280 g portion, approximately):**

| Ingredient | % of dairy | Grams per portion |

Divide into **3 × 280 g** after straining (about 5–7 g stays in the sieve and bowl).

**Scale-up ×2.5:** milk 750 g, cream 750 g, yolks 300 g, sugar 300 g, vanilla 15 g, salt 1.5 g (2,116.5 g) → 3 × 700 g, which is closer to production conditions: larger portions heat more slowly and are more prone to a hot bottom layer, so the differences become larger.

## Procedure

1. **Mix the base.** Whisk the yolks with the sugar and salt just to combine (no foam). Whisk in the cold milk, cream and vanilla. Strain through a fine sieve into a jug. Refrigerate to **5 °C** (probe).
2. **Divide.** Weigh **3 × 280 g** into three labelled containers: **A78**, **B82**, **C86**. Keep them in the refrigerator until each one is cooked.
3. **Set up.** Three small bowls, each in its own ice bath, each with a fine sieve over it, labelled. A small heavy saucepan (16 cm); one burner; decide the heat setting (medium-low) and mark the knob. Tare and record the weight of the empty saucepan.
4. **Cook B82 first** (the reference). Pour it into the pan, record the weight of pan + custard. Heat at the marked setting, stirring continuously with a spatula in a figure-eight across the base. Start a timer. Record temperature every **30 s**.
5. **Nappe checks.** At **74, 78, 80 and 82 °C**, dip a clean spoon, draw a finger across the back, and photograph it (label with the temperature). Work fast (under 5 s each).
6. **Pull at exactly 82 °C.** Remove from the heat, weigh pan + custard immediately (weight loss), then pour at once through the sieve into the B bowl in the ice bath. Stir until below **10 °C**.
7. **Clean and cool the pan.** Rinse under cold water, dry, and let it return to room temperature (it must start each run at the same temperature).
8. **Cook A78** exactly as in steps 4–6, pulling at **78 °C** (nappe check at 74 and 78 °C).
9. **Cook C86** exactly as in steps 4–6, pulling at **86 °C** (nappe checks at 78, 82, 84 and 86 °C). Note the time at which you first see graininess on the spoon, if you do.
10. **Weigh the sieve residue** for each variant: tare the sieve before straining, weigh it after, subtract. This is the curdled material that did not pass.
11. **Chill** all three, covered with film in contact, for **at least 2 h** at ≤ 5 °C (overnight is fine; test all three at the same time).
12. **Test** at 5 °C: line-spread, then smoothness and flavour (below).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** The 78 °C variant is above the 71 °C recommended for egg dishes ([FDA-eggs]), but it has not been held long; keep all three refrigerated and eat within 2 days. Contains egg and milk.

</div>

## Analysis questions

1. At what temperature did the nappe line first hold clean? Did it agree with your thermometer target of 82–84 °C?
2. Was C86 thicker or thinner than B82 by line-spread? Did its sieve residue and smoothness change the answer to "which is better"?
3. How much did the 4 °C difference between A and B change the line-spread? What does that tell you about how tightly you must control the endpoint?
4. How long did the last 4 °C take in each run? Would a faster heating rate have made C86 better or worse?
5. If the recipe had 25 % sugar instead of 20 %, where would you expect the three curves to move?
6. Could you have identified the correct endpoint by nappe alone? What would you need to practise to do that reliably?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**A78** is thin: at the pull the surface foam is only partly gone and the nappe line blurs and closes within a few seconds. Chilled, it pours like single cream and spreads furthest, typically 15–30 mm more than B82 in this line-spread setup (the absolute numbers depend on your ring, plate and temperature). It is smooth (0), with little residue in the sieve, and it may taste slightly raw or "milky-eggy" rather than custardy.

**B82** is the reference: glossy, the foam gone, the nappe line clean and sharp. Chilled, it pours slowly, coats a plate evenly and spreads clearly less than A78. Smooth (0), negligible residue, rich custard flavour with no sulfur.

**C86** is the interesting one. Hot, it often looks thickest, and some runs show fine grain on the spoon from about 84–85 °C. After straining, the sieve holds visible residue (often a few grams, sometimes more), the texture scores 1–2, and the flavour is noticeably more "cooked egg", sometimes faintly sulfurous. Its line-spread can be similar to or slightly smaller than B82, **or larger** if a lot of protein curdled out and was strained away, leaving a thinner liquid. Either way it is worse: thickness is no longer coming from a fine network but from clumps.

**Why.** Diluted and sweetened yolk proteins build a fine, water-holding network between roughly 78 and 84 °C; the viscosity rises steeply over those few degrees, which is why A and B differ so much. Past about 85 °C the network tightens into clusters that expel water: grain, then curds. The window for this formula is about 82–84 °C; the curdling point is not a sharp line, because it depends on the heating rate and on how hot the base of the pan was. Faster heating makes the bottom layer overshoot and moves graininess to a lower probe reading. More sugar would shift all three stages up by a few degrees and widen the window ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md), [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)).

**The lesson.** Use the thermometer to get into the window, and the nappe test to confirm. 4 °C is the difference between a sauce that is too thin and one that is correct, and another 4 °C the difference between correct and grainy.

</details>

## Going further

- Add a fourth variant at **90 °C** and blend it with an immersion blender after cooling: measure whether blending brings the line-spread and smoothness back to B82 levels.
- Repeat with **100 % milk** instead of half cream, or with **15 %** and **25 %** sugar, to see the window move.
- Hold a portion at **80 °C for 5 min** (stirring, on very low heat) instead of pulling at 82 °C: time as well as temperature builds the network. Stage 2 uses this time–temperature logic for ice-cream bases and sous-vide custards; Stage 3 uses viscosity measurement (Bostwick consistometer, rheometer) and pasteurization tables to set production specifications.
