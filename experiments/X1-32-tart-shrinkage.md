# X1-32 · Resting and chilling vs tart shrinkage

## Question

How much of a tart shell's shrinkage and slumping is prevented by resting the dough before rolling, and how much more by freezing the lined shell before it goes into the oven?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Before mixing, write down for each of the three shells (no rest; 1 h rest; 1 h rest plus freezing):

- How many millimetres of wall height will it lose from a 20 mm ring?
- Will the wall pull away from the ring, and by how much?
- Which will show slumped or uneven walls?
- Which single step (resting, or freezing) do you expect to be worth more? Commit to a number for each.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Treatment before baking: **A** no rest (mixed, rolled and lined immediately, baked at once); **B** dough rested 1 h at 4 °C, lined, baked at once; **C** dough rested 1 h, lined, frozen 30 min, baked |
| **Dependent** | Wall height at 4 points (mm) and height loss (%); gap between shell and ring at 4 points (mm); slumping (visual score); base doming; shell appearance |
| **Controlled** | Formula (R1-20 brisée) and ingredient lots; butter temperature at start; mixing method and time; rolled thickness (3 mm guides); identical rings; lining technique; trim flush with the ring; docking pattern; weights (same type, filled to the rim); oven, tray, position, temperature and times |

**Equipment note:** you need **three identical rings or pans** (for example three 16 cm × 2 cm tart rings, or three 10 cm tartlet rings, or three identical fluted tartlet pans). If you have only one, run the variants on three separate days with the oven measured each time, and treat the results with caution.

## Batch and scale

The three variants are mixed as three separate, identical batches so that each can be timed correctly.

**Scale-up ×2.5** (one 22–24 cm shell per variant, as in R1-20): per variant 375 g flour, 225 g butter, 7.5 g salt, 30 g yolk, 75 g water (712.5 g).

## Procedure

Each batch is mixed exactly as in [R1-20](recipes/R1-20-pate-brisee.md) steps 1–5 (rub in to coarse crumbs, add yolk–water, fraisage once). Use the same timings for each; record dough temperature at the end of each mix.

**Timeline (all three shells go into the oven together at about t = 95 min):**

| Time (min) | Action |
|---|---|
| 0 | Mix batch **C**. Disc 2 cm, wrap, refrigerate. |
| 25 | Mix batch **B**. Disc 2 cm, wrap, refrigerate. |
| 60 | Roll **C** to 3 mm, line ring C, trim flush, dock. Put in the **freezer** (on its tray or a small board). |
| 70 | Preheat oven to **180 °C (355 °F)** still, 170 °C fan, with a heavy tray on the lower-middle rack. |
| 80 | Mix batch **A**. Roll immediately to 3 mm (it will be soft: use parchment), line ring A, trim flush, dock. |
| 85 | Roll **B** (rested 60 min) to 3 mm, line ring B, trim flush, dock. |
| 90–95 | Line all three with parchment, fill **to the rim** with weights. Load A, B and C together on the preheated tray (C straight from the freezer). |

**Before loading**, for each ring: record ring height H (mm) and mark four points on the parchment beside the ring as N, E, S, W (a pencil mark or a small notch), so that you measure the same places after baking. Record dough surface temperature of each shell with an IR or probe thermometer (A and B will be around 14–18 °C, C around −5 to 0 °C).

**Bake:** **18 minutes** weighted; remove paper and weights from all three at the same time; bake **8 minutes** more unweighted. Remove all together. Cool 10 minutes in the rings on the tray.

## Measurements

| What | How | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Dough temperature | End of each mix; surface of each lined shell at loading | Probe or IR thermometer |
| Wall height | At N, E, S, W, measured from the tray (outside of the wall) to the top of the wall, before removing the ring | Ruler or calipers, 0.5 mm |
| Height loss | (H − mean wall height) ÷ H × 100 | Calculation |
| Ring gap | Gap between the outside of the shell wall and the ring at N, E, S, W, just below the rim | Feeler: a stack of cards of known thickness, or calipers |

| Base doming | Maximum height of any dome above the base (mm) | Ruler |
| Appearance | Even colour, cracks, blisters | Photo next to a white card |

## Analysis questions

1. Calculate mean height loss and ring gap for each shell. How much did resting (A → B) reduce height loss? How much did freezing add (B → C)?
2. Compare the spread of the four readings within each shell. Was shrinkage uniform or concentrated on one side? What in your lining could cause that?
3. A was also warmer at loading than B. Can you separate the effect of gluten relaxation from the effect of butter temperature with this design? What extra variant would do it?
4. Did any shell slump rather than shrink? Relate slumping to butter temperature at loading.
5. For a busy morning with limited freezer space, which treatment would you drop first, and what would it cost you in millimetres?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**A (no rest)** is typically the worst: wall height losses of several millimetres from a 20 mm ring (often 3–6 mm, 15–30 %), uneven around the ring, a visible gap between the shell and the ring (1–3 mm), and often some slumping where the soft walls slid under the weights. It was also the hardest to roll: soft, sticky, springy.

**B (rested 1 h)** is clearly better: losses of about 1–3 mm and a smaller ring gap, but still some shrinkage because the shell went into the oven at 14–18 °C with its butter already soft.

**C (rested + frozen)** is typically the best: under about 1 mm loss, walls straight and tight against the ring, sharp corner. Freezing is often worth as much as, or more than, the rest alone.

**Why.** Rolling and lining stretch the gluten that exists and store elastic strain. Resting lets that strain relax and lets the dough hydrate evenly and the butter firm ([11.2](stage-1/module-11/lesson-02.md)). But the recoil happens in the oven, during the window after the butter melts and before egg and starch set the structure. A frozen shell keeps its butter solid for several extra minutes while the surface heats and starts to set, so there is little time for the strands to pull back or for the walls to slide. Resting reduces the driving force; freezing shortens the time it can act.

**On question 3.** A differs from B in two ways: less gluten relaxation *and* warmer butter at loading. To separate them, add a variant **A′**: mixed without rest, lined, then frozen 30 minutes before baking (same temperature at loading as C, no relaxation). A′ vs C isolates the rest; A′ vs A isolates the freezing.

**If your results differ.** If all three shrank badly, suspect the formula or mixing (too much water, overworked) or stretched lining. If none shrank, your dough was very short (low water, well rubbed in) or your walls were supported by weights until fully set; repeat with 25 % water to make the effect visible.

</details>

## Going further

- Run the A′ variant described in the answer to separate relaxation from temperature.
- Repeat with [R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md) sucrée: is a low-water, sugar-rich dough as sensitive?
- **Rest-time series:** 0, 20 min, 1 h, overnight, all frozen before baking; plot height loss against rest time.
- **No weights:** bake frozen shells of sucrée in perforated rings on a perforated mat without weights (Stage 2 production practice).
- Stage 3 treats height loss as a process-capability metric: measure 10 shells per condition and calculate mean and standard deviation against a ±1 mm specification ([SC-14](stage-1/science/sc-14-measurement-sensory.md)).
