# X1-03 · Gluten wash: protein content across flours

## Question

How much gluten do cake, all-purpose and bread flour actually contain, how does it behave in your hands, and does the label protein predict it?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

In your lab notebook, before mixing anything, write down:

- The protein per 100 g printed on each of your three flours.
- Your predicted wet gluten weight from 100 g of each flour, and the order from least to most.
- What you expect the three raw gluten balls to feel like when stretched, and how much you expect each to expand when baked.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Flour: cake (or the lowest-protein flour available), all-purpose / "white", bread flour |
| Dependent | Wet gluten weight; estimated dry gluten; feel (elasticity, extensibility, cohesion); washing time; baked ball size and structure |
| Controlled | Flour weight (100.0 g); water weight (60 g) and temperature (~20 °C); kneading time (4 min); rest (20 min); washing water temperature (~18–22 °C); squeezing procedure before weighing; oven temperature and bake time |

## Batch and scale

Per variant (one flour):

| Ingredient | Grams | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100.0 | 100 % |
| Water, ~20 °C | 60.0 | 60 % |
| **Dough** | **160.0** | 160 % |

No salt: salt tightens gluten and changes the washing behaviour. Keep all three variants identical.

| Total ingredients (3 flours) | Grams |
|---|---|
| Flour (100 g of each of three) | 300 |
| Water | 180 |

**Scale ×2.5** (larger, easier-to-handle gluten balls; more accurate weighing): 250 g flour + 150 g water per variant; 750 g flour and 450 g water for all three. Divide the wet gluten weight by 2.5 before comparing with 100 g results.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Raw flour and raw dough can carry pathogens ([FDA-flour]). Do not taste the dough or the raw gluten. Wash hands, sieve, bowls and the sink area afterwards. The baked gluten ball is cooked but is not food — discard it.

</div>

## Equipment

Scale with 0.1 g resolution; three bowls; fine-mesh sieve; a large bowl or basin to catch wash water; kitchen timer; ruler or calipers; permanent marker and tape; baking tray with parchment; oven thermometer; optional: tincture of iodine (pharmacy antiseptic) and a white saucer, one glass jar per flour.

## Procedure

1. **Label** three bowls and three pieces of parchment: C (cake), A (all-purpose), B (bread). Record each flour's brand, protein per 100 g, and ingredients.
2. **Mix** (start variants 5 minutes apart so each gets the same rest): weigh 100.0 g flour and 60.0 g water. Stir until no dry flour remains, then knead 4 minutes into a smooth ball. Cake flour will be softer and stickier — knead it in the bowl if necessary; do not add flour or water.
3. **Rest** 20 minutes, covered, or submerged in a bowl of water at ~20 °C (submersion starts hydration of the surface and makes washing easier). Start a timer per variant.
4. **Wash:** hold the dough over a sieve set in a large bowl. Under a thin stream of cool water (or in a bowl of water you change every 2–3 minutes), squeeze, stretch and knead the dough with your fingers. The water turns milky with starch. At first the dough may threaten to fall apart — keep it compact, work gently, and return any pieces caught in the sieve.
5. **Endpoint:** continue until the wash water runs almost clear when you squeeze the ball, typically 8–15 minutes. Optional check: squeeze a few drops of wash water onto a white saucer and add a drop of iodine; blue-black means starch is still coming out, yellow-brown means you are done.
6. Record the washing time for each flour.
7. **Save starch (optional):** pour the milky wash water from the first 2 minutes into a labelled jar and let it stand 1 hour. The starch settles as a white layer; photograph the jars side by side.
8. **Squeeze** the gluten firmly between your palms for 10 seconds, then roll it on a dry towel for 5 seconds. Use the same squeeze for all three, because surface water adds weight.
9. **Weigh** the wet gluten to 0.1 g. Record.
10. **Feel test:** stretch each ball slowly between your hands. Note how far it stretches before tearing, how strongly it snaps back, and whether it is cohesive, stringy or crumbly. Photograph.
11. **Shape** each into a ball, measure its diameter and height with a ruler or calipers, place on labelled parchment on a tray at least 8 cm apart.
12. **Bake** at 230 °C (450 °F) for 25–30 minutes until firm and golden brown, then reduce to 150 °C (300 °F) for 10–15 minutes to dry out so they do not collapse on cooling. Do not open the oven in the first 15 minutes.
13. **Measure** baked diameter and height. Cool 20 minutes, then cut each ball in half with a serrated knife and photograph the interior.

| What | How | Tool |
|---|---|---|

| Washing time | Start to clear-water endpoint | Timer |
| Cohesion, elasticity, extensibility | Stretch test; score 1–5 each | Hands, ruler for stretch length |
| Expansion | Baked ÷ raw diameter; baked ÷ raw height | Ruler/calipers |

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the flours by wet gluten. Does the ranking match the label protein? Does the *ratio* of wet gluten to label protein stay constant?
2. Compare your estimated dry gluten with label protein. Which is larger, and name three reasons they differ.
3. Which gluten was most elastic, which most extensible? Relate this to glutenin and gliadin ([02.2](stage-1/module-02/lesson-02.md)).
4. Why does a baked gluten ball expand with no yeast at all? What gas is doing the work?
5. Which flour would you choose for a baguette, a pie crust and a sponge cake based on this experiment alone? What else would you need to know?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Typical numbers.** Home washing is less efficient than a laboratory gluten washer, so expect ranges rather than exact values:

| Flour (label protein) | Wet gluten per 100 g | Estimated dry gluten |
|---|---|---|
| Cake flour (~7–9 %) | ~15–22 g, sometimes only fragments | ~5–7 g |
| All-purpose (~10–11 %) | ~24–30 g | ~8–10 g |
| Bread flour (~12–13.5 %) | ~30–38 g | ~10–13 g |

**Ranking** usually follows label protein, but the ratio is not constant: stronger flours often give disproportionately more gluten, because weaker flours lose more fragments in the wash and not all protein is gluten-forming (roughly 75–85 % is).

**Dry gluten vs label protein.** Your estimate is usually somewhat lower than label protein: non-gluten proteins (albumins, globulins) dissolve and wash away; small gluten pieces are lost; "÷ 3" is an approximation (true water content of wet gluten varies around 60–70 %); and the label may use a different moisture basis and nitrogen factor ([02.1](stage-1/module-02/lesson-01.md)). Sometimes the estimate is higher, when starch remains trapped in an incompletely washed ball — a sign to wash longer.

**Feel.** Bread-flour gluten is cohesive, rubbery, springs back strongly and stretches into a long, thin sheet. All-purpose is similar but softer and tears sooner. Cake-flour gluten is soft, weak, may be sticky or crumble apart and hardly springs back; chlorinated US cake flour can be especially hard to collect.

**Baking.** Water in the gluten turns to steam at the surface of a closed, elastic network; the network stretches like a balloon, then sets as the proteins coagulate. Expect bread-flour balls to roughly double in diameter (several times in volume) into a hollow, honeycombed shell with a crisp crust; all-purpose balls a little less; cake-flour balls expand least and may crack or stay dense. The larger, stronger network holds more steam before rupturing — the same mechanism as oven spring in bread ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)).

**The lesson:** protein on the label predicts gluten *quantity* approximately; the stretch test shows gluten *quality*, which the number cannot. That is why bakers test a new flour rather than trusting its name.

</details>

## Going further

- Wash a whole-wheat flour: note how bran particles stay trapped and how weak the ball is relative to its protein.
- Wash two bread flours with the same label protein from different brands; compare quality, not just quantity.
- Wash 100 g of bread flour with 2 g salt in the dough and compare feel and yield with the salt-free wash (link to [X1-06](experiments/X1-06-salt.md)).
- Dry a gluten ball fully in a 100 °C oven for 2–3 hours, weigh it, and check your "÷ 3" estimate.
