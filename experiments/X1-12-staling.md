# X1-12 · Staling: room, refrigerator, freezer, reheating

## Question

How does storage temperature change the rate at which lean bread firms over four days, is that firming caused by water loss or by something else, and can it be reversed by reheating?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before baking:

- Rank the four storage conditions (room sealed, refrigerator sealed, freezer sealed, room unwrapped) from softest to firmest on day 4.
- Predict the weight change (%) of the sealed room-temperature sample and of the unwrapped sample by day 4.
- Predict what reheating a stale sample will do to its firmness, and what will happen 2 hours later.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Storage condition: A room temperature, sealed bag; B refrigerator (3–5 °C), sealed bag; C freezer (−18 °C), sealed bag; D room temperature, unwrapped (drying control). Plus R: a B sample reheated on day 4 |
| **Dependent** | Crumb firmness (force to compress a cube by 50 %); weight change %; crust texture; taste and mouthfeel scores; mould |
| **Controlled** | One loaf, one bake; sample size (half-slices of the same thickness); position in loaf (samples assigned in rotation, see step 5); bag type; evaluation method and evaluator; time of day of evaluation; samples brought to room temperature before testing |

## Batch and scale

One lean pan loaf gives uniform slices, which makes comparisons fair.

**Formula (one loaf):**

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100 | 400 |
| Water | 66 | 264 |
| Fine salt | 2 | 8 |
| Instant dry yeast | 1 | 4 |
| **Total** | **169** | **676** |

There is a single variant: the variable is applied after baking, so the per-variant formula is the whole loaf. Pan: 21 × 11 cm loaf pan, lightly oiled.

**Scale-up ×2.5:** flour 1,000 g, water 660 g, salt 20 g, yeast 10 g (1,690 g) → two loaves of 845 g in 23 × 13 cm pans. Use the second loaf to run every condition in duplicate, which lets you see how much of the difference is real and how much is sample-to-sample noise.

## Procedure

**Day 0: bake**

1. Mix to DDT 25 °C, moderate-to-full development. Bulk 60–90 min at 24–26 °C until about 1.75×. Shape as a pan loaf ([R1-02](recipes/R1-02-rolls-pan-loaf.md)), proof until the dough crowns about 1.5 cm above the rim.
2. Bake at 220 °C (430 °F), steam for the first 10 min, 35–40 min total, to an internal temperature of 97–98 °C. Demould at once. Record dough weight and baked weight.
3. Cool on a rack for **3 hours** (a pan loaf must be fully cool before slicing, and all samples must start at the same state).
4. Remove about 1.5 cm from each end. Slice the loaf into **6 slices, 25 mm thick** (use a ruler and mark the crust with a knife tip first). Cut each slice in half across its width: **12 half-slices**, numbered 1 to 12 from one end.
5. **Assign samples in rotation** so no condition gets all the end pieces:

| Sample | Half-slice numbers | Stored as | Tested on |
|---|---|---|---|
| F (fresh baseline) | 6 | — | Day 0, at once |
| A1, A2, A4 | 1, 5, 9 | A room, sealed | Days 1, 2, 4 |
| B1, B2, B4 | 2, 7, 10 | B refrigerator, sealed | Days 1, 2, 4 |
| D1, D2, D4 | 3, 8, 11 | D room, unwrapped, on a rack | Days 1, 2, 4 |
| C4 | 4 | C freezer, sealed | Day 4 |
| R4 | 12 | B refrigerator, sealed, then reheated | Day 4 |

6. **Weigh** every half-slice (0.1 g if possible) and record. Place each in its own zip bag, press out the air and seal (except D samples, which sit on a rack on the counter, cut faces exposed). Label with sample code. Put B and R samples in the refrigerator, C in the freezer, A and D at room temperature away from the oven and sunlight. Record room and refrigerator temperatures.
7. **Test F now** (see Measurements).

**Days 1, 2 and 4** (same time of day as the bake ± 1 h)

8. Take out that day's samples 30 min before testing. Leave refrigerated samples sealed while they warm to room temperature, so condensation forms on the bag, not the bread. Freezer sample C4: take it out 60 min before, still sealed.
9. Weigh each sample, then test firmness, crust and sensory as below.

**Day 4: reheating**

10. Test B4 as normal. Then take R4, bake it on a rack at **180 °C (355 °F)** for **4–5 minutes**, until the crumb is hot to the touch in the centre (above 60 °C if you can probe it).
11. Let R4 stand 5 minutes, then cut two cubes and test firmness and taste (**R4-warm**).
12. Leave the rest of R4 on the rack, uncovered, for **2 hours**, then cut two more cubes and test again (**R4-2h**).

## Measurements

**Firmness: compression force at 50 % strain.** This is a kitchen version of the texture analysers used in bread research.

1. From the centre of the half-slice, cut two **25 mm crumb cubes** (no crust).
2. Put the cube on the kitchen scale with a **12–13 mm spacer** beside it (a stack of coins or a small block). Tare.
3. Press down on the cube with a flat, rigid lid at least 5 cm across, slowly and steadily over about 3 seconds, until the lid touches the spacer (the cube is now at half its height).
4. Record the **peak** reading in grams. If your scale has no peak-hold, film the display with your phone and read the peak. Average the two cubes.

| What | How | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Firmness | Peak force (g) to compress a 25 mm cube to 12.5 mm | Scale, spacer, flat lid |
| Recovery | Does the cube spring back to full height within 30 s? (full / partial / none) | Ruler |
| Weight change | (day-0 weight − test-day weight) ÷ day-0 weight × 100 | Scale |
| Crust | crisp / leathery / soft / hard-dry | Bend and bite |
| Sensory | Score 1–5 for softness, moistness (mouthfeel), flavour freshness; note words | Taste, water between samples |
| Mould | Present / absent; day first seen | Look, do not sniff closely |

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the conditions by firmness on day 2 and day 4. Did the ranking match your hypothesis?
2. Compare the weight change of A and B with that of D. Can water loss explain the firming of A and B? What does?
3. D is both drying and staling. How would you separate the two effects using your data?
4. How close was C4 to F? What does that tell you about staling at −18 °C?
5. How much did reheating reduce the firmness of R4 relative to B4? How much had it recovered after 2 hours, and how does that compare with B4?
6. What would you now recommend for storing a lean loaf you will eat over three days?
7. Estimate how many days at room temperature the refrigerated sample's day-1 firmness corresponds to.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**Refrigerator (B) firms fastest.** By day 1 it is typically already firmer than the room-temperature sample (A) will be on day 2; by day 4 it is the firmest sealed sample by a clear margin, often two to three times the fresh firmness or more. Its crumb is crumbly, and it tastes dry despite having lost almost no weight.

**Room sealed (A)** firms steadily: noticeably by day 2, clearly stale by day 4. Its weight change is small, usually well under 2 %. That is the key evidence: **sealed samples firm a lot while losing little water**, so the firming is starch retrogradation, not drying. The crust of A goes soft or leathery within a day as moisture migrates into it.

**Unwrapped (D)** loses much more weight (commonly 5–15 % by day 4, depending on humidity) and becomes hard and dry at the cut faces; its firmness is the highest overall because drying and staling add together. Its crust is hard rather than leathery. To separate the effects, compare D with A: the extra firmness of D over A is mostly due to drying.

**Freezer (C4)** is close to fresh after thawing: frozen water and very low molecular mobility stop retrogradation. Any small increase comes from the time spent passing through the 0–10 °C zone while freezing and thawing.

**Reheating (R4).** Heating above about 50–60 °C melts the amylopectin crystals: R4-warm is much softer than B4, often near the fresh value, and tastes fresh. After 2 hours R4-2h has firmed again, often to close to B4 or beyond, because the starch re-crystallizes quickly from the nuclei left behind and the slice lost water in the oven. Staling is reversible, but only temporarily.

**If your results differ.** If A and B were similar, check refrigerator temperature (above 7 °C slows staling) and whether samples were warmed to the same temperature before testing (cold crumb is firmer by itself). If D was not firmer than A, your kitchen was humid; note the relative humidity if you can.

</details>
