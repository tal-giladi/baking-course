# X1-28 · Cake flour vs all-purpose flour

## Question

In the same butter cake, how do all-purpose flour, the lowest-protein "cake" flour you can buy, and all-purpose flour with 18 % cornstarch (the common home substitute) differ in cake height, top shape, crumb fineness, tenderness and colour? Is the substitute a real substitute?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before mixing:

- Copy the protein content (g per 100 g) from each flour's label, and note whether the cake flour is described as bleached or chlorinated.
- Predict the order of the three cakes by height, by dome, by tenderness and by crumb whiteness.
- Predict whether AP + cornstarch will behave more like the AP or more like the cake flour, and why.

| Type | Variable |

## Batch and scale

The educational butter-cake formula from [X1-27](experiments/X1-27-reverse-creaming.md), mixed by conventional creaming.

**Per variant:**

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Flour (variant; AC = 82 g AP + 18 g cornstarch) | 100.0 | 100 |
| Caster sugar | 100.0 | 100 |
| Baking powder (double-acting) | 3.0 | 3 |
| Fine salt | 1.0 | 1 |
| Unsalted butter, 18–20 °C | 50.0 | 50 |
| Whole egg, beaten, 18–22 °C | 60.0 | 60 |
| Whole milk, 18–22 °C | 50.0 | 50 |
| Vanilla extract | 1.0 | 1 |
| **Total** | **365.0** | **365** |

Each variant fills one **15 cm round pan** (at least 5 cm deep, base lined, sides greased) with **330 g** of batter.

**Total ingredients for three variants:** AP flour 182 g (100 + 82), cake flour 100 g, cornstarch 18 g, sugar 300 g, baking powder 9 g, salt 3 g, butter 150 g, egg 180 g, milk 150 g, vanilla 3 g.

**Scale-up:** ×1.78 per variant for a 20 cm round pan (about 650 g batter). ×2.5 (250 g flour, 912.5 g batter) for a 23 cm round pan.

**Where cake flour is not sold:** use a "pastry" or soft-wheat flour with the lowest protein you can find, and record its protein. Chlorinated flour is not sold in the EU or UK; in the US, most "cake flour" is chlorinated. If you can get both a chlorinated and an unchlorinated low-protein flour, add them as variants **CF-Cl** and **CF-U**: that isolates chlorination from protein ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)).

## Procedure

1. **Prepare.** Weigh three sets. For each variant sift the flour (or flour + cornstarch) with baking powder and salt, **twice** (cornstarch must be evenly dispersed). Combine egg + milk + vanilla per variant (111 g). Temper butter to **18–20 °C**. Label pans **AP, CF, AC**.
2. **Oven.** Preheat to **175 °C (345 °F)**, conventional, rack in the centre, 20 min. Place three pans on one rack in a triangle, at least 4 cm apart; record positions.
3. **Mix each variant** by the CR method of [X1-27](experiments/X1-27-reverse-creaming.md) step 3: cream butter + sugar 5 min; half the liquid in 4 additions; then dry/liquid alternately D-L-D-L-D, 10–15 s each at low; 3 spatula strokes.
4. **Measure** batter temperature and SG. Note the batter's consistency: lift a spatula and time how long a ribbon of batter takes to fall back and level (s), or describe it (thick / medium / fluid).
5. **Pan** 330 g, level. Mix the three variants back to back (about 8 min each), and keep the waiting time for the first variant under 20 min; if you cannot, mix them in a different order when you replicate.
6. **Bake** all three together. Start checking at 22 min. Pull each at a centre temperature of **96 °C** with springback; record the time.
7. **Cool** 10 min in pans, unmould, cool 1.5 h. Weigh.
8. **Cut** vertically through the centre; photograph all three together with a ruler, in daylight next to a white card (crumb colour).
9. **Compression test:** 3 cm centre cube, plate + 500 g weight for 10 s; compression % = (30 mm − compressed height) ÷ 30 mm × 100; note recovery after 30 s.
10. **Taste** blind (someone else labels the plates).

## Analysis questions

1. Rank the three by height and by dome. Is the tallest cake also the most domed?
2. Which crumb was finest and most tender? Which was chewiest?
3. Did AC behave like CF? Where did it match, and where did it differ?
4. Relate protein content to what you measured. Was protein the whole story?
5. If you live where only AP and unchlorinated flours are available, which would you use for a layer cake, and would you change the formula?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**AP** usually gives the most domed cake, sometimes with a slight peak or crack, a slightly coarser, springier, more "bready" crumb, lower compression, and a creamier colour. It is a perfectly good cake; the difference is texture.

**CF** gives a flatter top, a finer, more even, softer crumb that compresses more, and a whiter crumb. If it is chlorinated, the effect is stronger: whiter, finer, with better support of the sugar (a sturdier, flat top rather than a dip), because chlorination modifies the starch to absorb water and swell more readily in a sweet batter. An unchlorinated low-protein flour sits between AP and chlorinated cake flour. Height differences in this regular (sugar = flour) formula are often small; they become large in high-sugar formulas.

**AC** is noticeably more tender and finer than AP, and flatter-topped, because cornstarch dilutes the gluten-forming protein to roughly 8–10 %. It usually matches CF on tenderness but can be slightly more crumbly and a little lower, because cornstarch contributes starch but no protein and does not reproduce chlorination's effect on starch. It is a good substitute for regular cakes, not for high-ratio cakes.

**Protein is not the whole story:** particle size (cake flour is milled finer), starch damage, and chlorination or heat treatment all matter, which is why two flours of the same protein can bake differently.

</details>
