# X1-42 · Ganache ratio series

## Question

How does the chocolate-to-cream ratio change a dark ganache? For your chocolate, where do the textures for glazing, piping and rolling truffles fall on the ratio scale, and at what ratio does the ganache become too firm, grainy or greasy to use?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before you start:

- Sketch how you expect firmness (y) to rise with ratio (x) from 1 : 1 to 3 : 1: straight line, curve, or step?
- Which variant will be best for piping at 20 °C, and which for rolling truffles?
- Will any variant split or turn grainy? Which one, and why?
- Calculate the water % of each variant and predict at what water % the ganache stops being stable.

## Variables

| Type | Variable |
|---|---|
| **Independent** | Chocolate : cream ratio by weight: **1 : 1**, **1.5 : 1**, **2 : 1**, **3 : 1** |
| **Dependent** | Emulsion appearance at 35–40 °C (glossy / grainy / oily); line-spread when warm at 32 °C (mm); firmness after 12 h at 18–20 °C (penetration depth, mm); pipeability at 20 °C (0–3); mouthfeel (smoothness, melt, oiliness, 0–3 each); any split |
| **Controlled** | One chocolate (brand, %, lot); one cream (35 % fat, one carton); chocolate weight per variant (60 g); chocolate pre-melted to 45 °C; cream heated to 70 °C; same small bowl, same spatula, same emulsifying method and time; containers and fill depth; setting temperature and time (18–20 °C, 12 h, covered); same tasting temperature |

Dark chocolate 55–66 % (record the fat per 100 g from the pack). Cream 35 % fat.

**Scale ×2.5:** 150 g chocolate per variant; cream 150 / 100 / 75 / 50 g (600 g chocolate, 375 g cream). At that size you can use a stick blender in a narrow jug; results then match [R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md) more closely. At 60 g, use a spatula in a small bowl.

## Procedure

Why pre-melt the chocolate? With only 20 g of cream, R3 would not have enough heat to melt 60 g of chocolate; pre-melting all four to the same temperature makes the ratio the only difference.

1. **Label** four small bowls (R1, R1.5, R2, R3), four identical small containers for setting (e.g. 50–60 ml ramekins or shot glasses, filled to the same 2 cm depth), and four lines on a sheet for the line-spread.
2. **Melt the chocolate:** weigh 60 g into each bowl; melt each in the microwave (50 % power, 15–20 s bursts, stirring) to **45 °C**. Keep them at 40–45 °C (a warm-water tray at ~45 °C).
3. **Heat the cream** to **70 °C**.
4. **Emulsify R1:** weigh 60 g hot cream, pour **all at once** onto the chocolate. Stir with the spatula in small fast circles at the centre, widening outwards, for **60 s**. Record the temperature and appearance (glossy and elastic / grainy / oily).
5. **Repeat for R1.5, R2 and R3** with 40 g, 30 g and 20 g cream, the same 60 s stirring. Work one at a time, about 2 min apart.
6. **Line-spread at 32 °C:** as each ganache cools to **32 °C**, deposit **10 g** on the centre of a concentric-circle template (circles every 5 mm) under a clean plastic sheet. After **60 s**, read the diameter in two directions and average.
7. **Fill** the setting containers to 2 cm; press film onto the surface. Put the remainder (the rest of each batch) in a small covered cup for piping and tasting.
8. **Crystallize 12 h at 18–20 °C** (record the actual temperature).
9. **Firmness (penetration test):** tape a drinking straw vertically to a stand (a glass with a card across it) over the centre of each container. Drop a wooden skewer, pointed end down, with **20 g** of coins taped to its top, through the straw from a mark just above the surface (release it at the surface, don't drop from a height). After **10 s**, mark the skewer at the surface and measure the depth. Three readings per container, different spots; average. (Deeper = softer. If it goes to the bottom, record "≥ 20 mm".)
10. **Pipeability at 20 °C:** stir each reserved sample briefly (5 strokes), put it in a small bag with a star tip, pipe three rosettes. Score after 30 min.
11. **Taste** at 20 °C, water between samples: smoothness, melt, oiliness.
12. **Photograph** the cut surface of each container.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Hot cream and chocolate at 70 °C/45 °C: handle with care. Ganache is a dairy product: keep samples covered, refrigerate them as soon as testing is finished, and eat within 2 days. The 12 h crystallization at 18–20 °C is normal professional practice for ganache; do not extend it. Allergens: milk, soya.

</div>

## Analysis questions

1. Was the firmness curve linear? Where was the largest change?
2. Which variant would you choose to glaze a tart, pipe onto a tartlet and roll as a truffle? Does that agree with the ranges in [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md)?
3. Did any variant look grainy or oily at emulsification? At what water % did that happen? How would you rescue it, and how would you reformulate it?
4. How would the curve move with a 70 % chocolate instead of 55 %? With a milk chocolate?
5. The line-spread at 32 °C and the penetration at 20 °C measure different things. What does each tell you about how the ganache will behave on the bench?
6. Estimate the ratio that would give a penetration halfway between R1.5 and R2. How would you confirm it?

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**R1 (1 : 1)** emulsifies easily into a glossy, fluid ganache. At 32 °C it spreads widely: a pourable glaze. After 12 h it is soft and spoonable; the skewer sinks deep, often to the bottom. It will not hold a piped ridge at 20 °C (pipeability 0–1). Silky, fast melt.

**R1.5 (1.5 : 1)** is glossy, thicker when warm, sets soft-firm: the skewer sinks partway. It pipes well at 20 °C with defined ridges (2–3). This is the piping and filling ganache.

**R2 (2 : 1)** emulsifies to a thick, glossy, elastic mass; spreads little at 32 °C; sets firm (shallow penetration); scoops and rolls cleanly; pipes stiffly (fine with a plain tip, hard work with a star). This is the truffle ganache.

**R3 (3 : 1)** is the interesting one. With only ~12 g of water for 60 g of chocolate, the mixture is thick, often **dull and slightly grainy**, and with higher-% chocolates it may show an **oily sheen or fat separating** at the edges as you stir: the water phase cannot hold all the fat. It barely spreads when warm, sets very hard (almost no penetration), cracks when cut, and cannot be piped. In the mouth it may feel slightly grainy or greasy and melts slowly. With a 55 % chocolate it may just hold together; with a 66–70 % it usually splits. Adding 5–10 % of its weight of warm milk and re-blending at 32–35 °C usually recovers it, at which point it is no longer 3 : 1.

**The curve** is not linear. Firmness usually rises steeply between 1 : 1 and 2 : 1, then R3 is off the useful scale. Most of the change comes from water %: every extra part of chocolate adds fat to crystallize and particles to absorb water, and removes water phase.

**Why.** A ganache is fat droplets and cocoa particles in a sugar syrup ([16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). Its firmness at 20 °C comes from crystallized cocoa butter inside the droplets, cocoa particles crowding and absorbing water, and a viscous sugar syrup. As the ratio rises, all three increase, so firmness rises; but the water phase shrinks, and below roughly 15–18 % water (for a typical dark chocolate) it can no longer separate the droplets and wet the particles: the ganache goes grainy, then oily. A higher-% chocolate brings more fat and particles and shifts every point to the right (firmer, earlier split); a milk chocolate shifts the curve the other way (softer at every ratio).

</details>

## Going further

- Repeat with **milk chocolate** at 2 : 1, 2.5 : 1, 3 : 1, 3.5 : 1 and compare with the dark curve.
- Add **10 % invert sugar** (on chocolate) to R2 and R3: does it prevent graininess and soften the set?
- Store R1.5 and R2 for **7 days in the refrigerator** and re-test for graininess (sugar or fat recrystallization).
- **Stage 2** frames ganache at a chosen ratio, cuts it with a guitar, and measures water activity to set shelf life. **Stage 3** formulates ganache as a balance of fat, water, sugars and cocoa solids to hit a target firmness and a<sub>w</sub>, and uses a texture analyser instead of the weighted skewer.
