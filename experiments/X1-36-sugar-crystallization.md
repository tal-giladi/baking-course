# X1-36 · Controlling sugar crystallization

## Question

A sugar syrup cooked to 118 °C is supersaturated once it cools. How do an interfering agent (glucose syrup), a little acid (cream of tartar) and a deliberate seed crystal change whether it crystallizes, how fast, and whether the result is clear, smooth and fondant-like, or grainy, both when the cooling syrup is stirred and when it is left alone?

## Before you start: write your hypothesis

Write in your notebook, before you cook:

- For each of the four syrups (plain, glucose, acid, seeded), predict what the **stirred** half will look and feel like after 30 minutes: clear, opaque and smooth, or grainy.
- Predict which **undisturbed** halves will still be clear after 24 hours and which will have crystallized.
- Predict which variant will crystallize fastest of all, and explain why using the words *supersaturation* and *nucleation*.

## Procedure

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** You will stir syrup at up to 118 °C by hand. Use a long wooden spoon or heatproof spatula, set each bowl on a folded towel so it cannot slide, wear long sleeves, and keep a bowl of iced water beside you for burns (then cool the skin under running water for 20 minutes). No children or pets in the kitchen. Do not taste any sample until it is below 40 °C.

</div>

**Set-up**

1. Calibrate your thermometer in boiling water in the pan you will use ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)). Record the correction.
2. Label eight containers: **A-S, A-U, B-S, B-U, C-S, C-U, D-S, D-U**. For **U** (undisturbed) use small heatproof ramekins or glass jars; for **S** (stirred) use small stainless-steel bowls. Tare each and note the weight on the label.
3. Use a small heavy pan, 12–14 cm, so 200 g of syrup covers the probe (tilt it if necessary). Wash and dry it completely between batches: a single leftover crystal is an uncontrolled seed.

**For each variant, one at a time (about 15 minutes each)**

4. Put the water in the pan, then the sugar (and the glucose for B; the cream of tartar for C). Stir over medium heat until **completely dissolved** and clear.
5. Stop stirring. Wash the pan walls down with a wet brush. Boil on the same heat setting for all four, without stirring, to **118 °C** (corrected). Record time from boil to 118 °C.
6. Take the pan off the heat. Immediately pour about **70 g** into the U container and the rest (about **100 g**) into the S bowl. Record both weights.
7. **U half:** set it on a rack where it will not be moved or bumped. Do not touch, stir, scrape or cover it for the first 2 hours; after that, cover it loosely to keep dust out.
8. **S half:** start stirring at once with a clean wooden spoon, steadily (about 1 stroke per second), scraping the bottom and sides. Continue for **10 minutes** or until it sets too firm to stir. Record the temperature and time at which it first turns cloudy or opaque, and at which it sets.
9. **Seeding (D only):** leave both halves undisturbed until the syrup cools to **100 °C** (probe the S bowl; about 2–3 min), then sprinkle **0.5 g of granulated sugar** onto each half. Then start stirring D-S as in step 8, and leave D-U alone as in step 7. Record the time of seeding.
10. Record each variant's pour time. All observations are timed from that variant's own pour.

**Observations** at **5 min, 30 min, 2 h, 24 h and 72 h** (U halves: look without moving them until the 2 h reading; after that you may tilt and probe them).

## Analysis questions

1. Compare A-S with A-U. What does stirring do to a supersaturated syrup, and why?
2. Compare A-S, B-S and C-S. How did glucose and acid change the time to opacity and the grain size? What do they have in common?
3. What did the seed do in D-U? What does that tell you about the difference between nucleation and growth?
4. Which undisturbed halves were still clear at 72 h? What would you use to keep a hard-crack sugar glass clear, and why not acid?
5. The stirred samples were agitated while hot. Predict what A would have become if you had left it undisturbed until 45 °C and then stirred it. Why?
6. Rank the samples from most fondant-like to most grainy, and explain the ranking with supersaturation, nucleation rate and interfering agents.

<details class="answer"><summary>What you should expect to see</summary>

**A-S, plain, stirred hot:** turns cloudy within a few minutes, often while still well above 80 °C, then sets quickly into an opaque, crumbly, grainy mass that feels sandy on the tongue (grit 4–5). Stirring nucleates crystals while the syrup is still hot, supersaturation is modest and molecules move easily, so relatively few crystals form and they grow large.

**A-U, plain, undisturbed:** usually cools to a clear, very stiff, glassy-looking mass that stays clear for hours. It may stay clear for days, or develop crystals from the edges or surface within 1–3 days, especially if any grain was left on the container or the air is humid. It is metastable: supersaturated but waiting for a nucleus.

**B-S, 10 % glucose, stirred:** turns opaque later than A-S and more gradually. The result is smoother and softer, closer to a creamy, fudge-like or fondant-like paste (grit about 2–3), though not as fine as true fondant. Glucose and maltose molecules get in the way of sucrose molecules joining the crystal lattice, and they make the syrup more viscous, so crystals form more slowly and stay smaller. Some batches stay a stiff clear syrup for a long time even when stirred.

**B-U:** stays clear and non-crystalline for the whole 72 h in most kitchens.

**C-S, cream of tartar, stirred:** similar to B-S. During boiling, acid inverted part of the sucrose into glucose and fructose, which act as interfering agents. The effect depends on how long the syrup boiled and on the acid, so it varies more from batch to batch than glucose does. Expect slower, finer crystallization than A-S, sometimes a sticky, soft result, and a slightly yellower colour.

**C-U:** stays clear; may be noticeably stickier on the surface after a day in humid air (fructose is hygroscopic).

**D-U, seeded, undisturbed:** crystallizes even without stirring. Crystals grow outwards from the seed grains as a white crust or radiating needles, usually visibly within minutes to an hour, and the sample becomes opaque and grainy. The seed skips the hard step, nucleation; growth is easy.

**D-S, seeded and stirred:** usually the fastest of all to turn opaque, and grainy.

**Question 5:** left undisturbed to about 45 °C and then beaten, plain syrup becomes **fondant**: many tiny crystals form at once in a highly supersaturated, viscous syrup, each grows only a little, and the result is white, smooth and creamy (grit 1). The same sugar, water and endpoint give fondant or sand depending only on *when* you agitate.

**If your results differ.** If A-U crystallized within an hour, the pan or container carried a seed: undissolved sugar, crystals on the walls, or a dirty spoon. If A-S stayed clear for more than 10 minutes, check the endpoint: a syrup that only reached about 110 °C is much less supersaturated. If B-S and A-S looked the same, check that the glucose went in before boiling.

</details>

## Going further

- **Make fondant:** cook the plain syrup to 116 °C, pour it onto a lightly wetted marble slab or tray, leave it undisturbed until it reaches **40–45 °C**, then work it with a scraper in a figure-of-eight until it turns white and firm (5–10 min). Knead it smooth. Compare its grit score with A-S.
- Glucose series: 0, 5, 10, 20 % of the sugar, all stirred hot, with time to opacity as the response.
- Endpoint series: plain syrups to 112, 118 and 124 °C, stirred hot. Higher supersaturation should crystallize faster.
- Stage 2 uses these controls for fondant, fudge, pralines by sablage and pulled sugar; Stage 3 studies crystallization kinetics and glass stability of formulations with different sugar blends.
