# Eggs, foams and emulsions

This category covers faults in the three things eggs do in M09: setting by heat (coagulation), holding air (foams) and holding oil (emulsions). Many later faults are the same faults inside a bigger product: a low génoise is often a foam problem, a curdled crème anglaise is a coagulation problem, a split ganache is an emulsion problem. Those products have their own pages ([Cakes](troubleshooting/cakes.md), [Custards and creams](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md), [Syrups, meringues, buttercreams](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md), [Chocolate and ganache](troubleshooting/chocolate.md)), but check the mechanism here first. For the diagnostic method, see the [troubleshooting index](troubleshooting/README.md).

**Before you diagnose, measure three things.** Most egg faults trace back to *temperature* (of the egg, the mixture, the bath or the pan, measured with a probe), *cleanliness* (fat in the bowl) and *rate* (how fast heat, sugar or oil was added). The mechanisms are in [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md) (coagulation), [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md) (foams) and [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md) (emulsions).

### Egg whites will not whip

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Yolk in the whites | Look for yellow streaks or specks; foam creamy-tinged, stalls at soft peak | Separate with the three-bowl method; a white with any yolk goes to another use. Even ~0.5 g in 60 g cuts volume sharply ([X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md)) |
| Fat on bowl or beaters | Plastic bowl; beaters last used for butter or cream; bowl dried with a used towel | Use glass, stainless steel or copper; wash, then wipe bowl and beaters with vinegar on a paper towel |
| Sugar added at the start | Recipe followed with all sugar in at once; foam glossy but slow and dense | Whip to soft peak first, then add sugar ~10–15 g every 15–20 s |
| Whites too cold | Whites straight from the refrigerator (~4 °C) | Warm to ~18–22 °C (20–30 min at room temperature or a warm-water bath) |
| Pasteurized or carton whites | Label; slow foaming and lower volume | Add cream of tartar (~1 % of white), allow 2–3× the time; buy whites labelled "suitable for whipping" |
| Too small a batch for the bowl or mixer | Beaters or whisk not fully immersed; stand mixer with 1–2 whites | Use a hand mixer or a smaller, deeper bowl; stand mixers need ~100 g white or more |

Whites fail to whip because something stops proteins forming films at the bubble surfaces (fat), slows them getting there (sugar, cold, viscosity), or has already changed them (heat treatment). Find which. See [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md).

### Whipped whites grainy, dry or collapsed

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Overwhipped | Dull, cotton-wool clumps; liquid appearing; peak breaks rather than bends | Stop at firm peak for folded batters; for a mild overwhip, whisk in one fresh white (per 4 whites) by hand to loosen. Badly overwhipped whites cannot be recovered |
| No sugar or acid, so a narrow window | Unsweetened whites without cream of tartar went from stiff to grainy in under a minute | Add cream of tartar (~1 % of white weight) at the foamy stage; add some of the recipe's sugar after soft peak |
| Foam left standing before use | Foam made first, base prepared afterwards | Prepare everything else first; whip whites last and use within 2–3 minutes |
| Mixer too fast from the start with a small batch | Large, uneven bubbles; overwhips before it looks stiff | Start on medium to foamy, then increase; watch closely in the last minute |

Past stiff peak the proteins in the bubble films over-bond, the films become brittle and the network squeezes out water: the same over-coagulation as rubbery cooked egg, caused mechanically. See [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md) and the window test in [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md).

### Foam weeps liquid

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Underwhipped (soft peak used where firm was needed) | Foam drains within minutes; SG high for the stage | Whip to the specified stage; measure SG |
| Overwhipped | Grainy, with liquid squeezed out (see above) | Stop earlier; use acid |
| Too little sugar, or sugar undissolved | Grit when rubbed between fingers; syrupy beads | Increase sugar for stability (French meringue 1:1 to 2:1); add sugar gradually so it dissolves; use caster sugar |
| Foam held too long before use or setting | Liquid under the foam in the bowl after 10–30 min | Use promptly; set it (bake, cook or chill with a stabilizer) |
| Cooked foam (sabayon) undercooked | Final temperature below ~70 °C; thin ribbon | Cook to 72–78 °C ([R1-16](recipes/R1-16-sabayon.md)) |

Liquid under a foam is drainage: gravity pulls the water phase down the channels between bubbles. Viscosity (sugar), flexible films (acid) and setting (heat) slow it. Measure it as grams of liquid from 20 g of foam in 30 minutes ([X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md)).

### Batter deflates after folding

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Stirred instead of folded, or overfolded | Many strokes; batter runny and shiny; SG of batter much higher than the foam's | Sacrifice a third, then fold the rest in 1–2 additions; stop when streaks disappear |
| Base too heavy or too cold | Thick, cold base needed many strokes to combine | Loosen the base with the sacrificed third; bring base and foam to similar temperatures |
| Fat added carelessly (melted butter, chocolate, yolk-rich base) | Batter collapsed when the butter went in | Mix melted butter into a small portion of batter first, then fold that back in; add fat last |
| Foam overwhipped | Lumps of white in the batter that will not fold in | Stop whites at firm peak ([previous entries](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=whipped-whites-grainy-dry-or-collapsed)) |
| Flour dumped in one lump | Pockets of flour; extra strokes to break them | Sieve dry ingredients over the surface in 2–3 additions |
| Batter waited before baking | Pan filled, oven not yet hot | Oven preheated and pans ready before the foam is made |

Every fold stroke shears bubbles. Measure batter SG and compare it with the foam; if it rises by more than ~0.1–0.15, folding is costing volume. See [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md) and [10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md).

### Broken mayonnaise

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Oil added too fast at the start | Never thickened; oil pooling from early on | Rescue: fresh yolk (or 10 g water, or 5 g mustard + 5 g water) in a clean bowl; add the broken mixture drop by drop until thick, then in a thread. Next time, first 30 g oil drop by drop |
| Too much oil for the water phase | Was very stiff and gel-like, then suddenly thinned and turned curdled and greasy | Same rescue. Next time, add 5–10 g water or acid when it feels tight; keep the water phase at ~15–20 % of the oil |
| Oil or yolk too cold | Oil below ~15 °C (probe); cold kitchen; slow to thicken | Warm oil and a fresh yolk to 18–22 °C; rescue as above |
| Too warm | Oil or kitchen above ~28–30 °C; thin, oily sheen | Cool ingredients to ~20 °C; rescue in a cool bowl |
| Too little emulsifier | Large batch from one yolk, no mustard | One yolk per ~150–200 g oil; include mustard |

A broken emulsion is coalesced oil: the droplets merged because they were not dispersed fast enough, were packed with too little water between them, or the temperature was wrong. A fresh continuous phase with emulsifier re-disperses it. See [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md) and [R1-15](recipes/R1-15-mayonnaise.md).

### Greasy or oily emulsion

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Near the phase-volume limit; droplets starting to coalesce | Oil beads on the surface after standing; mouthfeel coats the tongue | Whisk in a little water or acid; reduce oil next time |
| Droplets too large (under-whisked, oil too fast later on) | Looser, yellower, less glossy than normal | Whisk harder, add oil more slowly; the blender method gives smaller droplets |
| Oil crystallizing in the refrigerator (olive oil) | Separation after chilling, not before | Use neutral oil for the base; olive oil ≤ ¼ of the oil |
| Warm storage or temperature cycling | Left out; refrigerator door | Store at ≤ 4 °C at the back of the refrigerator; use within 3 days |

Greasiness is often the early stage of breaking. The same diagnosis applies to a greasy ganache (fat too high for its water phase or too hot, [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md)) and a soupy buttercream (too warm, [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md)).

### Curdled or scrambled egg mixture

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Overheated: mixture went past its setting range | Probe reading above ~85 °C for a custard, above ~80 °C for a yolk foam; curds or grainy texture; liquid separating | Stop at the target temperature ([09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md) map). A slightly grainy stirred custard can be saved by pouring it immediately into a cold bowl and blending with an immersion blender, then straining; real curds cannot be reversed |
| Heat too high or uneven (direct heat, thin pan, no stirring) | Curds stuck at the base or sides while the centre is still thin | Use low heat, a heavy pan or a bain-marie; stir or whisk constantly, reaching the edges |
| Hot liquid added to eggs too fast (no tempering) | Threads or specks of cooked egg immediately after combining | Temper: whisk a third of the hot liquid into the eggs in a thin stream, then return to the pan; strain if needed |
| Acid mixture overcooked (curds, citrus) | Acidic mixture curdled at a lower temperature than expected | Acid lowers the setting temperature: cook to the low end of the range |
| Carry-over heat | Correct when removed; curdled a few minutes later in the hot pan | Pour into a cold bowl or stand the pan in ice water immediately |
| Baked egg dish overbaked (custard, quiche, cheesecake) | Weeping liquid, bubbles or holes, rubbery texture; centre temperature well above target | Bake in a water bath at a lower oven temperature; pull when the centre still wobbles |

Curdling and weeping are over-coagulation: the protein network kept tightening past a tender set and squeezed out its water (syneresis). You map the ranges in [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md); custard-specific faults are in [Custards and creams](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md).

### Sabayon collapses or scrambles

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Overheated (above ~80 °C) | Probe; grainy texture, curds at the bowl sides, liquid pooling, volume falling | Stop at 72–78 °C; whisk 30 s off heat; if slightly grainy, whisk over ice and strain (it will be less airy) |
| Bowl touching the water or water boiling | Bowl base wet or water bubbling hard; curds on the base | Bare simmer; bowl above the water |
| Not whisking the edges | Cooked egg on the sides of the bowl | Whisk the whole surface of the bowl constantly |
| Undercooked (below ~70 °C) | Thin foam that never holds a ribbon; drains in minutes; raw taste | Cook to 72–78 °C; ribbon holds 3–5 s |
| Served too long after cooking | Fine at the stove, flat and weeping at the table | Serve at once, or chill over ice and fold in whipped cream for a stable version |
| Acidic juice used instead of wine, cooked to wine temperatures | Scrambled at a lower temperature than expected | Aim for 72–75 °C with citrus |

A sabayon is a foam held up by partly coagulated yolk: undercook it and there is nothing to support the bubbles; overcook it and the yolk curdles and releases its water. See [R1-16](recipes/R1-16-sabayon.md) and [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md).

### Grey-green ring around the yolk

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Cooked too long | Boiled over ~12 min for a large egg; yolk dry and crumbly | Cook for the minimum time: bring to the boil, then 9–11 min for a hard-cooked large egg, or simmer gently |
| Cooled slowly | Eggs left in the hot water or cooled at room temperature | Plunge into ice water for 5+ min immediately |
| Old eggs (alkaline white) | Eggs near their best-before date | Expected; still safe. Use fresher eggs if appearance matters |

A curiosity rather than a fault in pastry: hydrogen sulfide released from heated white reacts with iron in the yolk to form ferrous sulfide. It is harmless, but it is a clear visible record of overcooking. See [09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md).
