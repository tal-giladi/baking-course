# Assembled pastries

This category covers faults that appear when finished components are put together: tarts filled with unbaked creams, fruit and glazes, chocolate tarts, and layered cakes and simple entremets. Faults inside a single component are in its own category: shells in [Short pastry, pies and tarts](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md), pastry cream and whipped cream in [Custards and creams](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md), sponges in [Cakes](troubleshooting/cakes.md), buttercreams in [Syrups, meringues and buttercreams](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md), ganache and tempering in [Chocolate and ganache](troubleshooting/chocolate.md). For the diagnostic method, see the [troubleshooting index](troubleshooting/README.md).

**Before you diagnose, check four things.** Almost every assembly fault traces back to (1) an **interface** between components with different water activity (a dry shell against a wet cream, cut fruit against a glaze, a cold cake in warm air), (2) a **component temperature** at the moment of assembly (buttercream, ganache, glaze, shell), (3) **structure**: whether each soft component was contained and set before it carried a load, and (4) **time** between assembly and service. Record all four every time. The mechanisms are in [17.1](stage-1/module-17/lesson-01.md) (components, moisture migration, timelines), [17.2](stage-1/module-17/lesson-02.md) (tarts) and [17.3](stage-1/module-17/lesson-03.md) (layered cakes).

### Soggy tart base

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Shell underbaked | Centre of the base pale or grey underneath; a cut edge is soft or cakey even in an unfilled offcut | Full bake: remove weights for the last 8–12 min, bake on a preheated tray on the lower-middle rack until the centre of the base is golden ([R1-21](recipes/R1-21-pate-sucree.md)) |
| No seal, or an incomplete one | No wash or film; soft spots concentrated at the base–wall corner or at pinholes | Egg wash baked 2–3 min; for holds over ~4 h add a complete cocoa butter or chocolate film, corner first ([X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md)) |
| Filled too long before service | Crisp at filling, soft hours later | Fill within 4–6 h of service (egg wash only) or seal with fat; plan with a backward timeline ([17.1](stage-1/module-17/lesson-01.md)) |
| Pastry cream thinned (over-whisked, under-boiled) or weeping | Liquid layer between cream and shell when a slice is lifted | Boil pastry cream 1–2 min when making; re-texture only 20–40 s ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)) |
| Wet fruit or juice | Juice pooled under the fruit; base wettest under cut fruit | Dry fruit after washing; cut within 1 h; glaze thinly; use a jam or almond-cream layer |
| Warm shell or warm cream at filling | Barrier smeared; cream soft at the edges | Shell at room temperature; cream ≤ 5 °C |

Water moves from the high-aw filling (about 0.97) into the low-aw shell (about 0.3) until their water activities approach each other. You can start the shell drier, put a barrier in the way, lower the filling's aw, or shorten the time ([SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md), [17.2](stage-1/module-17/lesson-02.md)).

### Fruit weeping or glaze sliding off

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit wet from washing | Water beads visible under the glaze; glaze beads up rather than coating | Drain on paper towels and air-dry 15–20 min before arranging |
| Fruit cut too early | Cut faces glassy and leaking before glazing | Cut within the last hour; keep cut side down on paper in the refrigerator |
| Glaze too thin (over-diluted) or not boiled | Glaze runs down into the cream and pools at the rim; does not gel on a cold plate test | Boil apricot glaze 30 s to re-dissolve and activate the pectin; use 10–20 % water; follow the maker's dilution for neutral glaze |
| Glaze applied to cold, wet fruit straight from the refrigerator | Condensation on fruit before glazing | Let fruit reach 10–15 °C and dry before glazing |
| Sugar in the glaze drawing juice out (osmosis), long hold | Fine juice layer between fruit and glaze after several hours | Thin glaze coat; serve within 4–6 h; firm fruit rather than very ripe |
| Frozen or very ripe soft fruit | Fruit collapses and releases juice | Use fresh, ripe-but-firm fruit; frozen fruit only in cooked fillings |

A glaze is a thin pectin (or carrageenan) gel that must set on a dry fruit surface. Free water, and juice drawn out of cut fruit, stop it from adhering ([R1-39](recipes/R1-39-fruit-tart.md)).

### Glaze cloudy or too thick

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Glaze too cool at application (already gelling) | Glaze drags on the brush, goes on streaky; temperature below ~60 °C | Reheat to just below boiling; brush at 80–90 °C; keep it warm in a small pan |
| Too little water, or reduced by long boiling | Thick in the pan even when hot | Add water 5 g at a time and re-boil; boil only 30 s |
| Re-boiled many times | Darker, thicker, sticky | Make a fresh small batch; strain |
| Neutral glaze not diluted or heated as specified | Cloudy, lumpy, will not flow | Follow the maker's ratio and temperature exactly; strain |
| Too thick a coat | Glaze visibly thick, pooling between fruit | Dab a thin coat; 60–80 g for a 22 cm tart |
| Brushed over condensation on cold fruit | Milky patches | Glaze dry fruit at 10–15 °C |

The glaze gel sets as it cools, so its temperature at application decides whether it flows into a thin, clear film or drags as partly set gel.

### Tart cream too runny to slice

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Pastry cream under-boiled (amylase survived, starch under-gelatinized) | Cream thinned overnight; watery layer | Boil 1–2 min, whisking, when cooking ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md), [Pastry cream runny the next day](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=pastry-cream-runny-the-next-day)) |
| Over-whisked before filling | Glossy and flowing in the bowl; slumps when piped | Whisk only 20–40 s, until just smooth |
| Diplomate not set before cutting or fruit | Cream spreads under fruit; no clean cut face | Let gelatin-set diplomate set 1–2 h at ≤ 5 °C before adding fruit and cutting |
| Gelatin broken down by raw protease fruit (kiwi, pineapple, papaya, mango, fig) | Softening mostly under those fruits | Use those fruits on pastry cream (not diplomate) or serve within 2–3 h; or use cooked fruit ([SC-09](stage-1/science/sc-09-enzymes.md)) |
| Too warm when cut | Tart has been out of the refrigerator > 30 min | Cut at ≤ 10 °C cream temperature |
| Too much cream for the shell | Cream level above the rim; slices bulge | 350–400 g in a 22 cm × 2 cm shell |
| Acid fruit juice added to the cream hot | Lemon or fruit cream thin | Add acid off the heat after cooking; increase starch slightly |

A tart cream must hold its cut face because it is a starch gel (or a gelatin gel for diplomate). Anything that breaks the gel (enzyme, shear, heat) or delays it (not set) makes it flow ([12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md)).

### Cracked chocolate tart surface

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Ganache poured too hot | Pour temperature above 45 °C recorded; surface skin formed first | Pour at 35–40 °C |
| Cooled too fast (refrigerator straight after pouring) | Fine cracks, often with dull patches | Set 2–3 h at 16–20 °C; refrigerate only after it has set, if at all |
| Ganache too firm (high chocolate ratio or high cocoa %) | Cracks when cut cold; very firm at room temperature | Use 1:1 with 60–70 % chocolate; add ~10 % butter; serve at 18–22 °C |
| Baked filling overbaked or baked too hot | Domed then sunken, cracked surface; grainy texture; centre above 75 °C | Bake at 150 °C; remove when the centre still wobbles (68–72 °C) ([R1-40](recipes/R1-40-chocolate-tart.md)) |
| Baked filling cooled in a draught or refrigerator | Cracks appear during cooling | Cool at room temperature for at least 1 h |
| Tart moved or knocked while setting | Cracks radiating from one side | Pour on the board where it will set; do not move for 1 h |

The surface sets and shrinks before the interior if it cools faster; a baked custard over-coagulates and contracts. In both cases the stress releases as cracks.

### Grainy or dull chocolate tart

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Emulsion not formed properly (stirred too vigorously from the edge, or not enough) | Grainy look, oil at the edge, dull even when fresh | Emulsify from the centre in small circles, or with a submerged hand blender, until glossy and elastic ([R1-36](recipes/R1-36-ganache.md), [16.2](stage-1/module-16/lesson-02.md)) |
| Overheated ganache or butter added too hot | Oily sheen on the surface, butter streaks | Add soft butter at 35–40 °C; never heat the finished ganache above ~50 °C |
| Split ganache | Visible separation of fat and a grainy paste | Rescue: warm to ~35 °C and blend in 5–10 % warm cream or milk with a submerged blender ([Chocolate and ganache](troubleshooting/chocolate.md)) |
| Condensation after refrigeration | Dull, spotted surface after coming out of the refrigerator; sometimes white sugar streaks | Set at room temperature; if refrigerated, warm up covered in its box |
| Fat bloom (warm storage, then cooling) | Grey-white streaks that melt on touch | Store at a stable 16–20 °C; do not let the tart warm above ~25 °C |
| Baked filling overcooked | Grainy texture, sweats fat | Lower the oven to 150 °C and pull earlier |
| Air bubbles | Pinholes over the surface | Blend with the head submerged; pour low; pop bubbles within 2 min of pouring |

Gloss comes from a fine, even emulsion and a smooth, undisturbed surface. Anything that coarsens the emulsion or disturbs the surface after setting scatters light.

### Layer cake layers sliding

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Buttercream too warm (over 24 °C) | Buttercream greasy, shiny, soft in the bowl | Work at 20–22 °C; chill the cake 15–20 min after stacking |
| Soft filling (Chantilly, diplomate, jam, fruit) not contained | Filling squeezing out at one side; no dam | Pipe a firm dam and chill it; or assemble in a ring with acetate ([R1-41](recipes/R1-41-layer-cake.md)) |
| Too much syrup, especially on the bottom layer | Syrup pooled on the board; base layer compressed and wet | Weigh syrup: about 30/35/40 g for 20 cm layers; less with wet fillings |
| Layers uneven or domed | Layer thickness varies more than 2–3 mm across | Level the génoise; split with guides |
| Filling too thick | Filling layer thicker than the sponge layers | 150 g per filling layer for 20 cm (about 1 cm) |
| Transported or served warm | Slides after being carried | Chill before moving; carry on a flat board in a box |

A layered cake is stable only if every filling layer is firmer than the load above it and contained at its edge ([17.3](stage-1/module-17/lesson-03.md)).

### Cake too dry despite syrup

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Too little syrup | Syrup not weighed; well below 25 % of sponge weight | Weigh syrup onto each layer on the scale |
| Syrup brushed only in the centre | Edges dry and crumbly, centre moist | Brush from the edges in, in two passes |
| Top layer under-soaked (uncut, crust side up) | Top layer driest | Put the top layer cut side down; give it the most syrup (about 40 g) |
| Génoise overbaked or very dense | Dry, dark crust; tight crumb even before soaking | Bake to springback, not beyond ([R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md), [Cakes](troubleshooting/cakes.md)) |
| Cake stored uncovered or uncoated | Dry rim of crumb under a cracked finish; cut faces dry | Coat fully (the buttercream coat is a barrier); cover cut cakes; press film on cut faces |
| Served too cold | Buttercream and sponge taste dry and hard at 4 °C | Temper 30–60 min before serving |

The sponge is designed to be dry: syrup is the moisture. Too little of it, uneven distribution, or evaporation from an uncoated surface leaves dry zones.

### Cake soggy from syrup

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Too much syrup | Syrup drains out when a layer is pressed; wet board | Weigh syrup; reduce to 25 % of the sponge weight or below |
| Full syrup with a wet filling (Chantilly, diplomate, fruit) | Sponge wettest next to the filling | Reduce syrup by about a third with wet fillings |
| Syrup too thin (low °Brix) | Syrup measured below 30 °Brix; sponge wet, bland | 40 °Brix soaking syrup ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)) |
| Warm syrup on a fresh, warm sponge | Crumb collapses and goes pasty | Cool sponge completely (ideally day-old); syrup at room temperature |
| Juicy fruit filling not dried | Juice patches around the fruit | Dry fruit; keep it 1 cm from the edge |

Syrup is taken up by capillarity; once the crumb is saturated, excess drains down and the bottom layer collapses under the load.

### Crumbs in the buttercream finish

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| No crumb coat, or not chilled | Crumbs appear where the scraper passes | Thin crumb coat, chill 20–30 min until firm, then final coat |
| Buttercream too cold or stiff | Buttercream tears the sponge surface | Re-beat to 20–22 °C before coating |
| Scraper excess returned to the main bowl | Crumbs appear later in the coat | Scrape excess into a separate bowl |
| Fresh, soft génoise | Surface tears and crumbles when spread | Use day-old or chilled génoise |
| Spreading back and forth | Crumbs dragged from cut edges | Spread in one direction, pushing buttercream ahead of the spatula; add buttercream, do not rub |

A crumb coat is a thin glue layer that holds loose crumbs; once chilled and firm, the final coat cannot pick them up ([17.3](stage-1/module-17/lesson-03.md)).

### Cake leaning or bulging

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Layers not level, or domed top not removed | Measure layer height at four points | Level and split with guides; ±2 mm |
| Layers stacked off-centre | One side overhangs | Centre each layer; check from above |
| Uneven filling thickness | Filling thicker on one side | Weigh filling; level with an offset spatula before the next layer |
| Soft filling flowing outward (no dam, unset diplomate, warm) | Bulge around the middle of the sides | Dam; set gelatin fillings; ring and acetate; chill before coating |
| Over-soaked lower layers compressing | Lean worst on the side that got more syrup | Weigh and distribute syrup evenly |
| Warm room at service | Bulge appears after 1–2 h out | Serve from the refrigerator after 30–60 min; keep ≤ 20 °C |

Check level after stacking and before coating: a lean cannot be corrected once the coat is on.

### Condensation on a chilled cake

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Cold cake (2–5 °C) taken into a warm, humid room | Droplets or wet sheen within minutes; the surface is below the dew point of the air | Keep it in its box while it warms; warm it in a cool, dry place; serve from the box |
| Cake refrigerated uncovered next to moist foods | Surface tacky even in the refrigerator | Store in a closed box |
| Frozen cake thawed uncovered | Water spots, sugar streaks | Thaw overnight in the refrigerator, in its wrapping |
| Chocolate décor added before chilling | Chocolate dull, spotted or streaked after coming out | Add chocolate décor last, after the cake has come to service temperature |

Water condenses on any surface colder than the dew point of the surrounding air (for air at 25 °C and 60 % relative humidity, about 16–17 °C). A cake at 4 °C is well below it.

### Decorations melting or weeping

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Buttercream décor too warm (room > 24 °C, warm hands) | Rosettes lose ridges, droop | Pipe at 20–22 °C; half-full bags; chill the cake right after piping; serve within 1 h out of refrigeration |
| Chantilly décor unstabilized | Liquid at the base of rosettes after a few hours | Use stabilized Chantilly for anything held > 2–4 h ([R1-27](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md)) |
| Fresh fruit on buttercream or cream | Juice bleeds around fruit | Dry fruit; glaze it; add close to service |
| Sugar or chocolate décor on a moist surface | Sugar shards go sticky; tempered chocolate softens or blooms | Add last; put a small buttercream "foot" under each piece; keep the cake cool and dry |
| Untempered chocolate décor | Soft, melts on touch, streaky | Use tempered chocolate ([R1-37](recipes/R1-37-tempering.md)) |

Décor is the component most exposed to air, warmth and moisture: it melts (fat-based décor above about 24 °C), dissolves (sugar décor on a high-aw surface) or bleeds (cut fruit). Add it last and keep the product cool.
