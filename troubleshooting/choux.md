# Choux

This category covers faults in choux paste, choux shells, and their filling and glazing ([M14](stage-1/module-14/README.md)). Choux is leavened only by steam and set by egg protein and gelatinized starch, so nearly every fault traces back to one of three balances: **how much water and egg the paste holds** (panade drying and the V test, [14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md)), **how fast heat arrives and how long the shell dries** (the oven profile, [14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md)), and **what happens to a dry shell next to a moist filling** (moisture migration). For the general diagnostic method, see the [troubleshooting index](troubleshooting/README.md).

**Before you diagnose, collect the batch record:** panade weight loss (g), paste temperature when the eggs went in, egg weight used and the V-test result, piece weight, oven mode and set temperatures (and the oven-thermometer reading), when the door was first opened, total bake time, bake loss %, and the time between filling and eating. Then cut a shell open. The cross-section usually tells you whether the problem was the paste (thick walls, dense web, greasy base) or the bake (wet lining, thick moist walls in a good-sized shell).

### Choux collapsed after baking

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Underbaked: walls not dried | Pieces looked golden but felt heavy; bake loss below ~30 %; cut shell shows thick (5 mm+) moist walls; pale inside the cracks | Bake to rigidity and bake loss (35–45 %), not colour; extend the low-temperature phase (170 °C) by 5–10 min; vent the door for the last 5 min |
| Oven too hot, so colour arrived before dryness | Dark outside at 20–25 min; oven thermometer reads high; fan oven at 200 °C | Lower the second stage to 165–170 °C conventional or 170–180 °C fan and bake longer |
| Door opened before the walls set | Opened to check or rotate in the first 15–20 min; pieces sank visibly | Keep the door closed for at least 20 min (25 min for éclairs) |
| Not vented after baking | Shells not pierced; left on the tray in a closed box | Pierce bases immediately; cool on a rack; optionally dry 10 min in the switched-off oven, door ajar |
| Over-egged paste | V tail long and dripping; pieces spread before baking | Stop eggs at the V test ([Paste too runny](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-too-runny)) |

A shell that stands up in the oven but falls on the rack was held up by steam, not by its own walls. When that steam condenses, an underdried wall cannot carry the shape. Compare profiles in [X1-38](experiments/X1-38-choux-oven.md); mechanism in [14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md).

### Choux did not rise or baked flat

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Paste too runny (over-egged) | Pieces spread 15–30 % before baking; flat, wide, smooth tops | Reserve 10–20 % of the egg and stop at the V test ([X1-37](experiments/X1-37-choux-eggs.md)) |
| Paste too stiff (under-egged) | Dull, short paste; no V tail; pieces kept their piped shape but rose little and burst | Add egg 5–10 g at a time until the V test passes |
| Panade under-dried | Panade weight loss under ~10 g; no film on the pan; paste needed far less egg than usual | Dry 1–3 min on medium heat to a film and 75–80 °C; record the loss |
| Oven too cool | Oven thermometer reads 20 °C+ below the set point; slow rise, pale | Calibrate ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md)); preheat 30 min; start at 200 °C conventional |
| Paste skinned or cold before baking | Paste left uncovered over 1 h; skin lumps in the bag; tray waited at room temperature | Cover paste with film in contact; pipe within 1 h; freeze piped trays that must wait |
| Weak flour | Cake or pastry flour used | Use bread or all-purpose flour |
| Split paste | Greasy sheen; see below | See [Paste split or greasy](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-split-or-greasy) |

No rise means the steam could not inflate the paste: either the paste flowed (too soft), could not stretch (too stiff, split), or the water heated too slowly. The shape before baking tells you which: a spread blob is soft paste; a sharp, dull blob is stiff paste.

### Cracked irregular tops

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Paste too stiff | Short, dull paste; jagged bursts; thick walls | Add egg to the V test |
| Surface set too early (oven too hot, strong top heat, or fan) | Large single splits; dark crack edges; fan oven above 180 °C | Lower the temperature (fan 170–180 °C); shelf in the middle; fan off for the first 15 min if strong |
| No surface control | Plain tip, no fork marks, no craquelin | French star tip or fork marks on éclairs; craquelin on balls ([R1-32](recipes/R1-32-eclairs-profiteroles.md)) |
| Points or tails left on piped pieces | Crack starts at the point; point dark | Flatten points with a wet finger before baking; flick the tip off sideways when piping |
| Paste skinned before baking | Dry patches on the surface before baking | Cover paste; pipe promptly; freeze trays that wait |
| Heavy egg wash | Thick, glossy wash that set in patches | Brush thinly, or omit and use craquelin |

Cracks are normal; their pattern is the diagnosis. Fine, even cracks mean the surface stretched uniformly. One jagged split means the surface was stronger in most places than at one point, so all the steam left there.

### Small hollow or dense wet interior

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Underbaked drying phase | Good outside, wet paste lining inside; bake loss low | Bake longer at 165–175 °C; vent; pierce and return to the switched-off oven 10 min |
| Paste too stiff | Thick walls, small cavity, dense web in the middle | Add egg to the V test |
| Paste too runny | Flat piece with a flattened cavity and a wet base | Stop eggs at the V test |
| Oven too cool at the start | Slow, low rise; wet centre | Start at 200 °C conventional; check the thermometer |
| Pieces too large for the time | Pieces heavier than specified | Pipe to weight (±2 g); give larger pieces more time at the lower temperature |
| Too much butter or milk sugar browning early | Formula changed (all milk, extra sugar, extra butter) | Return to R1-31 balance, or bake 10 °C cooler and longer |

Some moist membrane inside a choux is normal and many pastry chefs scrape it out of large pieces. A layer of wet paste, or a web of dense paste across the cavity, means the paste did not inflate or the walls did not dry.

### Paste too runny

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Too much egg | All the formula egg added without testing; V tail long and dripping; trench closes at once | Rescue: make a mini panade (one fifth of R1-31 without eggs), dry it, cool to 60 °C, and beat the runny paste into it gradually. Next time reserve 10–20 % of the egg |
| Panade under-dried | Low weight loss; no film; short drying | Dry 1–3 min to film and 75–80 °C |
| Eggs added too fast | Paste never came back together between additions; looks separated and wet | Add in 4–6 additions; wait until each is absorbed |
| Paste too warm when judged | Paste above ~45 °C at the V test | Judge at 30–40 °C; warm paste reads softer |
| Very large eggs or extra whites | Egg weight much higher per egg than usual | Weigh egg, do not count eggs |
| Low-absorption flour | Cake or pastry flour; flour changed | Use bread or all-purpose flour |

The egg amount in a formula is an estimate; the V test is the measurement. See [14.1](stage-1/module-14/lesson-01.md) and [X1-37](experiments/X1-37-choux-eggs.md).

### Paste split or greasy

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Panade over-dried | Dried 4 min or more, or on high heat; beads or pools of butter on the paste and pan; paste grainy | Dry 1–3 min on medium; stop at the film and 75–80 °C. A slightly greasy panade can come back when the first egg is beaten in vigorously; a badly split one should be remade |
| Butter not melted when the liquid boiled, then boiled longer | Liquid boiled several minutes while butter melted; less water left | Cube the butter; medium heat; butter melted at the moment of the boil |
| Liquid boiled too long before adding flour | Visible reduction; paste stiff and oily | Add the flour as soon as the full boil is reached |
| Eggs added to paste that was too hot | Paste above ~65 °C; cooked egg specks | Cool to ≤ 60 °C before the first egg |
| Too much butter for the flour | Formula changed; butter over ~100 % of flour | Return to 70–80 % of flour |

The panade is a starch gel holding dispersed butterfat. If it is dried too far, the gel tightens and loses water until it can no longer hold the fat, which separates. A split paste emulsifies the egg poorly and bakes into flat, greasy shells with heavy bases.

### Lopsided or uneven rise

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Egg wash ran onto the tray | Base glued on one side; lean away from that side | Brush thinly; wipe drips; no wash on craquelin pieces |
| Uneven piping | Pieces with a thick end or tail; off-centre peaks | Pipe vertically for balls; flick off sideways; flatten points |
| Fan airflow | All pieces lean the same way; worst near the fan | Lower fan temperature; fan off for the first 15 min; rotate only after 20–25 min |
| Uneven oven heat | Pieces at the back or one side bigger or darker ([X1-02](experiments/X1-02-calibration.md) oven map) | Use the evenest zone; bake one tray at a time on the middle shelf |
| Tray warped or parchment lifting | Tray rocks; parchment curls up in the oven | Flat tray; dab paste under parchment corners; or perforated mat |
| Craquelin off-centre or wrong size | Disc smaller or off-centre; piece bulges on the uncovered side | Disc the same diameter as the piece, centred, frozen |

Steam pushes equally in all directions; a piece rises unevenly only when one side is restrained (glued base) or sets earlier (airflow, heat). See [14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md).

### Soggy shells after filling

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Filled too long before eating | More than 4–6 h between filling and eating | Fill within 1–4 h of serving; store shells unfilled (airtight, or frozen) |
| Shells underdried | Soft even before filling; low bake loss | Bake to 35–45 % bake loss; vent; re-crisp at 160 °C for 5–8 min before filling |
| Warm filling or warm shells | Filling above ~10 °C; shells filled warm | Fill cold filling into fully cooled shells; refrigerate at once |
| Very soft, wet filling | Chantilly or loose diplomate; cream weeping | Use pastry cream for long holding; add gelatin to diplomate ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)) |
| Shells stored in a humid place | Shells soft before use; stored in the refrigerator unwrapped | Store shells airtight at room temperature or frozen, not in the refrigerator |
| No moisture barrier where one is needed | Production requires early filling | Professional option: spray the inside with melted cocoa butter before filling |

Water moves from the high-water-activity filling into the dry shell until they approach equilibrium; time, temperature and the shell's dryness decide how soft it gets. The same problem is measured in [X1-43](experiments/X1-43-moisture-migration.md).

### Pale shells

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Underbaked | Pale inside the cracks; soft sides; low bake loss | Bake longer at 170 °C until colour is even including in the cracks |
| Oven too cool | Oven thermometer low; (c) profile in [X1-38](experiments/X1-38-choux-oven.md) | Calibrate; use 200 → 170 °C conventional |
| All-water paste, no sugar | Formula uses water only; no egg wash | Expected: crisper, paler. Add egg wash, or use half milk |
| No egg wash (plain pieces) | Matte, pale surface | Brush thinly with egg wash |
| Tray on the bottom shelf, or shielded | Tops pale, bases dark | Middle shelf; one tray at a time |

Colour comes from Maillard reactions at the surface once it is dry and above 100 °C; milk, egg wash and sugar supply reactants ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)). Pale shells are often also underdried: check bake loss before only turning up the heat.

### Fondant dull or cracked

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Fondant overheated | Probe reading above ~38–40 °C; heated directly in a pan or long microwave bursts | Warm to 35–37 °C over barely simmering water, stirring; never above 38 °C. Overheated fondant can be used again only after it has cooled and been worked; results vary, so start with fresh fondant |
| Thinned with water instead of syrup | Water added to loosen | Thin with 1:1 syrup, a few grams at a time |
| Glazed onto warm or damp shells | Shells warm or refrigerated and sweating | Glaze cold, dry shells; fill and glaze, then refrigerate |
| Held too long in the refrigerator | Glaze glossy at first, dull or sticky next day | Glaze within a few hours of serving |
| Layer too thick | Thick coat; cracks on handling | Thin the fondant slightly; dip and let excess run off |

Fondant's gloss comes from very small sucrose crystals in syrup ([13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md), [SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)). Overheating dissolves the smallest crystals; on cooling the sugar recrystallizes as fewer, larger crystals: dull and brittle.

### Fondant too runny or too thick

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Too much syrup | Glaze translucent; runs down the sides; shell shows through | Add more fondant and re-warm to 35–37 °C |
| Too warm (but under 38 °C) and very thin | Flows off the spoon without coating | Let it cool to 35 °C; stir; test again |
| Too cold | Below ~32 °C; heavy, lumpy coat; tears the shell surface | Re-warm to 35–37 °C, stirring |
| Not thinned at all | Warm fondant still stiff | Add 1:1 syrup 5 g at a time until it coats the back of a spoon opaquely and just flows |
| Chocolate added without extra syrup | Chocolate fondant thick and dull | Add a little more syrup with the chocolate; keep 35–37 °C |

Viscosity is set by temperature and syrup content; gloss by crystal size. Adjust viscosity with syrup, and keep the temperature in the 35–37 °C window for gloss. See [14.2](stage-1/module-14/lesson-02.md).

### Misshapen or curved shells

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Uneven piping speed or pressure | Éclairs wider at one end or wavy; balls oval | Steady pressure and speed; pipe to guide lines; weigh the first pieces |
| Thick tail at the end of an éclair | Bulbous end; crack at the end | Stop squeezing, then flick back over the end; tap down with a wet finger |
| Plain tip without fork marks | Éclairs curve or twist; random cracks | Use a 16–18 mm French star tip or fork marks along the length |
| Worn or dented tip | Uneven ridges; one side thicker | Replace the tip |
| Paste too soft | Pieces spread before baking | See [Paste too runny](troubleshooting/choux.md?id=paste-too-runny) |
| Paste lumpy (skinned) | Lumps block the tip; broken lines | Cover the paste; pipe within 1 h |
| Tray not flat, or parchment lifting | Curved or twisted éclairs | Flat tray; secure parchment with dabs of paste; perforated mat |

Shape is fixed at piping: the oven exaggerates any unevenness. Weigh and measure the first two pieces before piping the tray ([R1-32](recipes/R1-32-eclairs-profiteroles.md)).
