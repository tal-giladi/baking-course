# Syrups, meringues and buttercreams

This category covers cooked sugar syrups, the three meringues (French, Swiss, Italian) raw and baked, and the buttercream family (Swiss, Italian, French, American, crème mousseline). Caramel colour and bitterness are in [Cookies and caramel](troubleshooting/cookies.md); basic egg-white foam faults (yolk in the whites, overwhipping unsweetened whites) are in [Eggs, foams, emulsions](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md). For the diagnostic method, see the [troubleshooting index](troubleshooting/README.md).

**Measure two things before you diagnose anything here.** For syrups and meringues, the true syrup temperature: calibrate your thermometer in boiling water and correct every target ([13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md)). For buttercreams, the temperature of the cream itself: almost every "split" buttercream is a correct formula at the wrong temperature ([13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md)). The mechanisms behind the meringue entries are in [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md).

### Syrup crystallized in the pan

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar not fully dissolved before the boil | Grains grated under the spatula when it started boiling | Stir over medium heat until completely clear before it boils |
| Crystals on the pan wall fell back in | White crust at the syrup line | Wash the walls down with a wet brush, or cover the pan 1–2 min at the boil |
| Stirred after boiling | You stirred or scraped once it boiled | Stop stirring at the boil; swirl the pan if needed |
| Dirty pan, spoon or thermometer (sugar grains, dust) | Crystallization started around the spoon or probe | Clean, dry equipment; rinse the probe before inserting |
| No interfering agent in a demanding syrup | Plain sucrose syrup for hard crack or long cooks | Replace 10–20 % of the sugar with glucose syrup |
| Syrup agitated while cooling (pouring, scraping) | Clear in the pan, grainy after pouring and scraping | Pour without scraping the pan; cool undisturbed |

A crystallized syrup can be rescued: add back about 30 % of the sugar weight in water, dissolve completely, wash down the walls and cook again. See [13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md) and [X1-36](experiments/X1-36-sugar-crystallization.md).

### Syrup temperature wrong or unreliable

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Thermometer reads high or low | Boiling water reads other than 100 °C (at sea level) | Record the offset; subtract (100 − reading) from every target |
| Altitude | Kitchen above ~300 m; water boils below 99 °C | Lower every target by about 1 °C per 300 m, or use the boiling-water offset |
| Probe not immersed; reading foam or air | Batch shallower than 2–3 cm; reading jumps when you tilt | Smaller pan, tilt the pan, or larger batch |
| Probe touching the pan base | Reading higher than the cold-water test suggests | Hold the tip in the middle of the syrup |
| Carry-over after the heat was off | Final temperature 2–4 °C above where you pulled it | Pull 1–2 °C early; dip the base in cold water for hard crack |
| Slow thermometer | Reading lags during the fast climb above 112 °C | Use a fast digital probe; confirm with the cold-water test |

If a meringue made with a "118 °C" syrup is runny, suspect a thermometer that reads high. Test every thermometer in boiling water before sugar work. See [R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md).

### Meringue weeping or beading

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Undissolved sugar (French, or Swiss not heated enough) | Grit when rubbed between fingers; syrup droplets appear within an hour | Add sugar gradually after soft peak; use caster or icing sugar; Swiss to 65–70 °C with no grit |
| French meringue left standing | Liquid at the base after 30–60 min | Pipe and bake within 10–15 min; use Swiss or Italian for anything that must stand |
| Pie topping: underside not set (weeping) | Liquid layer between filling and meringue | Spread meringue on a hot filling; use Swiss or Italian meringue; seal to the crust edge |
| Overbaked, overcoagulated proteins (beading) | Amber droplets on top; oven hotter than set or too long | Lower the oven; shorten time; check with an oven thermometer |
| Too little sugar | Ratio below 1 : 1 | Use 1 : 1.5 to 1 : 2 for stable toppings |
| Humid storage | Baked shells become sticky and wet on the surface | Airtight with desiccant; never refrigerate |

Weeping means liquid escaping from a foam that could not hold it; beading means proteins squeezed syrup out. See [13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md) and [X1-35](experiments/X1-35-meringue-stability.md).

### Meringue deflates or turns grainy

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Fat in the whites or bowl (a trace of yolk, greasy bowl, plastic) | Whites never pass soft peak; yolk streak visible | Separate eggs one at a time; wipe bowl with vinegar; use steel or glass |
| Sugar added too early or too fast (French) | Foam never became stiff | Start sugar at soft peak, 10–15 % at a time |
| Overwhipped before sugar | Whites dull, lumpy, grainy before the sugar went in | Whip only to soft peak before adding sugar; add cream of tartar at ~1 % of the white |
| Overmixed when folding in icing sugar | Loses volume after folding | Fold in two additions, just until streak-free |
| Italian: syrup too cool (below ~115 °C) | Thermometer offset; soft, runny meringue | Calibrate; cook to 118–121 °C |
| Italian: whites overwhipped while waiting for syrup | Whites grainy when syrup arrived | Start whites at 110–112 °C; keep at low speed if early |

Grainy here means overwhipped protein (the foam looks curdled) or undissolved sugar (it feels gritty). Distinguish them by rubbing a little between your fingers. See [09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md).

### Baked meringue sticky or chewy

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Underdried | Weight loss below ~25 % for a 1 : 2 French or Swiss meringue; centre soft | Bake longer at 90–100 °C; leave in the switched-off oven 1 h+ |
| Oven too hot: outside set before inside dried | Beige surface, sticky core | Bake at 90–100 °C, measured |
| Humid day or humid storage | Crisp when warm, sticky within hours | Store airtight with desiccant; bake on dry days; re-dry at 90 °C for 20–30 min |
| Refrigerated | Stored in the fridge | Never refrigerate meringues |
| Pieces too large for the time | Large nests or discs underdone, kisses fine | Increase time for size, or pipe smaller |
| Too little sugar | Ratio below 1 : 1 | Use 1 : 2 for crisp baked meringues |

A pavlova is supposed to be soft in the centre; a kiss is not. See [R1-29](recipes/R1-29-meringues.md).

### Baked meringue cracked or browned

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Oven too hot | Oven thermometer reads above 110 °C; ivory to beige colour | Bake at 90–100 °C; many ovens run hot at low settings |
| Sudden cooling | Cracks appear after removal | Cool in the switched-off oven with the door ajar 1 h+ |
| Fan blowing directly on the pieces | Cracks and colour on the fan side | Fan off, or 10 °C lower |
| Overwhipped meringue | Dull, lumpy before piping | Stop at stiff, glossy peak |
| Tray too close to top element | Tops browned, bases white | Middle rack; tray under as a shield |

Some cracking is expected in a pavlova; none in kisses or shells baked at 100 °C.

### Swiss meringue grainy

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar not dissolved over the bain-marie | Grit between fingers after heating | Heat to 65–70 °C and keep whisking until no grit at all |
| Coarse sugar | Granulated rather than caster sugar | Use caster sugar, or heat longer |
| Whites cooked at the bowl edge | White specks or strands | Whisk constantly, scrape the sides; water at a bare simmer, bowl not touching it |
| Heated above ~75–80 °C | Thick, lumpy, specks | Stop at 70–71 °C |

Grit carried into a buttercream stays there, so check before you whip. See [R1-29](recipes/R1-29-meringues.md).

### Hard sugar lumps or splatter in Italian meringue

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Syrup poured onto the whisk | Sugar threads on the bowl wall and whisk | Pour a thin stream down the side of the bowl, between whisk and wall |
| Mixer speed too high while pouring | Syrup flung around the bowl | Medium-high while pouring, then high |
| Syrup too hot (above ~125 °C) | Thermometer offset; syrup sets on contact | Calibrate; cook to 118–121 °C |
| Poured too fast | Syrup pooled at the bottom and set | Pour over 30–45 s |
| Syrup crystallized before pouring | Cloudy, grainy syrup | See [syrup crystallized in the pan](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=syrup-crystallized-in-the-pan) |

Syrup splatter is a burn risk: keep your face back and use a pan with a lip. Strain lumps out only if the meringue is for buttercream; for piping, start again.

### Buttercream curdled

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Butter too cold | Butter below ~17 °C; pieces visible | Warm the bowl sides 5–10 s at a time while beating; or melt 10 % of the cream and add it back |
| Normal midway stage | Only half the butter is in | Keep adding butter and beating |
| Buttercream rewhipped from the fridge | Cream below 17 °C | Let it warm to 18–20 °C, or warm gently while beating |
| Cold flavouring added (purée, liqueur) | Split right after the addition | Bring additions to ~20 °C; warm the bowl gently |
| Too much liquid flavouring | Additions above ~10–15 % of the cream | Reduce purées; use pastes or extracts; add more butter to rebalance |

Curdled means the fat is too solid to hold the syrup. Take the temperature first. See [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md).

### Buttercream soupy

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Meringue still warm when butter went in | Meringue above 25 °C | Chill the bowl 10–15 min until the edges firm, then rewhip; repeat |
| Butter too soft | Butter above ~22 °C, shiny or greasy | Chill and rewhip; next time butter at 18–20 °C |
| Warm kitchen or mixer heat | Cream above ~24–25 °C after long beating | Chill and rewhip; cool the bowl from outside |
| Warm chocolate added | Chocolate above 35 °C | Chill and rewhip; add chocolate at 30–35 °C |

Soupy means too much of the butterfat has melted to hold air and syrup. It always recovers with chilling. See [R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md).

### Buttercream too sweet or too buttery

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| American buttercream used where a meringue type was needed | Sugar about 58 % of the cream | Use SMBC or IMBC (about 29–30 % sugar) |
| Too little salt | No salt, or under 0.2 % | 0.2–0.4 % salt |
| Too much butter for the meringue (buttery, greasy) | Butter above ~3× the white weight | 250–300 g butter per 100 g white |
| Served too cold | Tastes of cold butter, firm and waxy | Serve at 18–22 °C: take cake out 30–60 min before serving |
| Bland butter | Taste the butter alone | Use a flavourful butter; add vanilla, praline paste or chocolate |

Sweetness perception depends on temperature and salt as well as the formula. Taste at serving temperature.

### Air bubbles in the buttercream finish

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Whipped at high speed and used straight away | Visible pinholes in a spread | Beat on lowest speed with the paddle 5–10 min, or stir hard by hand, before coating |
| Whisk used for the final mix | Airy, light cream | Switch to the paddle for the finish |
| Cream too cold to spread smoothly | Drags and tears on the cake | Bring to 19–22 °C |
| Scraper technique | Holes appear in the final pass | Hot, dry scraper for the last pass; fill holes with a thin coat |

### Mousseline split or greasy

| Likely cause | How to test | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Pastry cream too cold for the second butter | Pastry cream below ~17 °C; curdled look | Warm the bowl gently while beating until smooth |
| Pastry cream or butter too warm | Cream above ~24 °C; greasy, shiny, loose | Chill 10–15 min and rewhip |
| All the butter added cold to cold cream | Lumps of butter visible | Use the two-stage method: half into the hot cream, half soft into the cooled cream |
| Pastry cream not smooth before the butter | Lumps of set cream | Beat the cooled pastry cream smooth with the paddle first |
| Underboiled pastry cream thinning (amylase) | Cream thins over hours | Boil the pastry cream 1–2 min after it thickens ([12.2](stage-1/module-12/lesson-02.md)) |

Mousseline behaves like a meringue buttercream: temperature decides it. See [13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md) and [R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md).
