# R1-25 · Crème brûlée and crème caramel

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

An educational formulation built on established technique: two baked custards at standard professional proportions, written for this course so that you bake them side by side and see what egg type, dairy and protein level do to the set.

## Objective

Bake an egg custard to a measured endpoint using a water bath (or a low oven) as a heat limiter, and compare two systems: a yolk-and-cream custard that only just sets (crème brûlée) and a whole-egg-and-milk custard firm enough to unmould (crème caramel).

## Expected result

- **Crème brûlée:** 6 shallow custards, uniformly set from edge to centre, trembling as one sheet when tapped, perfectly smooth when spooned, no holes, no liquid on the surface. A glassy amber sugar crust 1–2 mm thick that cracks under the spoon, over cold custard.
- **Crème caramel:** 6 custards that turn out cleanly and stand with straight sides, smooth and fine-textured when cut, with a pool of thin amber caramel sauce around them.

## Formula

Percentages are of the dairy in each custard.

### Crème brûlée

| Ingredient | % of dairy | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Cream (35 % fat) | 100 | 500 |
| Egg yolks (5–6 large) | 20 | 100 |
| Caster sugar | 18 | 90 |
| Vanilla extract (or 1 pod) | 1 | 5 |
| Fine salt | 0.1 | 0.5 |
| **Total custard** | **139.1** | **695.5** |
| Topping: caster or demerara sugar | — | 36–48 (6–8 g per ramekin) |

**Yield:** 6 shallow crème brûlée dishes (10–12 cm across, 2.5–3 cm deep, about 125 ml) filled with **110 g** each = 660 g; the rest is lost in the sieve and the foam you skim.

### Crème caramel

| Ingredient | % of dairy | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Whole milk (3.5 % fat) | 100 | 500 |
| Whole eggs (3 large, out of shell) | 30 | 150 |
| Egg yolks (2 large) | 8 | 40 |
| Caster sugar | 20 | 100 |
| Vanilla extract (or 1 pod) | 1 | 5 |
| **Total custard** | **159** | **795** |
| Caramel: sugar | — | 150 |
| Caramel: water | — | 50 |

**Yield:** 6 ramekins or dariole moulds of about 150 ml, each with about **25 g** caramel and **130 g** custard (780 g); the caramel loses its water as it cooks, leaving about 150 g.

**Scaling:** ×0.5 gives 3 of each (brûlée 347.75 g; caramel 397.5 g custard, 75 g sugar + 25 g water for caramel, but caramel is easier to control at the full quantity: make the full batch and pour the surplus onto a silicone mat as decoration). ×2 gives 12 of each; bake in two water-bath pans so the water depth is right in both.

## Equipment

- Scale reading to 1 g (0.1 g for salt); instant-read probe thermometer; oven thermometer.
- Two saucepans (one heavy-based, 16–18 cm, light-coloured inside for the caramel).
- Whisk, spatula, fine sieve, a jug with a spout (1 L), ladle.
- 6 shallow brûlée dishes and 6 ramekins/dariole moulds (150 ml).
- Two roasting tins deep enough for water halfway up the moulds; a folded cloth or paper towel for the base of each.
- Kettle; kitchen torch (butane) for the brûlée top.
- Thin knife; serving plates with a rim.

## Mise en place

- Oven rack in the middle. Preheat to **150 °C (300 °F)**, conventional (fan off). Check with the oven thermometer.
- Line each roasting tin with a folded cloth or two layers of paper towel; set the empty moulds in them.
- Boil a kettle of water, then let it cool to about **60–70 °C** by the time you fill the bath.
- Separate eggs and weigh yolks and whole eggs.

## Procedure

### A. Caramel for the crème caramel (make first)

1. **Cook the caramel** as a wet caramel ([R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md)): 150 g sugar and 50 g water in the heavy pan over medium heat, stirring only until the sugar dissolves, then not at all (swirl the pan instead). Brush the pan sides with a wet pastry brush if crystals form.
2. **Endpoint:** medium amber, about **170–180 °C (340–355 °F)**, 8–12 min. Lighter caramel is sweet and bland; darker is bitter.
3. **Pour at once** about **25 g** into each ramekin and tilt to coat the base (and a little of the sides). It sets hard within a minute. Put the ramekins back in their tin.

### B. The custards

4. **Crème brûlée mix.** Heat the cream with 30 g of the sugar, the salt and vanilla pod (if using) to **70–80 °C**. Whisk the yolks with the remaining 60 g sugar just to combine (**do not foam**). Temper: pour the hot cream slowly onto the yolks, stirring with the whisk held down in the bowl (stir, do not beat). Add extract now if using.
5. **Crème caramel mix.** Heat the milk with 30 g of the sugar and the vanilla pod (if using) to **70–80 °C**. Stir the whole eggs, yolks and remaining 70 g sugar together until the whites are broken up but not foamy. Temper with the hot milk as in step 4.
6. **Strain** each mix through the fine sieve into a jug. **Skim** the foam from the surface with a spoon, or let the jug stand 10 min and skim again.
7. **Fill.** Pour **110 g** into each brûlée dish and **130 g** into each caramel-lined ramekin (weigh the first one of each to calibrate your pour). Burst any remaining surface bubbles with a quick pass of the torch flame, or a toothpick.
8. **Water bath.** Put the tins on the oven rack, then pour the 60–70 °C water into each tin to **half to two-thirds** of the mould height. Filling in the oven avoids carrying a sloshing tin.

### C. Baking

9. **Bake at 150 °C (300 °F)**:
   - Crème brûlée (shallow): **25–35 min**.
   - Crème caramel (deeper): **35–45 min**.
10. **Start checking** 5 min before the earliest time. **Endpoint:**
    - **Centre temperature 78–82 °C** (probe the centre of one mould at half depth): brûlée ~80 °C, caramel 80–82 °C.
    - **Wobble:** tap the side of the dish. The surface moves as one sheet, like set jelly; the centre 2–3 cm may tremble more but does not ripple like a liquid.
    - **Crème caramel:** a thin knife inserted 1 cm from the centre comes out clean and wet, not milky.
11. **Remove** the moulds from the water bath at once (tongs or a folded towel); they keep cooking in hot water. Cool on a rack **20–30 min**.
12. **Chill.** Refrigerate uncovered until cold, then cover. **Crème brûlée ≥ 4 h. Crème caramel ≥ 6 h, ideally overnight (12 h)**, so the caramel dissolves into a sauce.

**Low-oven alternative for crème brûlée (no water bath):** fan oven at **100–110 °C (212–230 °F)**, dishes on a tray, **45–70 min** to the same 80 °C centre. The oven air is the heat limiter. Verify your oven holds this low temperature within about 5 °C before relying on it.

### D. Finishing

13. **Brûlée top** (just before serving): blot any condensation with paper towel. Sprinkle **6–8 g** sugar in an even layer, tilt and tap off excess. Hold the torch flame tip 3–5 cm from the sugar and move constantly until the sugar melts and turns medium amber. Optional: a second thin layer, torched again, for a thicker crust. Rest **1–2 min** for the caramel to harden. Serve within **30 min**: the caramel absorbs moisture from the custard and softens.
14. **Unmould the crème caramel:** run a thin knife around the edge, pressing against the mould, in one continuous circle. Put a rimmed plate on top, invert, and give a sharp downward shake. Lift the mould; the caramel pours out as sauce. A thin layer of hard caramel left in the mould is normal.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Caramel at 170–180 °C sticks to skin and burns deeply: dry utensils, no children or pets near the stove, a bowl of cold water ready. Pour water baths in the oven, not across the kitchen. Torch: work on a heatproof surface, away from paper, cloths and hair; close the valve after use. Contains egg and milk.

</div>

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mixing | Stir, do not beat; strain and skim | Air becomes foam on top and holes in the gel |
| Caramel colour | Medium amber, 170–180 °C | Pale: bland; dark: bitter |
| Heat limit | 150 °C oven with water bath at half-to-two-thirds height, or 100–110 °C fan oven | Edges cannot overshoot while the centre sets |
| Endpoint | Centre 78–82 °C; sheet wobble; clean knife (caramel) | Below: liquid centre; above 85 °C: holes, weeping, grain |
| Out of the bath | Immediately at endpoint | Hot water keeps cooking |
| Chill | Brûlée ≥ 4 h; caramel ≥ 6 h (overnight) | The gel firms cold; caramel needs time to dissolve |
| Storage | ≤ 5 °C, 2–3 days, uncaramelized top | Food safety; torch only before service |

## Common failures

- **Weeping, grainy, holes.** See [Baked custard weeping, grainy or full of holes](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=baked-custard-weeping-grainy-or-full-of-holes).
- **Liquid centre.** See [Baked custard not setting](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=baked-custard-not-setting).
- **Foam layer, bubbles, tunnels.** See [Bubbles, foam or tunnels in baked custard](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=bubbles-foam-or-tunnels-in-baked-custard).
- **Crème caramel will not unmould or breaks.** Underbaked (too soft), not chilled long enough, knife not run to the base, or you substituted cream and yolks for the milk and whole eggs.
- **Caramel crystallized in the pan.** Stirred after the sugar dissolved or sugar crystals on the pan wall; see [R1-10](recipes/R1-10-caramel.md).

## Scientific explanation

Both are egg-protein gels formed without stirring, so the network sets as one continuous structure. What differs is protein concentration and type. The brûlée carries about 16 g of yolk protein in 695 g of mix (~2.3 %), in cream whose fat droplets interrupt the network: a soft, rich gel that just holds in its dish. The crème caramel carries about 25 g of egg protein in 795 g (~3.2 %), including egg-white proteins that form a firmer, more elastic gel, in milk with little fat: a gel strong enough to stand when turned out ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md), [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)).

The water bath works because water cannot exceed 100 °C and, with evaporation, stays around 80–90 °C in a 150 °C oven. The mould walls sit near the custard's target temperature, so the edge and centre set almost together. Without it, the edge reaches 90 °C and more while the centre is still at 70 °C; the edge proteins over-coagulate, contract and squeeze out water (weeping), and trapped water turns to steam, leaving holes ([SC-10](stage-1/science/sc-10-heat-transfer.md)).

The caramel under a crème caramel is hard sugar glass; it is hygroscopic, so over a night in contact with the custard it draws in water and dissolves partly into a syrup ([07.2](stage-1/module-07/lesson-02.md)). The brûlée top is the same glass made seconds before serving, which is why it must be torched at the last moment.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Yolks in brûlée | 16 → 25 % of dairy | Firmer, richer, more yellow; sets at a slightly lower temperature |
| Cream vs milk in brûlée | Part milk | Lighter, firmer set; less rich |
| Whole egg vs yolk in caramel | More yolk, fewer whites | Softer, richer; harder to unmould |
| Sugar | ±5 % of dairy | More: softer, sets higher; less: firmer, sets lower |
| Oven and bath | 150 °C bath vs 100 °C fan vs 170 °C no bath | Even set vs slower even set vs curdled edges and holes |
| Depth of mould | Shallow vs deep | Deeper takes longer and needs gentler heat to avoid overcooked edges |
| Infusions | Coffee, tea, citrus, spices in the hot dairy | Flavour; strain before adding the eggs |

## Experiment suggestions

- Bake two brûlées with and two without a water bath at 150 °C; probe edges and centres at the same time and compare texture after chilling.
- Make a crème caramel with the brûlée formula (cream and yolks) and try to unmould it.
- Stage the endpoint: pull brûlées at 74, 78, 82 and 86 °C centre and compare after chilling, as [X1-33](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md) does for anglaise.
