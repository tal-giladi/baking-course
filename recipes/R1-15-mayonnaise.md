# R1-15 · Mayonnaise — a model emulsion

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation built on established technique: a classic hand-whisked mayonnaise with proportions in the normal range (one yolk to 150–200 g oil), chosen so that the stages of emulsion formation are slow enough to observe and the water-phase ratio is easy to calculate. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe. It is here as a model system for [09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md), not as a pastry component.

## Objective

Make a stable oil-in-water emulsion of about 78 % oil by hand. Control the rate of oil addition, keep the water phase sufficient for the oil, recognize the moment the emulsion forms, and rescue a broken emulsion.

## Expected result

- About 200 g of thick, glossy, pale-yellow mayonnaise that holds a soft peak on a spoon and wobbles like a soft gel when the bowl is tapped.
- No visible oil at the edges or on the surface, no curdled texture.
- Clean, creamy mouthfeel (not greasy), balanced acidity and salt.
- Stable, refrigerated and covered, for 3 days without separating.

## Formula

There is no flour, so percentages are given as **% of total batch** (formula percentage). The oil percentage is the number that matters: it tells you how close you are to the phase-volume limit.

| Ingredient | % of batch | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Egg yolk, **pasteurized** (1 yolk, 18–20 g) | 9.8 | 20 |
| Dijon mustard | 2.9 | 6 |
| Lemon juice or white wine vinegar (split 5 + 5) | 4.9 | 10 |
| Water | 2.9 | 6 |
| Fine salt | 1.0 | 2 |
| Neutral oil (sunflower, rapeseed/canola, grapeseed), 18–22 °C | 78.4 | 160 |
| **Total** | **99.9** (rounding) | **204** |

**Yield:** about 200 g (a few grams stay on the whisk and bowl).
**Water phase:** yolk water (~10 g) + acid (~10 g) + mustard water (~4–5 g) + water (6 g) ≈ **30 g**, about 19 % of the oil weight. Keep the water phase at **15–20 % of the oil** when you change the formula.
**Oil choice:** up to a quarter of the oil (40 g) can be a mild olive oil for flavour. All extra-virgin olive oil tastes bitter when whisked hard and may crystallize and separate in the refrigerator.
**Scaling:** ×0.5 is too small to whisk reliably by hand (use a small bowl or a blender jar). ×2 = 2 yolks (40 g), 12 g mustard, 20 g acid, 12 g water, 4 g salt, 320 g oil. For more than ×2, use a food processor or an immersion blender.

## Equipment

- Scale (1 g).
- Medium stainless-steel or glass bowl (1.5–2 L), standing on a damp towel twisted into a ring so it does not spin.
- Balloon whisk (or an immersion blender and a tall narrow jar for the variant).
- Jug or squeeze bottle with a narrow spout for the oil, weighed (so you can read oil added from the scale).
- Probe thermometer.
- Clean, lidded container for storage.

## Mise en place

1. **Temperature.** Bring yolk and oil to **18–22 °C**. Check the oil with a probe; cold oil is the most common cause of failure. If the yolk is cold, stand it (in its bowl) in warm water for 5 minutes.
2. Weigh the oil into the jug. Put the jug on the scale and tare it, so the scale reads negative grams as you pour: that is your cumulative oil added.
3. Measure the lemon juice into two 5 g portions. Weigh the water and salt.
4. Wipe the bowl and whisk dry and clean.

## Procedure

**Step 1 — Base (1 min).** Whisk the yolk, mustard, salt and the first 5 g of acid until smooth and slightly frothy. The salt dissolves and the mixture thickens very slightly.

**Step 2 — Start the emulsion: first 30 g oil, drop by drop (3–5 min).** Whisking constantly and fairly fast, add oil **one drop at a time**, about a drop every 1–2 seconds; tap the jug spout or use a squeeze bottle. After each few drops, check that no oil is visible on the surface. **Endpoint:** around 20–30 g of oil the mixture turns opaque, pale and noticeably thick, like a thin custard; the whisk leaves faint trails. That is the emulsion forming. Do not speed up before you see it.

**Step 3 — Build (to 100 g oil, 3–4 min).** Increase to a thin thread, about 5–10 g every 10 seconds, whisking constantly. Pause pouring (keep whisking) if oil starts to pool. **Endpoint:** thick, glossy, holds whisk marks clearly.

**Step 4 — Adjust the water phase (at ~100 g oil).** Whisk in the remaining 5 g acid and the 6 g water. The mayonnaise loosens slightly and becomes smoother.

**Step 5 — Finish (to 160 g oil, 2–3 min).** Continue in a thin thread. **Endpoint:** thick, glossy mayonnaise that holds a soft peak on the whisk; a spoon drawn through it leaves a clean track. If at any point it becomes very stiff, shiny and gel-like and resists the whisk ("tight"), stop adding oil and whisk in 3–5 g water before continuing.

**Step 6 — Season and check.** Taste: adjust salt (0.5 g at a time) and acid (a few drops). Record the final weight. Check the temperature (should be 18–24 °C).

**Step 7 — Store.** Transfer to a clean container, press film onto the surface, lid, label with the date and time, and refrigerate at **4 °C or below** immediately. Use within **3 days**.

### Rescue: if it breaks

If the mixture never thickens, or suddenly thins, looks curdled or has oil pooling:

1. Stop. In a clean bowl, whisk a new pasteurized yolk (or 10 g water, or 5 g mustard with 5 g water) at room temperature.
2. Add the broken mixture to it **drop by drop**, whisking hard, until a thick emulsion forms (usually after 20–30 g).
3. Add the rest of the broken mixture in a thin stream, then any remaining oil.
4. Record the cause in your notebook.

### Variant: immersion-blender mayonnaise

Use the same formula with all the acid and water at the start. In a jar only slightly wider than the blender head (7–8 cm), put yolk, mustard, acid, water and salt; pour the oil on top and let it settle for 30 s. Put the blender flat on the bottom, run on high for 10–15 s without moving until a white emulsion climbs the jar, then slowly tilt and lift to incorporate the remaining oil (another 15–20 s). The result is stiffer and paler (smaller droplets). It works because the blades emulsify a little oil at a time at the bottom of the jar.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Egg | Pasteurized yolk | Raw yolk is not cooked at any point |
| Ingredient temperature | Oil and yolk 18–22 °C | Cold oil is too viscous to break into droplets; warm conditions thin the emulsion |
| First 30 g oil | Drop by drop until the mixture thickens and turns opaque | No emulsion yet to absorb oil; the most common failure point |
| Water phase | ≥ 15–20 % of oil weight (add water when tight) | Films between packed droplets must stay intact |
| Storage | ≤ 4 °C immediately, ≤ 3 days | Raw-egg product; microbial safety |

## Common failures

- Never thickens, or thins suddenly and turns oily → [Broken mayonnaise](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=broken-mayonnaise)
- Greasy mouthfeel, oil beading or separating in the refrigerator → [Greasy or oily emulsion](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=greasy-or-oily-emulsion)

## Scientific explanation

Mayonnaise is an oil-in-water emulsion: oil droplets of a few micrometres dispersed in a small water phase of yolk water, acid and mustard ([09.3](stage-1/module-09/lesson-03.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). Yolk phospholipids (lecithin) and lipoproteins adsorb at each oil droplet's surface, lower the interfacial tension so the whisk can make small droplets, and form a film that makes colliding droplets bounce apart rather than merge. Mustard contributes additional surface-active material and fine particles. At the start there is no emulsion, so each drop of oil must be broken up individually; once a viscous emulsion exists, it shears incoming oil efficiently. As oil accumulates, the droplets pack beyond the ~64 % of random sphere packing and deform against each other, separated by thin films of water phase. That packed, deformed structure is what makes mayonnaise stiff without any thickener. If oil is added faster than it can be dispersed, or the water films become too thin for the volume of oil, droplets coalesce and the emulsion breaks. A fresh base provides new continuous phase and emulsifier into which the broken mixture can be re-dispersed.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Oil | 160 → 200 g, same water phase | Stiffer, then tight; risk of breaking unless ~5–8 g more water is added |
| Water phase | Add 10 g extra water | Softer, pourable; a dressing-like mayonnaise |
| Mustard | 6 → 0 g | Slightly harder to start, less stable; milder flavour |
| Yolk | Replace with 50 g pasteurized whole egg | Lighter, looser, less rich emulsion (blender method works best) |
| Oil | Neutral → 100 % extra-virgin olive | Bitter, heavier; may separate in the refrigerator |
| Temperature | Oil at 8 °C | Slow to form, frequent breaking |
| Method | Whisk → immersion blender | Faster, smaller droplets, stiffer, paler |

## Experiment suggestions

- Oil-addition rate: two half-batches, one with the first 30 g drop by drop, one with it poured in 10 g lots; record whether and when each forms.
- Phase-volume limit: keep adding oil to a finished batch with no extra water; weigh the oil at the point it breaks; repeat with 5 g water added at 150 g oil.
- Oil temperature: 8 °C vs 20 °C oil, same everything else.
- Stability: store samples made with neutral oil and with extra-virgin olive oil at 4 °C; inspect after 24 and 72 h.
