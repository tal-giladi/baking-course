# R1-18 · Génoise

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation built on established technique. The génoise (a warm whole-egg foam with flour and a little melted butter) is a classical preparation described in all the professional pastry texts; these proportions sit in the normal range for a buttered génoise and were chosen for a single 20 cm layer and for measurement. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make a 20 cm génoise that is 4–5 cm tall, level, with a fine, even, springy crumb. Warm and whip a whole-egg foam to the ribbon stage, fold in flour and tempered butter with a measured loss of air (specific gravity), bake to springback, and prepare the layer for splitting and soaking.

## Expected result

- One round layer, 20 cm diameter, **4–5 cm tall**, flat or very gently rounded top, about 400–420 g after cooling.
- Thin, even, golden-brown top skin; pale golden sides.
- Fine, even, pale yellow crumb with no large holes, no dense or rubbery band at the base, no streaks of flour or butter.
- Springy, slightly dry to the touch: it is designed to be split into 2–3 layers and moistened with syrup ([10.4](stage-1/module-10/lesson-04.md)).

## Formula

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Whole eggs, out of shell (about 4 large) | 166.7 | 200 |
| Caster or fine granulated sugar | 100.0 | 120 |
| Cake flour or soft plain flour (8–10 % protein), sifted | 100.0 | 120 |
| Unsalted butter, melted | 25.0 | 30 |
| Fine salt | 0.4 | 0.5 |
| **Total** | **392.1** | **470.5** |
| *Butter and flour for the pan; parchment disc (not in total)* | | |

**Yield:** 470.5 g of batter → one 20 cm round layer, pan at least 5 cm deep (batter about 3.3 cm deep), about 400–420 g baked.
**Flour variant:** replace 20 g of the flour with 20 g cornstarch (flour 100 g + cornstarch 20 g; percentages stay on the 120 g total). The crumb becomes slightly finer, softer and more tender, and the layer a little less strong; a useful substitute where true cake flour is not sold.
**Flavour:** 2–4 g vanilla extract added to the eggs after warming, or the grated zest of half a lemon folded in with the flour.
**Scaling:** by pan area ([10.4](stage-1/module-10/lesson-04.md)): 18 cm ×0.81; 23 cm ×1.32; 24 cm ×1.44. A 15 cm layer at the same depth needs ×0.56 (about 265 g of batter). For a 30 × 40 cm rolled sheet use a biscuit ([10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md)) rather than génoise.

## Equipment

- Scale (1 g, and 0.1 g for the salt if available).
- Stand mixer with whisk (or a hand mixer; allow about double the whipping time).
- Heatproof mixer bowl and a saucepan it sits on without touching the water (bain-marie).
- Probe thermometer; oven thermometer.
- Round cake pan **20 cm**, at least 5 cm deep, light-coloured metal; parchment disc.
- Sieve; large silicone spatula or large balloon whisk for folding.
- Specific gravity cup (known water weight).
- Small bowl for tempering butter; cooling rack; parchment.

## Mise en place

1. **Oven.** Rack in the centre. Preheat to **175 °C (345 °F)** conventional (160–165 °C fan, fan preferably off) for at least 20 min.
2. **Pan.** Line the base with a parchment disc. Brush the sides thinly with soft butter and dust with flour; tap out the excess.
3. **Flour.** Sift the flour (and cornstarch, if used) and salt together twice onto a sheet of parchment, so you can pour it in three even additions.
4. **Butter.** Melt the butter and hold it at **40–50 °C** (warm to the touch, not hot).
5. **Bain-marie.** Bring 3–4 cm of water in the saucepan to a bare simmer (small bubbles at the base, no rolling boil), then lower the heat.
6. Set out the SG cup, a timer and a probe.

## Procedure

**Step 1 — Warm the eggs (3–5 min).** Whisk the eggs and sugar together in the mixer bowl. Set the bowl over the bain-marie and whisk **constantly** by hand until the mixture reaches **40–45 °C** on the probe. The sugar dissolves (no grit when rubbed between your fingers) and the mixture becomes slightly foamy and thinner. Remove from the heat at once; above about 50 °C, the egg starts to cook.

**Step 2 — Whip (7–10 min total).** Whip on **high speed** for **5–8 min** until the foam is about **three to four times** its starting volume, pale ivory, and has cooled to about body temperature. Then whip on **medium** for **1–2 min** to refine the bubbles. **Ribbon endpoint:** lifted, the foam falls from the whisk in a thick ribbon that stays on the surface for **3–5 s** before sinking. Add vanilla (if used) in the last 30 s. Optional: measure foam SG (indicatively 0.25–0.35).

**Step 3 — Fold in the flour (about 2 min).** Take the bowl off the mixer. Sift or scatter one-third of the flour over the surface and fold it in with a large spatula: cut down through the centre, sweep along the bottom, lift and turn over, rotate the bowl a quarter turn. About **10–15 strokes** per addition, stopping as soon as no dry flour is visible. Repeat twice more. Check the bottom of the bowl for flour pockets.

**Step 4 — Temper and fold in the butter (1 min).** Scoop about **50 g of batter** into the warm melted butter and stir until homogeneous. Pour this back over the batter and fold in with **5–8 strokes**, until no streaks of butter remain.

**Step 5 — Measure (30 s).** Fill the SG cup gently and level it. **Target SG about 0.4–0.5** (indicative). Above about 0.55, note it: the cake will be lower. Pour the batter in the cup back into the bowl.

**Step 6 — Pan and bake immediately.** Pour the batter into the pan from close to the surface, in one place, letting it spread. Tilt the pan gently to level it; do not bang it. Into the oven **within 2–3 min** of the last fold. Bake at **175 °C for 22–28 min**. Do not open the door in the first 18 min.

**Step 7 — Doneness.** **Done when:** the top is evenly golden, springs back fully when pressed lightly in the centre, and the edges have pulled slightly away from the sides; a probe in the centre reads about **95–99 °C** (indicative); a skewer comes out clean. A génoise that is pressed and leaves a dent, or makes a faint crackling, bubbling sound, needs 2–3 more minutes.

**Step 8 — Unmould and cool.** Cool in the pan for **5 min**. Run a thin knife around the edge, invert onto a rack lined with parchment, peel off the base paper, and cool completely (**at least 1 h**). Cooling it upside down flattens the top.

**Step 9 — Use or store.** For layers, wrap and rest the cooled génoise for a few hours or overnight at room temperature: it cuts more cleanly. Split horizontally into 2–3 layers with a long serrated knife, soak with syrup ([10.4](stage-1/module-10/lesson-04.md)). Wrapped, it keeps 1–2 days at room temperature or about 1–2 months frozen.

**Safety:** Keep the bain-marie water at a bare simmer and the bowl dry underneath when you move it to the mixer; steam burns. The warmed eggs are not pasteurized; do not taste the batter. Contains wheat, egg and milk.

</div>

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Warming temperature | 40–45 °C, whisking constantly | Dissolves sugar, lowers viscosity: more volume. Above ~50 °C egg coagulates |
| Whipping endpoint | 3–4× volume, ribbon holds 3–5 s, refined at medium | Under-whipped foam: dense cake with a heavy base |
| Folding | 3 additions, 10–15 strokes each, just combined | Every extra stroke loses air |
| Butter | 40–50 °C, tempered with ~50 g batter | Hot or untempered fat sinks and bursts the foam: rubbery base |
| Batter SG | ~0.4–0.5 | Objective check of how much foam survived |
| Time to oven | ≤ 3 min | Foam drains and coarsens while standing |
| Doneness | Springback, edges pulling away, ~95–99 °C | Underbaked génoise sinks and is gummy |

## Scientific explanation

Warming whole eggs with sugar dissolves the sugar and lowers the viscosity of the egg, so the proteins can unfold at the air-water interface and form films around more, smaller bubbles ([09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). The dissolved sugar thickens the liquid between the bubbles and slows drainage, which stabilizes the foam. Flour, sifted and folded, is suspended in the bubble walls; in the oven the air expands, water turns to steam inside the cells, and then the egg proteins coagulate and the starch gelatinizes, fixing the foam as a sponge ([10.1](stage-1/module-10/lesson-01.md)). No chemical leavener is needed because the foam already contains the cells. Fat is the enemy of a protein foam: it displaces the proteins from the bubble films. Tempering the butter with batter disperses it in fine droplets throughout the batter instead of letting it sink and burst the cells on its way down. The small amount of butter tenderizes the crumb and improves flavour and keeping quality; its low sugar and fat, compared with a butter cake, make génoise springy and slightly dry, which is exactly what a layer that will be soaked needs.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Warming | 40–45 °C → no warming (room-temperature eggs) | Longer whipping, less volume, coarser and slightly lower cake |
| Whipping | Ribbon 3–5 s → just thick, no ribbon | Denser cake with a heavy, wet base |
| Folding strokes | 10–15 → 30 per addition | Higher batter SG, lower, tighter cake |
| Flour | 120 g flour → 100 g flour + 20 g cornstarch | Finer, softer, more tender, slightly weaker |
| Butter | 25 % → 0 % | Lighter, drier, stales faster (génoise ordinaire) |
| Butter | 25 % → 40 % | Richer, moister, harder to fold; risk of lower volume |
| Sugar | 100 → 80 % | Less stable foam, drier, less tender; paler crust |
| Oven | 175 → 190 °C | Faster set; domed top, darker crust; risk of dip if pulled early |
