# R1-16 · Sabayon — a model cooked egg foam

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation built on established technique: the classic French sabayon / Italian zabaglione, yolks whisked with sugar and wine over a bain-marie until they cook into a thick foam. The proportions sit in the traditional range (roughly equal weights of yolk and wine, sugar at 60–100 % of the yolk) and were chosen so the foam is stable enough to measure. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make a heated yolk foam that is both aerated and cooked: whisk yolks, sugar and wine over a bain-marie to **72–78 °C** so that the yolk proteins partly coagulate around the air bubbles, giving a thick, voluminous, ribbon-forming foam without any scrambled egg. Measure the endpoint by temperature and by ribbon, and measure the foam's specific gravity.

## Expected result

- About 215 g of warm, pale-gold, very light foam, 3–4 times the volume of the raw mixture (about 600–800 ml), with a specific gravity of roughly 0.25–0.35.
- Falls from the whisk in a thick ribbon that sits on the surface for 3–5 s before sinking.
- Completely smooth: no curds or streaks of set egg, no liquid pooling at the bottom of the bowl.
- Tastes cooked (no raw-egg flavour), sweet, winey, with a silky, airy mouthfeel.
- Served warm, it holds for about 5–10 minutes before it starts to drain; the chilled variant holds for 1–2 days.

## Formula

There is no flour, so percentages are on **yolk weight** (yolk = 100 %), the usual way to express yolk-based preparations.

| Ingredient | % of yolk | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Egg yolks (4, about 20 g each) | 100 | 80 |
| Caster or fine granulated sugar | 75 | 60 |
| Marsala (or sweet white wine, e.g. Moscato, Sauternes-style) | 100 | 80 |
| Fine salt (a pinch) | 0.4 | 0.3 |
| **Total** | **275.4** | **220.3** |

**Yield:** about 215 g (a little water and alcohol evaporate) → 4 servings of ~55 g, or a sauce for 6–8 portions of fruit.
**Non-alcoholic version:** replace the Marsala with 60 g orange juice + 20 g lemon juice, or 80 g passion-fruit juice. The acid makes the yolks set sooner; see the CCPs.
**Scaling:** ×0.5 (2 yolks) works in a small bowl but heats very fast; watch the temperature closely. ×2 (8 yolks) needs a large bowl (3–4 L) and 10–15 min of whisking; by hand it is tiring, so use a hand mixer on medium over the bain-marie.

## Equipment

- Scale (1 g, and 0.1 g for the salt if you have one).
- Round-bottomed stainless-steel bowl (2–3 L) that sits on a saucepan with its base **above** the water.
- Saucepan with 3–4 cm of water.
- Balloon whisk (or hand mixer).
- Probe thermometer (fast-reading).
- Bowl of ice water large enough to hold the mixing bowl (for the cold variant and to stop cooking).
- 100 ml cup and scale for specific gravity (optional).
- Serving glasses, warmed if serving hot.

## Mise en place

1. Separate the eggs cold (keep the whites for [X1-24](experiments/X1-24-egg-white-foam.md)). Weigh 80 g yolk.
2. Bring the water in the saucepan to a **bare simmer** (small bubbles on the base, not boiling; about 85–90 °C). Check the bowl sits on the pan without touching the water.
3. Prepare the fruit or glasses you will serve with, so the sabayon is used the moment it is ready.
4. For the cold variant, whip the cream (below) first and keep it refrigerated.

## Procedure

**Step 1 — Whisk cold (1–2 min).** Off the heat, whisk yolks, sugar and salt together in the bowl until the mixture is pale and slightly thickened and the sugar has mostly dissolved. Whisk in the Marsala.

**Step 2 — Whisk over the bain-marie (6–10 min).** Set the bowl over the simmering water. Whisk continuously and vigorously, reaching all of the base and the sides of the bowl; the sides and base heat fastest and are where egg scrambles. What you see:

| Temperature of the foam | What happens |
|---|---|
| ~30–45 °C | Frothy, thin; large bubbles; volume starts to grow |
| ~50–65 °C | Volume increases fast; foam turns paler and finer; still flows easily |
| ~65–72 °C | Foam thickens noticeably; whisk leaves trails; bottom of the bowl visible as you whisk |
| **72–78 °C** | **Endpoint:** thick, voluminous, falls in a ribbon that holds 3–5 s |
| Above ~80 °C | Risk: small curds form at the bowl edges; foam turns grainy, then loses volume and leaks liquid |

Check with the probe every 30–60 seconds from about 3 minutes on (stop whisking briefly, probe the centre of the foam, and continue). If the foam is heating faster than about 10 °C per minute, lift the bowl off the pan for a few seconds while whisking.

**Step 3 — Endpoint.** Stop at **72–78 °C** when the ribbon holds 3–5 s and no liquid pools at the base of the bowl when you tilt it. Take the bowl off the heat and **keep whisking for 30 s** so the hot sides do not overcook the foam.

**Step 4a — Serve warm.** Spoon immediately over fruit or into warm glasses. Warm sabayon starts to drain within 5–10 minutes.

**Step 4b — Or cool (for a stable, cold sabayon).** Set the bowl in ice water and whisk until the foam is below 20 °C (3–5 min). It will thicken further. Fold in **100 g whipping cream (35 % fat) whipped to soft peak**, in two additions ([09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md) folding). Refrigerate, covered, and use within 24 hours.

**Step 5 — Measure (optional).** Fill a 100 ml cup level with foam and weigh it; SG = grams ÷ 100. Note the SG and time. Leave a small sample in a clear glass and note when liquid first appears at the base.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Cook the foam to at least **72 °C** (above the 71 °C / 160 °F that the FDA advises for egg dishes, [FDA-eggs]); use pasteurized eggs if you cannot measure the temperature. Steam from the bain-marie scalds: keep your hand and forearm out of the steam at the edge of the bowl, and use a cloth to hold it. Alcohol is only partly driven off at these temperatures; the finished sabayon contains alcohol. Egg and (in some wines) sulfites are declarable allergens.

</div>

### Variants

- **Zabaglione:** the Italian name and original; traditionally Marsala, served warm with biscuits.
- **Savoury sabayon:** no sugar, dry white wine or champagne and a little stock, seasoned; the base of glazed fish sauces (Stage 2).
- **Fruit sabayon:** juice instead of wine (see the formula note); glaze under a grill for a gratin of berries.
- **Pâte à bombe (preview of M13):** the same yolk foam, but cooked by pouring hot sugar syrup (118–121 °C) onto whipping yolks instead of using a bain-marie; the base of mousses and parfaits.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bain-marie | Bare simmer; bowl above the water | Direct contact or boiling water overheats the base and scrambles the egg |
| Whisking | Continuous, reaching base and sides | Hot spots at the bowl surface coagulate before the centre is cooked |
| Endpoint temperature | 72–78 °C, never above 80 °C | Below ~70 °C: thin, unstable, not safe; above ~80 °C: curds, grainy, collapse |
| Ribbon | Holds 3–5 s; no liquid pooling | Confirms enough air and enough protein set |
| Stop cooking | Whisk 30 s off heat; ice bath for the cold version | Carry-over heat from the bowl can push the foam past 80 °C |
| Acidic liquid (juice) | Aim for the low end, 72–75 °C | Acid lowers the coagulation temperature |

## Common failures

- Curds or scrambled bits, grainy foam, liquid at the bottom → [Sabayon collapses or scrambles](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=sabayon-collapses-or-scrambles)
- Thin foam that will not hold a ribbon → same entry (undercooked or under-whisked)
- Foam drains quickly after serving → [Foam weeps liquid](troubleshooting/eggs-foams-emulsions.md?id=foam-weeps-liquid)

## Scientific explanation

A sabayon is a foam and a cooked egg preparation at the same time ([09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). Whisking drags air into the yolk-sugar-wine mixture, and yolk proteins and lipoproteins adsorb at the bubble surfaces. A raw yolk foam is weak, because the yolk's fat limits how strong those films can be. Heat does two things. First, it lowers viscosity so the whisk incorporates air faster, which is why volume increases steeply between 50 and 65 °C. Second, from about 65 °C, the yolk proteins start to coagulate ([09.1](stage-1/module-09/lesson-01.md)), and the liquid between the bubbles thickens into a soft gel. That gel is what lets the foam hold a ribbon and stand in a glass. The sugar and the wine dilute the yolk, so it sets higher and more gently than pure yolk (~70 °C), which gives the working window of 72–78 °C. Above ~80 °C the proteins over-coagulate into curds, the gel contracts and squeezes out liquid (syneresis), and the bubbles lose their support, so the foam turns grainy and falls. Once cooked, the foam still drains and coarsens over minutes; chilling it and folding in whipped cream slows both by making the continuous phase stiffer.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Endpoint temperature | 65 °C → 75 °C | 65 °C: thinner, drains within minutes, raw taste. 75 °C: thick, stable |
| Endpoint temperature | 75 °C → 85 °C | Grainy, curdled edges, loss of volume, weeping |
| Sugar | 60 → 100 % of yolk | Sweeter, more stable, sets slightly higher, glossier |
| Sugar | 75 → 40 % | Less stable, scrambles more easily |
| Liquid | Wine → citrus juice | Sets sooner (acid); aim for the low end of the window |
| Liquid | 100 → 150 % of yolk | Lighter, more fluid, needs more cooking time; less stable |
| Whisk | Hand → hand mixer on medium | More volume, finer foam; easier to overshoot temperature unnoticed |

## Experiment suggestions

- Endpoint series: three small batches (2 yolks each) stopped at 65, 75 and 82 °C; compare SG, ribbon time, drainage after 10 minutes and texture. This is a foam version of [X1-25](experiments/X1-25-egg-coagulation.md).
- Sugar level: 40 % vs 100 % sugar on yolk, same endpoint; compare stability.
- Warm vs cold service: measure drainage of warm foam and chilled foam with folded cream over 30 minutes.
