# R1-05 · Levain: building and maintaining a sourdough starter

<span class="tag established">Established technique</span>

Establishing a starter from flour and water by repeated feeding is traditional, established technique described in every professional bread text. The specific schedule, quantities and checkpoints below are this course's educational version of it, designed around small feeds (20–50 g flour) and measurable readiness criteria.

## Objective

Establish a stable, vigorous wheat sourdough starter at 100 % hydration from flour and water in 7–14 days, prove it is ready by measurement, set up a maintenance regime, and build a levain for [R1-06](recipes/R1-06-sourdough-loaf.md).

## Expected result

A starter that, fed 1:2:2 at 24–26 °C:

- **at least doubles** in a straight-sided jar (≥ +100 %) and peaks within **4–8 h**, on three consecutive feeds, with peak times within about 1 h of each other;
- has a domed top at peak, bubbles throughout the mass (visible through the glass), and a thick, stringy, mousse-like texture when stirred;
- smells pleasantly sour: yogurt, green apple, mild vinegar, fruit or wine; not cheesy, not like nail polish when used at peak;
- reads **pH ~3.8–4.2** at peak if you have a calibrated meter.

## Formula

### Establishment feeds (days 1–4)

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Wholemeal rye or wholemeal wheat flour | 100 | 50 |
| Water at ~26–28 °C | 100 | 50 |
| **Total** | **200** | **100** |

### Transition feeds (days 5–7 onward), 1:1:1

| Ingredient | Ratio | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Starter (seed) | 1 | 40 |
| Flour: 70 % bread flour + 30 % wholemeal | 1 | 40 (28 + 12) |
| Water at ~26 °C | 1 | 40 |
| **Total** | | **120** |

### Maintenance feed, 1:2:2 (once established)

| Ingredient | Ratio | Baker's % of feed flour | Grams |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ripe starter (seed) | 1 | 50 | 20 |
| Flour: 90 % bread flour + 10 % wholemeal | 2 | 100 | 40 (36 + 4) |
| Water | 2 | 100 | 40 |
| **Total** | 5 | | **100** |

Hydration is 100 % throughout (the seed is itself 100 %, so the mix stays at 100 %).
**Scaling:** multiply all three by the same factor. 1:5:5 with a 10 g seed is 10 + 50 + 50 = 110 g. 1:2:2 with a 10 g seed is 10 + 20 + 20 = 50 g.
**Levain build for R1-06:** 20 g ripe starter + 65 g bread flour + 65 g water = 150 g (see step 11).

## Equipment

- Scale (1 g). A 0.1 g scale is useful for small seeds.
- Two identical straight-sided glass jars, 350–500 ml, with loose lids. Straight sides make rise % meaningful; tapered jars exaggerate it.
- Rubber bands or a marker, a ruler.
- Thermometer to place next to the jar. A warm spot at 24–27 °C: an oven with only the light on (check the temperature: some get too hot), the top of a fridge, a cooler with a jar of warm water, or a seed-germination mat.
- Spatula, small bowl for discard.
- Optional: pH meter with pH 4 and pH 7 calibration buffers (strips are hard to read in thick paste); a phone for time-lapse photos.

## Mise en place

1. Use unbleached flour. Bleached white flour may start poorly; wholemeal rye starts fastest.
2. Use water you would drink. Heavily chlorinated tap water can be left open overnight or boiled and cooled, though most municipal water works fine.
3. Find and measure your warm spot for a day before starting. Record min/max.
4. Label jar A (culture) and keep jar B clean for swaps.

## Procedure

**Day 1 — Mix.** In jar A, mix 50 g wholemeal flour and 50 g water at 26–28 °C to a thick paste with no dry flour. Scrape down the sides, put a rubber band at the surface, lid loosely, and keep at **24–27 °C**. Record temperature and the time.

**Day 2 (24 h) — First feed.** Note any rise and smell. Stir. Discard all but **50 g**, then add 50 g wholemeal flour and 50 g water. Mix, scrape down, reset the band. Many starters show little change today; some begin the false rise.

**Day 3 (48 h) — Feed.** Expect the **false rise**: a vigorous, foamy expansion, sometimes 2–3×, and unpleasant smells (cheese, sour milk, socks). This is not yet your sourdough ([05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md)). Discard to 50 g, feed 50 g wholemeal + 50 g water.

**Day 4 (72 h) — Feed.** Activity usually drops sharply: little rise, perhaps liquid on top, sour smell. This is normal. Same feed: 50 g starter + 50 g flour + 50 g water.

**Days 5–7 — Transition, once every 24 h, then every 12 h.** Switch to the 1:1:1 transition feed: 40 g starter + 28 g bread flour + 12 g wholemeal + 40 g water. When the starter shows a clear rise (≥ +50 %) within 12 h of a feed, move to **two feeds a day, 12 h apart**. Record rise % and time to peak at each feed.

**Days 7–14 — Establishment at 1:2:2, every 12 h.** When the starter reliably rises at least +50 % between feeds, switch to the maintenance feed: 20 g starter + 36 g bread flour + 4 g wholemeal + 40 g water, every 12 h at 24–26 °C. Measure at every feed:
- rise % = (height at peak − height at feed) ÷ height at feed × 100, with both heights measured from the inside bottom of the jar, on the outside of the glass with a ruler. Example: 3.0 cm after feeding, 7.0 cm at peak → (7.0 − 3.0) ÷ 3.0 × 100 = +133 %;
- time to peak (check every 1–2 h in the daytime; a time-lapse photo helps);
- smell at peak and at the next feed;
- pH at peak if you have a meter.

**Readiness test.** The starter is ready when, on **three consecutive 1:2:2 feeds at 24–26 °C**, it rises **≥ +100 %** and peaks in **4–8 h**, with peak times within ~1 h of each other, and smells pleasantly sour. Most starters get there between day 7 and day 14. If you are at day 14 without this, see [starter not rising](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-not-rising).

**Float test: optional and unreliable.** A spoonful of ripe liquid starter often floats in water because it is full of gas. A starter that sinks can still be ready, especially with wholemeal flour. Trust rise and peak time.

**Step 9 — Choose a maintenance regime** ([05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md)).

| You bake | Regime |
|---|---|
| Daily or every 2 days | Room temperature (20–24 °C), 1:2:2 to 1:5:5 every 12 h, or 1:5:5 to 1:10:10 every 24 h |
| Weekly | Feed 1:5:5 (10 g + 50 g + 50 g), 1–2 h at room temperature, then refrigerate at 3–5 °C. Repeat weekly |
| Occasionally | Stiff starter: 10 g starter + 50 g flour + 28 g water (≈ 55 % hydration of the feed flour), 2 h at room temperature, refrigerate. Feed every 1–2 weeks |

**Step 10 — Refresh before baking.** Take the starter out of the fridge 1–2 days before you bake. Feed 1:2:2 at 24–26 °C, then 1:2:2 again after 12 h (or at peak). It is ready to build a levain when it peaks on time again.

**Step 11 — Build the levain for R1-06.** At the peak of a refreshed starter, mix **20 g ripe starter + 65 g bread flour + 65 g water** at a water temperature giving ~26 °C. This is roughly 1:3:3. Mark the jar. It peaks in about **5–7 h at 25–26 °C** or **8–10 h at 21 °C**. Use it at peak or just before: domed, at least doubled, bubbly throughout. Keep the remaining starter and feed it as normal.

**Step 12 — Make a backup.** Once the starter passes the readiness test, spread 20 g very thinly on parchment, let it dry at room temperature for 1–2 days until brittle, crumble it into a sealed jar, and store it cool and dark. To revive: 10 g dried starter + 20 g water, soak 1 h, add 20 g flour, then feed 1:1:1 every 12–24 h at 25 °C until active (3–5 days).

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** A starter is raw flour: do not taste it raw ([FDA-flour]). Wash your hands before handling it. **Discard it entirely if you see mould**: fuzzy growth or pink, orange or black spots or streaks. Wash the jar in hot soapy water and scald it before restarting. Grey or brown liquid (hooch) is not mould. Use loose lids; a sealed jar of active starter can build up pressure.

</div>

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Culture temperature | 24–27 °C during establishment; 24–26 °C for readiness tests | Cold starters establish slowly (2–3 weeks) and give misleading readiness results |
| Feeding on schedule | Every 24 h (days 1–4), then every 12 h | Starving the culture or feeding too early both disrupt establishment |
| Days 3–6 quiet phase | Keep feeding; do not add yeast | The false rise is replaced by the sourdough community during this phase |
| Hydration | 100 % | Changing hydration changes speed and flavour; keep it fixed until the culture is stable |
| Readiness | ≥ +100 %, peak in 4–8 h at 1:2:2, 3 feeds in a row | Baking with an immature starter is the most common cause of dense first sourdough |
| Hygiene | Clean jar edges, loose lid, discard at the first sign of mould | Dried crust on the rim can grow mould |

## Common failures

- No activity after a week → [starter not rising](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-not-rising)
- Strong rise on day 2–3, then nothing → [starter rose strongly on day 2 then stopped](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-rose-strongly-on-day-2-then-stopped)
- Nail-polish smell → [starter smells of acetone or nail polish](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-smells-of-acetone-or-nail-polish)
- Grey liquid on top → [hooch on the starter](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=hooch-on-the-starter)
- Fuzzy or coloured spots → [mould on the starter](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=mould-on-the-starter)
- Starter peaks and collapses in 2–3 h → [starter peaks too fast and collapses](troubleshooting/sourdough.md?id=starter-peaks-too-fast-and-collapses)

## Scientific explanation

Flour carries a small, mixed population of bacteria and yeasts. At first, with a pH near 6 and plenty of nutrients, fast-growing bacteria that are not typical of sourdough, including enterobacteria and *Leuconostoc*-type lactic bacteria, bloom and produce gas and unpleasant odours: the false rise. The acids produced by these and other lactic acid bacteria lower the pH. Below roughly pH 4.5 most of the early bacteria can no longer compete, so activity collapses. Acid-tolerant lactic acid bacteria and yeasts, present all along in small numbers, then take over. Regular feeding selects for organisms that grow fast on flour sugars, especially maltose from amylase activity, and that tolerate low pH. After one to two weeks the community stabilizes: heterofermentative LAB produce lactic and acetic acid and some CO₂, and wild yeasts produce most of the CO₂, plus ethanol and aroma compounds ([05.3](stage-1/module-05/lesson-03.md), [Arora 2021]). Each feed dilutes the acid and supplies fresh sugar; the culture grows, acidifies, peaks when gas production can no longer keep up with gas loss and structural weakening, and then declines as sugar runs out and acid rises.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 21 → 28 °C | Faster peak (roughly half the time), milder, more lactic smell |
| Feeding ratio | 1:2:2 → 1:5:5 | Slower peak (about 1.5–2× the time), milder start, often taller rise |
| Hydration | 100 % → 55 % (stiff) | Slower, tangier (more acetic), keeps longer in the fridge |
| Flour | 10 % → 30 % wholemeal | Faster, more sour, earthier; higher buffering |
| Maintenance | Room temperature → fridge | Slower on revival; needs 2–3 refreshes before baking |
