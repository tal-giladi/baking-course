# R1-20 · Pâte brisée

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

An educational formulation built on established technique: a lean French short dough of flour, butter, a little egg yolk and water, in the proportions used in standard professional practice for quiche and savoury tarts. It is written to make the water–gluten–butter balance of [11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md) visible and measurable.

## Objective

Make a lean short dough whose tenderness comes from controlling water and butter temperature rather than from sugar. You will measure how much water the dough actually needs, keep the dough at or below 18 °C, and line and par-bake a shell that keeps its height.

## Expected result

- **Raw dough:** a smooth, cool, pliable disc with no dry streaks and no visible white butter streaks (or small, flattened flecks if you choose the flakier variant). It rolls to 3 mm without cracking at the edges after a proper rest.
- **Baked shell:** crisp, tender, lightly flaky; breaks cleanly; golden, with the base dry and lightly coloured at the centre. Wall height within 2 mm of the ring height after baking. Neutral, buttery, lightly salted flavour.

## Formula

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Plain / all-purpose flour (9.5–11.5 % protein) or T55 | 100 | 250 |
| Unsalted butter, cold (4–8 °C), 1 cm cubes | 60 | 150 |
| Fine salt | 2 | 5 |
| Egg yolk | 8 | 20 |
| Ice water (1–4 °C) | 20 | 50 |
| **Total** | **190** | **475** |

**Yield:** 475 g dough. Enough for one deep 24 cm × 3 cm quiche ring (rolled to about 34 cm) with some trim to spare, or two 20 cm × 2 cm tart shells (about 230 g each).

**Water:** added water 20 %. Including the water in butter (~16 %, ≈ 24 g) and yolk (~50 %, 10 g), total water is about 84 g, or ~34 % of flour ([11.1](stage-1/module-11/lesson-01.md)). Hold back 10 g of the ice water and add it only if needed.

**Scaling:** ×0.5 = 125 g flour, 75 g butter, 2.5 g salt, 10 g yolk, 25 g water (237.5 g, one 20 cm shell). ×2 = 500 g flour, 300 g butter, 10 g salt, 40 g yolk, 100 g water (950 g).

**Sweet variant:** add 10 g (4 %) caster or icing sugar with the flour for rustic fruit tarts. Total 485 g.

## Equipment

- Scale reading to 1 g; probe thermometer.
- Large bowl, or food processor, or stand mixer with paddle.
- Bench scraper; rolling pin; 3 mm rolling guides if you have them; ruler.
- Tart ring 20–24 cm (2–3 cm high) on a tray with parchment or a perforated mat, or a fluted tart pan with a removable base.
- Parchment and baking weights (ceramic beads, dried beans or rice) for blind baking; pastry brush; fork or docker.

## Mise en place

- Cube the butter and return it to the refrigerator for at least 15 minutes (target 4–8 °C).
- Mix the ice water with a few ice cubes; weigh it out just before use.
- Separate the yolk; mix the yolk and 40 g of the water, keep the other 10 g apart.
- Flour a cool bench lightly. If your kitchen is above 24 °C, chill the flour and the bowl for 15 minutes.

## Procedure

1. **Dry mix.** Combine flour and salt in the bowl.
2. **Rub in the butter.** Add the cold butter cubes. Toss them in the flour, then rub between fingertips (or pulse 6–10 × 1 s in a processor, or paddle on low 1–2 min) until **most of the mixture looks like coarse breadcrumbs with a few flattened butter pieces the size of lentils** (3–5 mm). Work fast; if the butter feels soft or greasy, chill the bowl 10 minutes. **Mixture temperature ≤ 16 °C.**
3. **Add liquid.** Sprinkle the yolk–water mixture over the surface while tossing with a fork or pulsing 3–4 times. Squeeze a handful: **endpoint: it holds together as a clump with only a little crumbling.** If it falls apart, add the reserved water 5 g at a time. Record how much water you used in total.
4. **Bring together.** Tip onto the bench and press into a rough mass with the scraper. Do not knead.
5. **Fraisage.** With the heel of your hand, smear portions of the dough away from you across the bench in one firm stroke each, then gather everything with the scraper. One pass is usually enough. **Endpoint:** uniform colour, no dry flour, no white butter streaks (small flattened flecks are fine). **Dough temperature ≤ 18 °C.**
6. **Shape and rest.** Flatten into a disc 2 cm thick (one disc for a large ring, two of about 235 g for two shells). Wrap tightly. Refrigerate **at least 2 hours, ideally overnight**. Keeps 3 days refrigerated or 3 months frozen.
7. **Temper.** Let the disc stand 5–10 minutes, or beat it a few times with the pin, until the core is about **8–12 °C** and it bends without cracking.
8. **Roll.** On a lightly floured bench, roll from the centre outward, quarter-turning often, to **3 mm** and a diameter of ring + 2 × height + 2–3 cm (for 24 × 3 cm: about 32–34 cm). Brush off excess flour. If the sheet softens, chill it on a tray for 10–15 minutes.
9. **Line.** Line the ring or pan without stretching the dough, pressing a sharp corner at the base ([11.2](stage-1/module-11/lesson-02.md)). Trim flush. Dock the base every 1–2 cm, lightly if it will hold a liquid filling.
10. **Chill the lined shell.** **30 minutes refrigerated or 15–20 minutes frozen.** Preheat the oven to **180 °C (355 °F)** still, 170 °C fan, with a tray on the lower-middle rack.
11. **Blind bake, weighted.** Line with crumpled parchment and fill **to the rim** with weights. Bake **18–20 minutes** until the rim is set and pale gold and the walls look dry when you lift the paper.
12. **Remove weights and finish.** Lift out paper and weights. **Par-bake:** 5–8 minutes more, until the base is dry and matt, very light gold. **Full bake:** 12–15 minutes more, until evenly golden at the centre of the base.
13. **Seal (for liquid fillings).** Brush the inside with beaten egg and return to the oven for **2–3 minutes** until the wash is set and glossy.
14. **Cool** 10 minutes on the tray before filling or unmoulding.

### Application: a basic quiche (optional)

Custards are studied properly in [12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md); this is a working application. For a par-baked, sealed 24 × 3 cm shell: whisk 150 g whole egg (3 eggs) and 20 g yolk with 350 g of milk and cream mixed half and half, 3 g salt and pepper. Scatter 150 g cooked fillings (bacon lardons, sautéed onion, grated Gruyère) in the shell, pour in the custard to 5 mm below the rim, and bake at **160 °C (320 °F)** for **30–40 minutes** until the edge is set and the centre wobbles slightly as a whole; **centre 75–80 °C**. Rest 15 minutes before cutting.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Butter temperature at start | 4–8 °C | Firm butter coats flour without melting and keeps its water locked in |
| Dough temperature after fraisage | ≤ 18 °C | Warmer butter smears, releases water, forms gluten and goes greasy |
| Added water | 18–24 % of flour; record it | Every extra gram adds gluten: tougher, more shrinkage |
| Rest | ≥ 2 h, ideally overnight | Hydration evens out, gluten relaxes, butter firms |
| Rolled thickness | 3 mm, even | Even baking; thick areas stay raw |
| Lined shell before baking | Chilled 30 min or frozen 15–20 min | Butter solid in the oven's first minutes: less shrinkage and slump |
| Blind bake | Weights to the rim; 180 °C; base dry at centre | Supports walls until set; avoids a raw base under a filling |

## Common failures

- **Tough, elastic pastry that shrinks.** Too much water or overworking. See [Tough pastry](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=tough-pastry) and [Shrinking tart shell](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=shrinking-tart-shell).
- **Cracks and breaks when rolled.** Too cold or too dry. See [Cracks when rolling](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=cracks-when-rolling).
- **Soggy base under quiche.** Shell underbaked or unsealed. See [Soggy bottom](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=soggy-bottom).
- **Greasy, butter pooling on the tray.** Butter softened during mixing. See [Greasy pastry, butter leaking](troubleshooting/short-pastry.md?id=greasy-pastry-butter-leaking).

## Scientific explanation

Brisée is held together by water and a small amount of gluten, so it is the short dough in which water control matters most. Rubbing cold butter into the flour first coats many flour particles with fat, shielding their proteins from the water that comes later ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md), [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)). The water then hydrates only the uncoated protein, and because mixing stops as soon as the dough holds together, the gluten that forms stays in short, discontinuous strands. The yolk adds lecithin and fat that help bind the dough with less water, plus colour and richness.

Leaving some butter as small flattened flecks gives slight flakiness: those flecks melt and release steam in the oven, separating thin layers. Rubbing all of it in fine gives a more uniform, more tender, more "sandy" crust that resists soaking better under a quiche custard.

Resting lets the water spread evenly and the gluten relax; chilling the lined shell keeps the butter solid through the first minutes of baking, while the structure begins to set, so the strands that were stretched during rolling cannot recoil and the walls cannot slide ([11.2](stage-1/module-11/lesson-02.md)).

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Added water | 18 → 25 % | Easier rolling, fewer cracks; tougher bite, more shrinkage |
| Butter | 50 → 75 % | More tender and flavourful; softer at the bench; more slump risk |
| Butter piece size | Fine → lentil-sized flecks | Fine: more tender, mealy, better under custards; flecks: flakier |
| Egg yolk | 0 → 10 % (replace some water) | Richer, more tender, better colour; slightly less water needed |
| Flour | Pastry flour → bread flour | Bread flour: tougher, more elastic, shrinks; avoid |
| Rest | 30 min → overnight | Longer: more even, less shrinkage, easier rolling |
| Sugar | 0 → 5 % | Slightly more tender, better colour; still neutral enough for savoury use at 2 % |
