# R1-09 · Brioche

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation built on established technique: a French brioche at 50 % butter, with eggs as the main liquid, a fully developed base dough, butter added gradually at a controlled temperature, and an overnight cold bulk. Brioche formulas range from about 20 % to over 100 % butter; 50 % is the middle of the classic range and a good first high-fat dough. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make one brioche Nanterre loaf and eight brioches à tête from one batch. Develop the base dough to a full windowpane, incorporate 50 % butter without the dough breaking or turning greasy, keep the final dough at or below 24–26 °C, retard overnight, shape cold, proof at no more than 27 °C, and bake to 88–93 °C internal.

## Expected result

- **Loaf (Nanterre):** about 420 g after baking, in a 22 × 11 cm pan, six rounded lobes rising 3–5 cm above the rim.
- **Brioches à tête:** 8, about 50 g each after baking, with a well-centred, upright head on a fluted base.
- Thin, glossy, deep golden-brown crust, uniform in colour.
- Fine, even, pale yellow crumb with small, thin-walled cells. It is feathery and tears into soft strands (a "pulled" crumb), melts in the mouth, and doesn't feel greasy or leave your fingers oily.
- Rich butter and egg flavour, lightly sweet, with no yeasty or sour note.

## Formula

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour (11.5–13 % protein), or French T45 *gruau* | 100.0 | 400 |
| Whole eggs, cold (4–5 large), beaten | 55.0 | 220 |
| Whole milk, cold | 5.0 | 20 |
| Sugar | 12.0 | 48 |
| Fine salt | 2.0 | 8 |
| Instant yeast, osmotolerant (gold) | 1.5 | 6 |
| Unsalted butter (≥82 % fat), at 13–16 °C | 50.0 | 200 |
| **Total** | **225.5** | **902** |
| *Egg wash (not in total)* | | *1 whole egg + 1 yolk + pinch of salt, beaten and strained* |

**Yield:** 902 g of dough → one Nanterre loaf (6 × 75 g = 450 g) and 8 brioches à tête (8 × 55 g = 440 g), with about 10 g lost to the bowl and bench.
**True hydration:** egg 220 × 0.75 + milk 20 × 0.87 + butter 200 × 0.16 = 214.4 g → **54 %**. The dough will be soft and sticky at room temperature and firm when cold.
**Sugar in the water phase:** 48 ÷ (48 + 214) ≈ 18 %, which is why the yeast is osmotolerant at 1.5 %. With standard instant yeast, expect a slower proof; raise it to about 2 % if you are not retarding.
**Scaling:** ×0.5 (451 g) is too small for most stand mixers to develop properly; the hook just spins the dough around. If you must, use it for 8 à tête only. ×1.5 (600 g flour) fits a 5–6 L stand mixer and gives two loaves plus rolls.
**Allergens:** egg, milk (butter), wheat (gluten).

## Equipment

- Stand mixer with dough hook, **strongly recommended**. Brioche needs 20–30 min of intensive mixing. The hand method below works, but it is hard, messy work.
- Digital scale (1 g), probe thermometer (you will take the dough temperature four times).
- Parchment, rolling pin (to plasticize the butter), bench scraper, pastry brush.
- One loaf pan, **22 × 11 cm**, 6–7 cm deep, buttered.
- 8 fluted brioche moulds (about 8 cm top diameter), buttered, or a buttered muffin tin.
- A tray or dish about 25 × 30 cm with cling film, for the overnight dough.
- A proofing spot at 24–27 °C with a thermometer: the oven with only the light on, or the oven off with a tray of warm water on the floor.

## Mise en place

1. **The day before, or at least 3 h before mixing:** nothing to prepare, but plan the timeline: mix (45 min) → bulk at room temperature (1 h) → refrigerate overnight (8–16 h) → shape (30 min) → proof (2–3 h) → bake.
2. **Temperature plan.** The target is a base dough at **≤24 °C** before the butter goes in, and a final dough at **24 °C** (acceptable up to 26 °C). Long, intensive mixing adds a lot of heat: a stand mixer can add 10–15 °C over 25 min. So use **eggs and milk straight from the fridge** (about 4–5 °C). In a kitchen above 24 °C, chill the flour for 30 min too.
3. **Butter.** Take the butter out of the fridge and cut it into slices. Place them between two sheets of parchment and beat them with the rolling pin, then fold and beat again, until the butter is a single pliable slab at **13–16 °C**. At that point it bends without cracking and your finger dents it easily, but it is still cool and not shiny or greasy. Cut it into about 2 cm pieces and keep them on a plate at about 15 °C. If the kitchen is warm, put the plate in the fridge for a few minutes and check again.
4. Weigh the flour, sugar and salt into the mixer bowl. Weigh the yeast separately. Beat the cold eggs with the milk.

## Procedure

**Step 1 — Base dough: combine (t = 0).** Add the yeast to the flour mixture and stir. Add the egg and milk.
Speed 1 for **4 min**, scraping down once. The result is a stiff, sticky, shaggy paste.

**Step 2 — Base dough: develop (t = 4 to about 16 min).** Speed 2 (medium; 4 on a KitchenAid) for **8–12 min**. The dough first sticks to the bowl walls, then begins to pull away from the sides and slap around the hook with a loud slapping sound, forming a ball that cleans the sides (it may still stick at the bottom). **Endpoint:** a piece stretches into a thin membrane you can see light through before it tears (a good windowpane). Brioche must be developed *before* the butter goes in; the butter will not let you catch up afterwards.

**Step 3 — Check temperature.** If the base dough is **above 24 °C**, put the bowl, covered, in the fridge for 10–15 min before continuing. Record it.

**Step 4 — Add the butter (about 8–10 min).** On speed 1–2, add the butter one piece at a time, about a fifth of it at a time (4–5 additions). Wait until each addition has disappeared into the dough before adding the next, typically 1–2 min. The dough will lose its structure, slide and break into pieces, and then come back together. Scrape down the bowl and hook once or twice. If the butter smears onto the sides of the bowl as a greasy film instead of being absorbed, the dough or the butter is too warm: stop and chill the bowl for 10 min.

**Step 5 — Final mix (2–4 min).** Once all the butter is in, mix on speed 2 until the dough is smooth, glossy and elastic, cleans the sides of the bowl again, and stretches into a **very thin, even windowpane**. It should feel soft and slightly tacky, never oily.

**Step 6 — Check the temperature. Target 24 °C, maximum 26 °C.** Above 26 °C the butter starts to soften to the point of melting out of the emulsion, and the dough turns greasy, loses strength and ferments too fast. If it reads 26–27 °C, go straight to Step 7 and shorten the room-temperature bulk to 30 min. If the dough is greasy or broken, see [greasy, broken brioche dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=greasy-broken-brioche-dough).

**Step 7 — Bulk at room temperature (about 1 h at 22–24 °C).** Place in a lightly buttered container, cover, and leave until the dough has risen about **+30–50 %** and looks softly puffed.

**Step 8 — Degas and retard (8–16 h at 3–5 °C).** Turn the dough onto the bench, fold it over itself two or three times to degas it, and press it into a flat rectangle about **2–3 cm thick** on a parchment-lined tray. Wrap it tightly and refrigerate overnight. The flat shape chills quickly and evenly, which stops fermentation from running on in a warm centre. Cold, the butter is firm again, the dough loses its stickiness, and it becomes easy to shape. Fermentation continues slowly, adding flavour.

**Step 9 — Divide (dough at 4–8 °C).** Work quickly on a lightly floured bench, with the dough straight from the fridge.
- Loaf: **6 × 75 g**.
- À tête: **8 × 55 g**.

If the dough softens and sticks while you work, return it to the fridge for 10 min.

**Step 10 — Shape.**
- **Nanterre loaf:** round each 75 g piece into a tight ball, cupping your hand over it and rolling it on the bench. Place the balls seam-down in the buttered pan in two rows of three, touching lightly.
- **Brioche à tête:** round each 55 g piece into a tight ball. Lay the side of your hand on the ball about a quarter of the way along, and roll it back and forth so that it forms a small ball (the head) joined to the larger ball (the body) by a thin neck, about 5 mm across, which must not break through. Hold the head, lower the body into the buttered mould, and press two or three fingertips down around the head, right to the bottom of the mould, so the head sits in a well. *Easier alternative:* divide each 55 g into 45 g and 10 g, round both, make a deep hole in the centre of the larger ball with a floured finger, and sit the teardrop-shaped small ball in the hole, pointed end down.

**Step 11 — First egg wash.** Brush lightly with the egg wash. It keeps the surface moist and gives you a base coat.

**Step 12 — Final proof (about 2–3 h at 24–27 °C, never above 27 °C).** Leave uncovered, or covered loosely with film that doesn't touch the dough, in your proofing spot, with a thermometer. Above about 27–28 °C the butter in the dough begins to soften and seep, the dough weakens and the crumb bakes greasy and coarse. **Done when:** the pieces have at least doubled from shaping (typically 2–2.5×), feel light and jiggle when you move the tin, and a gentle poke springs back very slowly. The loaf balls should reach about **1 cm below the rim** of the pan, and the à tête about the rim of their moulds. Cold dough takes the first hour just to warm up, so don't judge it early.

**Step 13 — Preheat.** À tête: **190 °C (375 °F)**. Loaf: **180 °C (355 °F)**. Conventional, rack in the centre (lower third for the loaf). Preheat at least 20 min.

**Step 14 — Second egg wash.** Very gently: brioche at full proof deflates if you press it. Use a soft brush, work from the top down, and don't let the wash pool at the base of the head or between the balls.

**Step 15 — Bake.**
- **À tête:** **14–18 min** at 190 °C. Done at **88–93 °C** internal (probe through the side of the body), uniform deep golden-brown.
- **Loaf:** **30–35 min** at 180 °C. Check at 15 min and tent with foil when the top reaches deep golden. Done at **88–93 °C** in the centre of a middle ball, with golden sides when tipped out.

**Step 16 — Cool.** Unmould the à tête after 5 min and the loaf after 5–10 min (brioche is fragile hot). Cool on a rack for at least 1 h. Store sealed at room temperature for 2–3 days, or freeze.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Raw egg and raw flour: don't taste the dough, and refrigerate it whenever it is not deliberately fermenting. A stand mixer running for 25 min on a heavy dough can walk across the counter; stay with it, and never reach into the bowl while the hook is turning.

</div>

### Hand method (alternative)

This works, but budget 40–50 min of hard, sticky work, and consider making your first hand batch with 30–35 % butter (120–140 g).

1. Mix the base dough in a large bowl with a scraper until no dry flour remains.
2. **Rubaud** (in the bowl): hold the bowl with one hand, and with the other hand's fingers cupped, scoop under the dough and pull it up and towards you, fast and repeatedly, about 2 strokes per second, for 5 min. Rest 2 min. Then turn it onto the bench and **slap-and-fold** for 12–20 min until you get a good windowpane. Use a scraper, not flour. Check the temperature: chill for 15 min if above 24 °C.
3. Add the butter in three additions. Spread each over the dough, fold it in, and squeeze it through your fingers until it disappears, then slap-and-fold until the dough comes back together, 4–6 min per addition. It will feel hopeless for the first minute each time.
4. Finish with 3–5 min of slap-and-fold to a smooth, glossy, extensible dough. Check the temperature, then continue from Step 7.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Base-dough development | Good windowpane **before** butter | Butter blocks further development; an under-developed base gives a dense, cakey brioche |
| Base-dough temperature | ≤24 °C | Leaves room for the heat of the butter mix |
| Butter temperature and state | 13–16 °C, plastic, added in 4–5 additions | Cold, hard butter won't incorporate; warm butter smears and melts out |
| Final dough temperature | 24 °C, max 26 °C | Above ~26–27 °C the dough turns greasy and weak |
| Cold retard | Flattened 2–3 cm thick, 8–16 h at 3–5 °C | Firms the butter for shaping; even chilling; flavour |
| Shaping temperature | Dough at 4–8 °C | Warm brioche dough is sticky and unshapeable |
| Proof temperature | 24–27 °C, never above 27 °C | Butter softens and leaks above this |
| Proof endpoint | 2–2.5× volume, loaf ~1 cm below the rim | Under: dense, tearing. Over: collapse, coarse crumb |
| Oven | 180 °C loaf, 190 °C small | Sugar and egg crust over-browns at higher temperatures |
| Internal temperature | 88–93 °C | Lower: sinking, gummy. Higher: dry |

## Common failures

- Dough looks oily, curdled or won't come back together → [greasy, broken brioche dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=greasy-broken-brioche-dough)
- Heavy, tight, cake-like crumb → [dense brioche](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=dense-brioche)
- Very slow proof → [slow or no rise in sweet dough](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=slow-or-no-rise-in-sweet-dough)
- Loaf sinks or sides cave in → [loaf collapses or caves in](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=loaf-collapses-or-caves-in)
- Tops dark, centre under-baked → [crust too dark before the inside is done](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=crust-too-dark-before-the-inside-is-done)
- Heads tilted or fallen off, torn sides → [torn or burst sides](troubleshooting/enriched-dough.md?id=torn-or-burst-sides)

## Scientific explanation

Brioche is a gluten network carrying an emulsion. The base dough is a stiff, egg-hydrated dough (about 46 % true hydration before the butter, from the eggs and milk alone: 165 + 17.4 = 182 g of water on 400 g of flour) that is mixed intensively into a strong, extensible network. Egg yolk lecithin and the proteins of the egg then help the network take up an amount of fat equal to half the flour weight. Butter is added at 13–16 °C, where it is *plastic*: a mix of solid fat crystals and liquid oil that can be smeared into thin films and small particles between the gluten strands and around starch granules without melting ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)). Above about 26–27 °C, enough of the butterfat melts that the fat separates out of the dough as free oil. The dough then looks wet, shiny and curdled, loses strength, and bakes greasy. This is why temperature, not time, is the controlling variable of the brioche mix. The overnight retard re-solidifies the butterfat, so the dough behaves like a firm, easily shaped dough, and slow fermentation adds flavour. Fat and 12 % sugar in low water slow the yeast (osmotolerant yeast and a long proof compensate). During baking, egg proteins coagulate and starch gelatinizes around the fat, setting a fine, tender crumb. Butter's milk solids, the egg wash and the residual sugar brown the crust quickly, so the oven stays at 180–190 °C ([06.4](stage-1/module-06/lesson-04.md)).

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Butter | 50 → 20–30 % | Easier to mix and handle, faster rise, taller, more bread-like and chewier; less rich ([X1-17](experiments/X1-17-brioche-butter.md)) |
| Butter | 50 → 70–100 % (rich brioche) | Richer, more cake-like, melts in the mouth; harder mix and tighter temperature control; lower volume unless development is excellent |
| Egg | 55 → 45 % + 10 % milk | Slightly less rich, whiter crumb, a little more volume |
| Sugar | 12 → 8 % | Faster proof, paler crust; better for savoury uses (burger buns) |
| Retard | Overnight → same day (chill 2 h) | Less flavour, stickier shaping |
| Flour | Bread → all-purpose | Weaker base dough, lower volume, less even crumb at 50 % butter |
| Proof temperature | 26 → 30 °C | Faster, but butter seeps; greasy base, coarse crumb |
