# R1-24 · Crème anglaise

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

An educational formulation built on established technique: the classic stirred custard at the standard professional ratio of dairy 100 : yolks 20 : sugar 20, written for this course.

## Objective

Cook an egg-yolk custard to a measured endpoint (82–84 °C) and recognise the nappe stage, so that you can reproduce a smooth, pourable custard sauce on demand and use it later as a base for ice cream, bavarois and chocolate creams.

## Expected result

About 680 g of pale yellow, glossy sauce that coats the back of a spoon; a finger drawn across the coated spoon leaves a clean line that does not close. Chilled, it pours slowly, like single cream thickened, and spreads in a thin even layer on a plate. Smooth on the tongue with no grain, sweet, rich, vanilla-forward, with no sulfurous "cooked egg" note.

## Formula

Percentages are of the total dairy (milk + cream = 100 %).

| Ingredient | % of dairy | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Whole milk (3.5 % fat) | 50 | 250 |
| Cream (35 % fat) | 50 | 250 |
| Egg yolks (5–6 large) | 20 | 100 |
| Caster (superfine) sugar | 20 | 100 |
| Vanilla extract (or 1 vanilla pod, split and scraped) | 1 | 5 |
| Fine salt | 0.1 | 0.5 |
| **Total** | **141.1** | **705.5** |

**Yield:** about 680–690 g after cooking and straining (1–3 % evaporation, a little left in the sieve and pan): 10–12 portions of 60 g as a sauce.

**Scaling:** ×0.5 = 125 g milk, 125 g cream, 50 g yolks, 50 g sugar, 2.5 g vanilla, 0.25 g salt (352.75 g); use a small pan (16 cm) so the depth is enough to read the thermometer. ×2 = 500 g milk, 500 g cream, 200 g yolks, 200 g sugar, 10 g vanilla, 1 g salt (1,411 g); larger batches heat more slowly, so allow more time and stir without pause.

**All-milk version:** 500 g whole milk and no cream gives a lighter sauce that is slightly thinner at the same temperature.

## Equipment

- Scale reading to 1 g (0.1 g for salt); instant-read probe thermometer, calibrated (±0.5–1 °C).
- Heavy-based saucepan, 18–20 cm, stainless steel (thin or aluminium pans create hot spots).
- Heatproof flat-ended spatula (silicone or wood) for stirring; balloon whisk.
- Mixing bowl for yolks (1.5 L); ladle.
- Fine sieve (chinois or fine mesh).
- Ice bath: a large bowl with ice and a little water, and a stainless bowl (2 L) that sits in it.
- Food film; container with lid.

## Mise en place

- Set up the ice bath and the clean, dry bowl in it, with the sieve resting on top, **before** you start cooking.
- Separate the eggs. Weigh yolks to 100 g (large yolks are 17–19 g each). Keep whites for meringue ([R1-29](recipes/R1-29-meringues.md)) or financiers; they keep 2–3 days refrigerated.
- Weigh the sugar and split it: **25 g** for the dairy, **75 g** for the yolks.
- If using a vanilla pod, split it lengthwise and scrape the seeds into the milk; add the pod too.

## Procedure

1. **Heat the dairy.** Put the milk, cream, 25 g sugar, salt and vanilla pod (if using) in the saucepan. Heat over medium heat to **80–90 °C**: steam rises and small bubbles form at the edge; do not let it boil over. If infusing a pod, cover, take off the heat and infuse **15–30 min**, then reheat to 80–90 °C and remove the pod.
2. **Mix the yolks.** In the bowl, whisk the yolks with the remaining 75 g sugar for about **30 s**, just until smooth and slightly paler. Do not beat to a thick foam; foam hides the nappe stage and leaves bubbles on the sauce.
3. **Temper.** While whisking the yolks, pour in about **one third (~170 g)** of the hot dairy in a thin stream. The yolk mixture should now be warm, about **45–55 °C**.
4. **Combine.** Pour the tempered yolks back into the saucepan with the rest of the dairy, stirring with the spatula. The mixture should read about **60–68 °C**.
5. **Cook, stirring.** Over **medium-low** heat, stir continuously in a figure-eight, scraping the whole base and the corners of the pan. Hold the thermometer in the middle of the liquid, not touching the base. Near the end, the temperature should rise no faster than about **2–3 °C per 10 s**; if it climbs faster, lift the pan off the heat for a few seconds while you keep stirring. Typical time: **3–6 min**.
6. **Watch the signs.** At about 75 °C the surface foam begins to disappear. At 80–82 °C the sauce looks glossier and slightly heavier and begins to coat the spatula.
7. **Endpoint: 82–84 °C.** Do the nappe test: dip the spatula, hold it horizontal, draw a finger across the back. The line stays clean and sharp. Do not go above **85 °C**.
8. **Strain and stop the cooking immediately.** Pour through the fine sieve into the bowl sitting in the ice bath. The saucepan holds heat; left in it, the custard rises another 1–3 °C.
9. **Cool fast.** Stir the custard in the ice bath until it is below **10 °C** (about **15–20 min**; add ice as it melts). Taste for sweetness and vanilla. If using extract instead of a pod, you can add it here rather than in step 1 for a fresher flavour.
10. **Store.** Press film onto the surface, cover, label with date and time, and refrigerate at **≤ 5 °C**. Use within **2–3 days**. Stir before use; it thickens slightly as it chills.

**Rescue.** At the first sign of graininess (step 7 or 8), pour immediately into the cold bowl and blend with an immersion blender for 20–30 s. A slightly overcooked anglaise becomes smooth again; a curdled one with visible curds in watery liquid does not.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Yolk mixing | Just combined, not foamed | Foam hides the nappe stage and leaves bubbles |
| Tempering | ~1/3 of hot dairy into yolks, whisking | Prevents scrambling where hot liquid meets concentrated yolk |
| Heating rate near the end | ≤ 2–3 °C per 10 s; medium-low heat | The bottom layer must not overshoot while the centre reads low |
| Stirring | Continuous, scraping base and corners | Hot spots on the base curdle first |
| Endpoint | 82–84 °C, nappe line holds; never above 85 °C | Below 80 °C thin; above 85 °C grainy and eggy |
| Stop cooking | Strain immediately into a bowl in ice | Carryover heat in the pan can add 1–3 °C |
| Cooling | Below 10 °C within ~20 min; ≤ 5 °C storage; 2–3 days | Food safety: custard is an ideal bacterial medium |

## Common failures

- **Grainy or curdled.** See [Curdled or grainy anglaise](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=curdled-or-grainy-anglaise).
- **Too thin.** See [Thin anglaise](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=thin-anglaise).
- **Skin on the surface after chilling.** See [Skin on custard or pastry cream](troubleshooting/custards-creams.md?id=skin-on-custard-or-pastry-cream).
- **Bubbly surface.** Yolks beaten to a foam, or stirred with a whisk during cooking. Skim, and use a spatula next time.

## Scientific explanation

The yolk proteins, diluted in five times their weight of dairy and sugar, are too far apart to bond at the 65–70 °C where a yolk alone sets. As the temperature climbs through the high 70s, enough proteins unfold and meet to form a fine, loose network that thickens the liquid without trapping it into a gel: a stirred custard. Sugar stabilizes the folded proteins and binds water, pushing thickening higher and softening it; cream fat interrupts the network, which makes the sauce silky ([12.1](stage-1/module-12/lesson-01.md), [SC-03](stage-1/science/sc-03-proteins.md)).

Above about 85 °C the network keeps tightening. Protein clusters grow large enough to feel and they squeeze water out: the sauce becomes grainy, then separates into curds and thin liquid. Because the stirring breaks any continuous structure, the custard stays pourable at the nappe stage instead of setting like a baked custard. The yolk lecithin and lipoproteins also help keep the cream fat emulsified ([SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)).

At 82–84 °C the custard is also well above the 71 °C recommended for egg dishes ([FDA-eggs]).

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Yolks | 16 → 30 % of dairy | Thicker, richer, more eggy; thickens at a lower temperature, less margin before curdling |
| Sugar | 15 → 25 % of dairy | Sweeter; thickens a few degrees higher; more forgiving |
| Cream share | 0 → 100 % of dairy | Richer and thicker mouthfeel; 100 % cream approaches an ice-cream base |
| Endpoint | 78 → 86 °C | Thin → nappe → grainy ([X1-33](experiments/X1-33-custard-endpoint.md)) |
| Infusion | Vanilla, coffee beans, tea, citrus zest, herbs; 15–30 min at 80–90 °C | Flavour; strain before adding the yolks; tannic infusions (tea) can slightly lower the thickening temperature |
| Chocolate | Add chopped chocolate to the hot strained anglaise | Thicker, sets when cold: the start of a crémeux (Stage 2) |
