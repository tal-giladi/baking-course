# R1-03 · Pita and flatbreads

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

An educational formulation built on established technique: a lean, moderately hydrated dough baked on a very hot surface so that steam generated inside separates it into a pocket. Proportions are in the standard range for pita in professional and home practice.

## Objective

Use a thin, lean dough to see heat transfer and steam leavening directly. You will control the one thing that decides whether a pita puffs: how fast the outer surfaces set compared with how fast the interior water turns to steam. The skillet variant shows the same dough cooked by conduction only.

## Expected result

- **Pita:** 8 rounds about 17 cm across, fully puffed into a balloon within 60–120 s of loading, deflating to a flat bread with a continuous pocket. Pale to light golden with a few brown spots, soft and flexible after resting in a towel. Top and bottom layers of similar thickness (the bottom slightly thicker is normal).
- **Skillet flatbread:** 8 thinner, softer, blistered flatbreads with brown freckles, flexible enough to fold without cracking.

## Formula

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour or strong all-purpose (11–12.5 % protein) | 100 | 500 |
| Water (at the temperature from step 1) | 62 | 310 |
| Fine salt | 2 | 10 |
| Instant dry yeast | 1 | 5 |
| Olive oil (optional; see note) | 2 | 10 |
| **Total** | **167** | **835** |

**Yield:** 835 g dough → 8 pieces of 104 g (about 1 g spare). Hydration 62 % (water ÷ flour). Oil is not counted as water.

**Scaling:** ×0.5 = 250 g flour, 155 g water, 5 g salt, 2.5 g yeast, 5 g oil (417.5 g, 4 pita). ×2 = 1,000 g flour, 620 g water, 20 g salt, 10 g yeast, 20 g oil (1,670 g, 16 pita).

**Note on oil:** 2–3 % oil gives a softer, more pliable bread that stays soft longer; without oil the pita is chewier and slightly crisper. It does not change puffing much at this level.

## Equipment

- Scale reading to 1 g (0.1 g for yeast at half batch); probe thermometer; infrared thermometer if available.
- Stand mixer with dough hook (optional; hand mixing is easy at this hydration).
- Bowl or 2 L container with lid, marked for volume.
- Rolling pin; bench scraper; ruler.
- Baking stone (15–20 mm) or steel (6–10 mm), or the upturned heavy tray method if you have neither.
- Peel, or a flat tray without rims, dusted with flour.
- Heavy skillet, cast iron or carbon steel, 24–28 cm (for the skillet variant or as an alternative bake).
- Clean kitchen towel, and a bowl or container to stack baked pita in.

## Mise en place

- Place the stone or steel on a rack in the **upper-middle third** of the oven (pita benefits from roof radiation) and preheat at the oven's maximum, typically 250–290 °C (480–550 °F), for **45–60 minutes**. Start this during bulk fermentation.
- Calculate water temperature for a desired dough temperature (DDT) of **25 °C** ([01.3](stage-1/module-01/lesson-03.md)).
- Weigh all ingredients. Flour the bench lightly; have a tray with a little flour for the rolled rounds.

## Procedure

1. **Water temperature.** Water temperature = (DDT × 3) − flour temperature − room temperature − friction factor. Example: (25 × 3) − 21 − 22 − 6 (hand mixing, small batch) = 26 °C. With a stand mixer use your measured friction factor.
2. **Mix.** Combine flour, yeast and salt, add water and oil. Hand: mix to a shaggy mass (2 min), rest covered 10 min, then knead 6–8 min until smooth. Mixer: 2 min low, then 4–5 min medium. **Endpoint:** smooth, slightly tacky, not sticky; a thin windowpane that tears with a jagged edge is enough (moderate development). **Dough temperature 24–26 °C.**
3. **Bulk fermentation.** Cover and ferment at 24–26 °C for **60–75 minutes**, until about **1.5×** the original volume. A floured finger pressed 1 cm in leaves a dent that springs back slowly. (For flavour, you can instead refrigerate the covered dough at 3–5 °C for 12–24 h after 30 min at room temperature; let it warm 30–45 min before dividing.)
4. **Divide and pre-shape.** Divide into **8 × 104 g**. Round each into a tight ball with a smooth, unbroken skin. Cover with film or an upturned bowl.
5. **Bench rest.** **20–30 minutes**, covered, until the balls relax and roll easily without springing back. The surface must not dry: a dry skin makes cracks that leak steam.
6. **Roll.** Flatten one ball with your palm, then roll from the centre outward, turning a quarter turn between strokes, to a round about **17 cm across and 4–5 mm thick**. Use only enough flour to prevent sticking. Keep the thickness even, especially at the edges. Do not fold, crease or pierce the dough. Place on a floured tray, **covered**, and roll the rest.
7. **Short rest.** Let the rolled rounds rest covered **5–10 minutes** while you roll the others (they puff slightly). Bake in the order you rolled them.
8. **Check the oven.** Stone or steel surface at **≥ 250 °C** by IR thermometer. If your oven maxes out lower, allow a full 60 min preheat.
9. **Bake.** Slide 1–3 rounds onto the stone with the peel (flip them over as you load so the side that was up during resting goes down). Close the door. **Endpoint:** the round balloons fully within about **60–120 s** on a steel or **90–150 s** on a stone. Bake another **30–60 s** after full puff until set and pale with a few light golden patches: total about **2–3.5 minutes**. Do not wait for all-over colour; that makes a dry, cracker-like pita.
10. **Soften.** Transfer immediately to a bowl lined with a towel and cover with the towel. The trapped steam softens the crust. Stack the rest on top as they come out. Rest at least 5 minutes before serving.
11. **Store.** Once fully cool (30 min), bag in plastic (for a soft pita the crust should soften). Eat within 1 day or freeze ([04.3](stage-1/module-04/lesson-03.md)).

### Alternative bake: dry skillet

12. Heat a heavy skillet over medium-high heat for 5 minutes until the surface is about **230–260 °C** (IR thermometer), or until a sprinkle of flour browns in about 10 s. No oil.
13. Lay in a rolled round. **30–45 s:** bubbles appear on top. Flip. **60–90 s:** brown spots underneath; the round starts to puff in patches. Flip again and press gently on a puffing area with a folded towel to push steam into the flat areas; it often inflates fully. Cook **30 s** more. Wrap in the towel as in step 10.

### Variant: soft skillet flatbread (no pocket)

14. Use the same dough, with oil increased to **3 %** (15 g) if you like a softer result. Divide into 8 × 104 g as above.
15. Roll thinner, to about **22 cm and 2.5–3 mm**, or stretch by hand. Pocket formation is not the goal: a thin round sets through before a continuous steam layer forms, giving scattered blisters instead.
16. Cook in the dry skillet at **220–240 °C**: **45–60 s** first side until large blisters form and the underside has brown freckles; flip; **30–45 s**. Brush with melted butter or oil if you want (optional) and stack in a towel. **Endpoint:** flexible, no raw grey patches when torn, brown freckles on both sides.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Surface temperature at loading | Stone/steel ≥ 250 °C; skillet 230–260 °C | The surfaces must set in seconds and the interior water flash to steam before the gas escapes |
| Preheat time | 45–60 min for stone/steel | Air reaches set point long before the stone does |
| Rolled thickness | 4–5 mm, even | Too thin sets through before steam separates the layers; too thick heats too slowly and puffs partially |
| Surface condition | Moist, unbroken, not over-floured | Cracks, holes or a dry skin leak steam; a crease welds the layers together |
| Bake time | 2–3.5 min in oven | Beyond the puff, extra time only dries it |
| Post-bake | Into a towel immediately | Trapped steam softens the crust; left out, pita turns crisp and brittle |

## Common failures

- **Does not puff, or puffs only partly.** See [Pita does not puff](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=pita-does-not-puff).
- **Hard, dry, cracker-like.** Overbaked or left uncovered to cool. Shorten the bake; wrap immediately.
- **Pale, underbaked patches and a gummy layer inside.** Too thick, or pulled too early. See [Gummy crumb](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=gummy-crumb).
- **Burnt spots on the base, pale top.** Steel too close to the bottom element or on the lowest rack: move it up. See [Uneven browning](troubleshooting/bread-baking.md?id=uneven-browning).

## Scientific explanation

A pita puffs because it is a thin slab heated extremely fast from both faces. In the first 20–40 seconds the bottom (conduction from the stone) and the top (radiation from the roof and walls) heat above 70–90 °C, setting a thin skin of gelatinized starch and firmed gluten on each face ([04.2](stage-1/module-04/lesson-02.md)). The interior, a few millimetres away, is still soft. As it approaches 100 °C, the water in it turns to steam. One gram of water becomes well over a litre of steam at 100 °C, so even a small fraction of the dough's water produces a large volume of gas. The two set skins are stronger than the soft middle, so the steam pushes them apart along the middle plane, and the round inflates like a balloon ([SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)). Fermentation gas helps a little, but steam does most of the work; you could puff an unleavened dough the same way, as many flatbread traditions do.

The failures follow from the race between setting and steam:

- **Too thin (below ~3 mm):** the heat sets the whole thickness before enough steam builds, so there is no soft middle to split. You get blisters.
- **Too thick (above ~6 mm):** the interior heats slowly, the skins become thick, and the steam that forms is not enough to lift them; you get a partial pocket or a bread with a dense middle.
- **Not hot enough:** the surfaces set slowly, the interior warms gradually, and steam leaks out through the soft dough instead of accumulating.
- **Holes, creases or a dried skin:** steam escapes through the weak point, or the layers are welded together at the crease.

The skillet version relies only on conduction from one side at a time, which is why flipping and pressing help: they heat the second face and push steam into the areas that have not separated.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hydration | 58 % → 66 % | Wetter dough puffs more readily and stays softer, but is harder to roll evenly |
| Rolled thickness | 3 → 6 mm | Thinner: faster, crisper, less reliable pocket; thicker: softer, breadier, partial pocket |
| Surface temperature | 220 → 290 °C | Hotter: faster puff, paler, softer; cooler: slow or no puff, drier |
| Oil | 0 → 5 % | More: softer, more tender, slightly slower puff |
| Whole-wheat flour | 0 → 50 % of flour | Heavier, more flavour; add ~3–5 % water; puff slightly less reliable |
| Rest after rolling | 0 → 20 min | Longer: more fermentation gas, slightly softer; too long uncovered dries the skin |

## Experiment suggestions

- **Thickness series:** roll three rounds from one batch to 3, 4.5 and 6 mm and bake together. Record puff time and pocket quality.
- **Surface series:** bake one round on a steel, one on a stone, one on a preheated upturned tray and one in the skillet. Record time to full puff. Relate to thermal effusivity from [04.1](stage-1/module-04/lesson-01.md).
- For steam in a loaf rather than a flatbread, run [X1-10](experiments/X1-10-steam.md).
