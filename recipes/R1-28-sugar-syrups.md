# R1-28 · Sugar syrups: simple syrup to hard crack

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational set of four syrups built on established technique: a 1:1 simple syrup, a 40 °Brix flavoured soaking syrup, a 118 °C syrup of the kind used for Italian meringue and buttercream, and a hard-crack sugar made into glass shards and spun sugar. The ratios are standard professional practice; the batch sizes are chosen for a home kitchen and for learning. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make four syrups that span the concentration range from 40 % to 99 % sugar, hitting each endpoint by measurement: weight for the uncooked syrups, a calibrated thermometer (confirmed by the cold-water test) for the cooked ones. Keep the cooked syrups clear and crystal-free, and work with hot sugar safely.

## Expected result

- **A. Simple syrup:** clear, colourless, 50 °Brix (51–52 °Brix after a short boil), pourable when cold.
- **B. Soaking syrup:** clear, 40 °Brix before flavouring, noticeably thinner than A.
- **C. 118 °C syrup:** clear, thick, forms a pliable firm ball in iced water; about 230 g (without glucose) from 200 g sugar.
- **D. Hard-crack glass:** clear, colourless or pale straw, brittle; snaps cleanly and shatters into glass-like shards; spun sugar forms fine, dry, glossy threads.

## Formula

The base for the percentages is the sugar (sugar = 100 %).

**A. Simple syrup (1:1)**

| Ingredient | % of sugar | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Granulated sugar | 100 | 200 |
| Water | 100 | 200 |
| **Total** | **200** | **400** |

Yield: about 390 g (a little water boils off). 50 °Brix as mixed.

**B. Soaking syrup (40 °Brix, flavoured)**

| Ingredient | % of sugar | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Granulated sugar | 100 | 100 |
| Water | 150 | 150 |
| Vanilla pod, split and scraped (or 3 g vanilla extract after cooling) | — | ½ pod |
| Optional: rum, kirsch or orange liqueur, after cooling | 15 | 15 |
| **Total (without vanilla)** | **265** | **265** |

Syrup before liqueur: 100 ÷ 250 = **40 °Brix**. With 15 g liqueur it is about 38 % sugar by weight. Yield: enough to soak one 20 cm génoise split into three layers (use about 25 % of the sponge's weight as a starting point).

**C. 118 °C syrup (firm ball)**

| Ingredient | % of sugar | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Granulated sugar | 100 | 200 |
| Water | 30 | 60 |
| Optional: glucose syrup (DE 38–42, ~80 % solids) | 10 | 20 |
| **Total** | **140** | **280** |

Yield: 200 ÷ 0.87 ≈ **230 g** without glucose; about 248 g with it (200 g sugar + 16 g glucose solids = 216 g, ÷ 0.87). For the Italian meringue in [R1-29](recipes/R1-29-meringues.md), cook 180 g of the sugar with the water and whip the other 20 g into the whites.

**D. Hard-crack sugar (glass shards and spun sugar)**

| Ingredient | % of sugar | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Granulated sugar | 100 | 200 |
| Water | 35 | 70 |
| Glucose syrup (DE 38–42) | 20 | 40 |
| **Total** | **155** | **310** |

Yield: about 235 g (200 g sugar + 32 g glucose solids = 232 g solids, at ~99 %). Enough for one 30 × 40 cm silicone mat of thin glass, or a few nests of spun sugar. Do not add acid or lemon juice to D: it inverts sugar, and the fructose yellows the glass and makes it sticky.

**Scaling:** A and B scale freely. C: ×0.5 (100 g sugar) is too shallow for most probes; use a 12–14 cm pan and tilt it. ×2 needs an 18–20 cm heavy pan. D: do not scale up on your first attempt; 200 g of sugar is plenty to handle safely.
**Allergens:** none (liqueur contains alcohol).

## Equipment

- Digital scale (1 g).
- Digital probe thermometer, calibrated (see Mise en place). A clip-on candy thermometer is fine for C and D if it can be immersed at least 2–3 cm.
- Heavy stainless-steel saucepans: 16–18 cm for A–C, 16 cm for D. Light-coloured interior so you can see colour. No non-stick for D (coatings are not rated for 150 °C+ in all cases).
- Pastry brush (natural or silicone) and a cup of water for washing down.
- A bowl of iced water (cold-water test and first aid) and a second, larger bowl of cold water for stopping D.
- Silicone baking mat or an oiled marble slab/baking tray; a heat-resistant spatula.
- For spun sugar: a whisk with the ends of the loops cut off (or two forks back to back), two wooden spoons, parchment on the floor and counter.
- Refractometer (optional, 0–90 °Brix or 28–62 °Brix) for A and B.
- Long sleeves, closed shoes. No children or pets in the kitchen for C and D.

## Mise en place

1. **Calibrate the thermometer.** Bring water to a full boil in the pan you will use for C and D. Hold the probe in the centre, 2–3 cm deep, not touching the pan. Record the reading. **Your correction = 100 − reading.** Subtract it from every target: if water reads 99 °C, cook C to 117 °C and D to 149–154 °C.
2. **Altitude check.** If you are above about 300 m, your water reading already includes the altitude effect (roughly −1 °C per 300 m). Use the correction from step 1 and nothing else.
3. Set up the station for C and D before you light the burner: iced water, cup of water with brush, mat on a heatproof surface, spatula, oven glove, and the pan's handle turned inwards.
4. Weigh everything. Wipe the pan and utensils clean and dry; any sugar grains or dust on them are seeds for crystallization.

## Procedure

### A. Simple syrup

1. Combine sugar and water in the pan. Heat on medium, stirring, until the sugar has **fully dissolved**: the syrup is clear and no grains grate under the spatula (about 3–4 min).
2. Bring just to a boil (about 101–102 °C). Remove from heat.
3. Cool, covered, to room temperature (a lid prevents evaporation). Measure with a refractometer: expect **50–52 °Brix**. Record.
4. Store in a clean jar in the refrigerator for up to 1 month.

### B. Soaking syrup

1. Combine water, sugar and the scraped vanilla pod and seeds. Heat, stirring, until the sugar dissolves, then bring to a boil and simmer 1 minute.
2. Cover and cool to room temperature (about 1 h; faster over iced water). Remove the pod.
3. Measure °Brix *before* adding alcohol: expect **40–41 °Brix**. Then add the liqueur if using.
4. Use at room temperature for soaking sponges (brush evenly, working from the edges in). Keeps 1 week refrigerated.

### C. 118 °C syrup

1. Put the water in the pan first, then the sugar (and glucose) on top, so the sugar is evenly wet. Stir gently with a clean spatula over medium heat until the syrup is clear and fully dissolved. Do not let it boil before this.
2. **Stop stirring.** Wash down the inside wall of the pan with the wet brush, just above the syrup line, so no crystals remain. Alternatively, cover the pan with a lid for 1–2 minutes once it boils.
3. Insert the thermometer. Boil on medium-high heat. **Record the temperature every minute** from the boil. The rise from 102 to 110 °C is slow; above 112 °C it accelerates to 2–4 °C per minute.
4. At **110 °C, 114 °C and 118 °C**, do the cold-water test: drop a little syrup from a clean spoon into the iced water, wait 5–10 s, gather it underwater and lift out. Expect: 110 °C thread, 114 °C soft ball that flattens, 118 °C firm ball that holds its shape but is pliable.
5. Remove from heat at **116–117 °C** (the pan carries over 1–2 °C). Confirm the final reading: **118 °C** (± 1 °C, corrected).
6. Use immediately for Italian meringue ([R1-29](recipes/R1-29-meringues.md)). If you are making it only for practice: tare the empty pan before step 1 and weigh pan and syrup now. Expect about 230 g of syrup. Then let the pan stand undisturbed on a rack until cool; it should stay clear. Wash the pan with hot water.

### D. Hard-crack sugar: glass shards and spun sugar

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety, read before starting.** Sugar at 150 °C is hotter than frying oil, sticks to skin and keeps burning. Clear the kitchen of children and pets. Wear long sleeves and closed shoes. Keep a bowl of iced water right beside you: if sugar touches your skin, plunge the area in immediately, then hold it under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not peel set sugar off skin. Seek medical help for any burn larger than a coin, on the face or hands, or that blisters. Never touch or taste the sugar until it is fully cold. Never put the hot pan in water in a sink (it spits steam and sugar); stop the cooking by dipping only the base of the pan in the cold-water bowl, briefly. Work on a heatproof surface. Do not leave the pan for any reason once it is above 130 °C.

</div>

1. Dissolve the sugar, water and glucose as in C, steps 1–2. Wash down the walls carefully: at this concentration any crystal will spread through the batch.
2. Boil on medium-high heat without stirring. Past 140 °C watch closely.
3. At **150–154 °C** (hard crack; corrected), take the pan off the heat and **dip the base in the cold-water bowl for 3–5 seconds** to stop the temperature rising. Let the bubbles subside for 20–30 seconds.
4. Confirm with a cold-water test: a drop sets instantly into a brittle thread that snaps.

**Glass shards**

5. Pour about two-thirds of the sugar onto the silicone mat in a thin, even layer, tilting the mat, or spread it with the back of a spoon to 2–3 mm.
6. Leave to cool completely, about 15–20 minutes. Break it into shards by hand. The shards are sharp: handle by the edges and do not serve pieces thinner than about 2 mm to children.

**Spun sugar**

7. Rest two wooden spoons over the edge of the counter, 30 cm apart, with their handles projecting, and cover the floor below with parchment.
8. Let the remaining sugar cool in the pan for 1–3 minutes, until a fork lifted out trails long, thin threads rather than drips.
9. Dip the cut whisk in the sugar, lift it and let the first heavy drips fall back. Then wave it back and forth over the spoon handles in a fast, loose motion. Fine threads fall across the handles.
10. Gather the threads gently with dry hands and shape into a nest or ball. Reheat the pan briefly on low if the sugar thickens too much to spin.
11. Use spun sugar within about 1 hour (less in humid weather). Store shards airtight with a desiccant for up to a few days.

**Cleaning:** soak pans and whisk in hot water; the sugar dissolves by itself. Never scrape set sugar with a knife.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thermometer calibration | Correction recorded before cooking | A 2 °C error changes the texture of C and the colour of D |
| Sugar fully dissolved before the boil | No grains under the spatula | Undissolved crystals seed crystallization |
| No stirring after the boil; walls washed | Clean walls above the syrup line | Wall crystals fall back and seed the batch |
| Probe immersion | ≥2–3 cm in syrup, not foam; tilt small batches | Air and foam read low |
| Endpoint C | 118 °C ± 1 (corrected); firm ball | Concentration ~87 %; sets Italian meringue texture |
| Endpoint D | 150–154 °C (corrected); brittle thread | Below ~146 °C sticky; above ~155 °C colour appears |
| Carry-over | Pull C 1–2 °C early; dip D's base in cold water | Heavy pan keeps heating |
| Brix check A, B | 50 and 40 °Brix at room temperature, before alcohol | Consistent sweetness and soaking |
| Burn prevention | Iced water beside you, sleeves, no distractions | Hot sugar burns are severe |

## Common failures

- Syrup turns cloudy and sandy while boiling → [syrup crystallized in the pan](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=syrup-crystallized-in-the-pan)
- Thermometer reading does not match the cold-water test, or meringue is too soft or too firm → [syrup temperature wrong or unreliable](troubleshooting/meringues-buttercreams.md?id=syrup-temperature-wrong-or-unreliable)
- Sugar glass yellow, sticky or cloudy → see [caramel and sugar faults](troubleshooting/cookies.md) and the notes under Scientific explanation below

## Scientific explanation

Dissolved sucrose lowers the vapour pressure of water, so a syrup boils above 100 °C; as water evaporates the concentration rises and so does the boiling point, and at a given pressure each boiling temperature corresponds to one concentration ([13.1](stage-1/module-13/lesson-01.md), [SC-11](stage-1/science/sc-11-phase-changes-gases.md)). The cooked syrups end supersaturated: at 20 °C water holds only about 67 % sucrose, and C is about 87 %, D about 99 %. They stay clear only if nothing starts nucleation, which is why the sugar must be fully dissolved, the walls washed and the syrup left unstirred after the boil ([SC-13](stage-1/science/sc-13-crystallization.md)). Glucose syrup's mix of glucose, maltose and longer chains interferes with sucrose crystals and raises viscosity, making C forgiving and D workable. At hard crack there is too little water and too little time for crystals as the syrup cools: it solidifies as an amorphous glass. A glass absorbs water from humid air, which plasticizes its surface (sticky) and eventually lets it crystallize (cloudy). Acid or invert sugar adds fructose, which browns at lower temperatures and is more hygroscopic, hence the yellow, sticky glass.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Endpoint of C | 118 → 121 °C | Firmer, less water; firmer Italian meringue that holds sharper piping |
| Endpoint of C | 118 → 114 °C | Softer syrup; softer meringue; soft-ball uses (fondant) |
| Glucose in C | 0 → 10 % | More tolerant of agitation; slightly softer, chewier set; crystallization much less likely |
| Glucose in D | 20 → 0 % | Crystallization risk rises sharply; more brittle, faster setting |
| Acid in D | None → cream of tartar | Glass yellows sooner and goes sticky faster (not recommended) |
| Water at start | 30 → 60 % of sugar | Longer cook, same final concentration, more wall splashing |
| Soaking syrup | 40 → 30 °Brix | Less sweet, wetter sponge per gram of sugar; shorter keeping |
