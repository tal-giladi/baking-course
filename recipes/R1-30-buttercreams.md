# R1-30 · Swiss and Italian meringue buttercreams, crème mousseline

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation built on established technique: Swiss and Italian meringue buttercreams, crème mousseline by the two-stage butter method, and short reference versions of French (pâte à bombe) and American buttercream for comparison. Ratios and temperature windows are standard professional practice. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make Swiss meringue buttercream (SMBC), Italian meringue buttercream (IMBC) and crème mousseline, controlling temperature at every step: meringue at or below 25 °C before the butter, butter at 18–20 °C, finished cream at 19–22 °C. Recognize the normal curdled stage during mixing, rescue a deliberately broken batch in both directions, and finish a cream smooth enough to coat a cake.

## Expected result

- **SMBC:** smooth, silky, ivory to white, light but not airy; holds a crisp edge from a star tip; tastes of butter and vanilla, moderately sweet; melts cleanly in the mouth, no grit or greasiness.
- **IMBC:** as SMBC, slightly whiter, lighter and firmer, holding sharper piped ridges.
- **Crème mousseline:** pale yellow, glossy, creamy, thicker than pastry cream; holds a piped rosette at room temperature and sets firm enough to slice in a cake when chilled; rich custard and butter flavour.
- **French and American (reference):** French is yellow, rich and soft; American is dense, very sweet and forms a dry crust within an hour.

**Scaling:** all versions scale ×0.5 with a hand mixer and ×2 in a 5 L stand mixer. For IMBC below about 120 g sugar in the syrup, use a 12–14 cm pan so the probe is covered.
**Allergens:** egg, milk (butter, cream, pastry cream). Crème pâtissière also contains wheat or corn starch; check your version.

## Equipment

- Stand mixer with whisk and paddle (strongly recommended; buttercream takes 20–30 min of mixing). A hand mixer works for half batches.
- Probe thermometer, calibrated ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)).
- Heatproof bowl and pan for the bain-marie (SMBC); small heavy saucepan with a lip (IMBC, French).
- Scale (1 g); spatula; a kitchen towel and a bowl of warm water (or a hair dryer, or a kitchen torch) for rescuing a curdled cream.
- Cling film; a shallow tray for cooling pastry cream quickly.

## Mise en place

1. **Butter to 18–20 °C.** Cut it into 20 g pieces and leave it on a plate for about 45–60 min in a 20–22 °C kitchen. Check with the probe pushed into the centre of a piece. It should dent easily with a finger but not look shiny or greasy.
2. Whites at room temperature; weigh everything.
3. Calibrate the thermometer if you have not done so today.
4. For mousseline: have the pastry cream ingredients ready, the first 125 g butter cut cold, and a shallow tray lined with cling film.

## Procedure

### Swiss meringue buttercream

1. **Swiss meringue:** whisk the whites and sugar in the heatproof bowl over barely simmering water, constantly, to **70 °C** (71 °C for pasteurization), until no grains remain when rubbed between the fingers (5–7 min).
2. Whip at high speed until stiff and glossy, then keep whipping on medium-high until the meringue reads **≤25 °C** (ideally 22–25 °C), about **10–15 min**. Probe it. If it is still warm after 15 min, hold a bag of ice or a cold wet towel against the outside of the bowl while whipping.
3. **Add the butter** on medium speed, one 20 g piece every 10–15 seconds. The meringue deflates, thickens and then, often around halfway, looks curdled or soupy. **Keep going.**
4. When all the butter is in, switch to the paddle (or continue with the whisk) and beat 2–3 min until it is smooth, thick and satin-finished. Add the salt and vanilla.
5. **Check the temperature: 19–22 °C.** If it is curdled or soupy, go to *Rescue*, below.
6. **Smooth it:** beat on the lowest speed with the paddle for **5–10 min**, or stir hard by hand with a spatula, to knock out large bubbles before coating a cake.

### Italian meringue buttercream

1. Make an **Italian meringue** ([R1-29](recipes/R1-29-meringues.md)): syrup of 160 g sugar and 50 g water to **118–121 °C**; whites with 20 g sugar whipped to firm peak, started when the syrup reaches 110–112 °C; syrup poured in a thin stream down the side of the bowl.
2. Whip at high speed until stiff and glossy, then continue until the meringue reads **≤25 °C** (10–15 min).
3. Continue from SMBC step 3.

### Crème mousseline

1. **Cook the crème pâtissière** as in [R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md), boiling it for 1–2 min after it thickens.
2. **First butter:** take off the heat and whisk in the **cold 125 g** of butter in pieces while the cream is hot (about 60–80 °C). It melts in and enriches the cream.
3. **Cool fast:** spread onto the lined tray, press cling film onto the surface, and cool to **18–22 °C** (about 30–45 min in the fridge; check with the probe; don't let it get fridge-cold). For food safety, the shallow tray should take it from 60 °C to about 20 °C well within 2 hours; finish the mousseline straight away.
4. **Beat smooth:** put the cooled pastry cream in the mixer with the paddle and beat 1 min until smooth and elastic.
5. **Second butter:** switch to the whisk and add the **soft 125 g** of butter (18–20 °C) in pieces, on medium speed, then whip on medium-high for **3–5 min** until lighter in colour and creamy. Add the kirsch or vanilla.
6. **Check:** 19–22 °C, smooth, glossy, holds a piped rosette. Use at once for filling, then refrigerate the assembled product. Keep refrigerated; use within 2–3 days.

### Reference: French buttercream (pâte à bombe)

1. Whip the yolks on high speed until pale and thick.
2. Cook the syrup (120 g sugar, 40 g water) to **118–121 °C** and pour it in a thin stream down the side of the bowl onto the whipping yolks.
3. Whip until thick, pale, ribbon-forming and cooled to **≤25 °C** (8–10 min). This is the pâte à bombe.
4. Add the butter as for SMBC. For vulnerable eaters, whisk yolks and syrup over a bain-marie to **71 °C** or higher before whipping ([R1-16](recipes/R1-16-sabayon.md)), or use pasteurized yolks.

### Reference: American buttercream

1. Beat the butter with the paddle on medium speed for 2 min until creamy.
2. Add the sifted icing sugar in three additions on low speed (to avoid a cloud), then the cream, vanilla and salt.
3. Beat on medium-high for **4–6 min** until pale and fluffy and no longer gritty between the fingers.

### Rescue

| What you see | Temperature | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| **Curdled**: lumpy, cottage-cheese look, liquid at the edge | Below ~17 °C | Keep the mixer running on medium and warm the outside of the bowl for 5–10 s at a time (warm wet towel, hair dryer, or a torch held well away), until it comes together. Or melt about 10 % of the cream to 35–40 °C and pour it back in while beating. |
| **Soupy**: shiny, runny, flat | Above ~25 °C | Refrigerate the bowl 10–15 min until the edges firm, then rewhip on medium-high. Repeat if needed. |

**Deliberate rescue drill:** take 150 g of finished SMBC. (a) Refrigerate 30 min, then beat: record the temperature at which it curdles and rescue it by warming. (b) Warm it over a bain-marie to 27–28 °C, then beat: record the soup and rescue it by chilling. Record both recovery temperatures.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Swiss meringue sugar dissolved | 70 °C (71 °C for safety), no grit | Undissolved sugar stays gritty in the finished cream |
| Italian syrup temperature | 118–121 °C, corrected | Controls meringue firmness |
| Meringue temperature at first butter | ≤25 °C | Warm meringue melts the butter: soup |
| Butter temperature | 18–20 °C | Colder: curdles; warmer: soup, greasy |
| Butter addition | 20 g pieces, 10–15 s apart | Too fast overloads the emulsion |
| Finished cream temperature | 19–22 °C | The plastic range of butterfat |
| Mousseline cream temperature before second butter | 18–22 °C | Cold cream curdles the butter |
| Flavour additions | Chocolate at 30–35 °C; liquids ≤10–15 % | Temperature shock or excess water splits it |
| Smoothing | 5–10 min on lowest speed | Removes bubbles for a clean coating |
| Storage | Meringue buttercreams: refrigerated 1 week, frozen 2–3 months; mousseline refrigerated 2–3 days | Egg and dairy; flavour and safety |

## Scientific explanation

A meringue buttercream is a fat-continuous system: partly crystalline butterfat surrounds droplets of concentrated sugar syrup and air bubbles carried in by the meringue ([13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). The fat can do this only while it is plastic, a network of fat crystals holding liquid oil, which for butter is roughly 18–22 °C ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)). Below that range the fat is too solid to flow around the aqueous phase, so it forms separate lumps with syrup between them (curdled). Above it too much fat is liquid to hold air or keep the syrup dispersed (soupy). The normal curdled stage halfway through mixing is the same problem in a different form: there is not yet enough fat to take up all the meringue's syrup. In crème mousseline the aqueous phase is a starch-thickened custard; the first butter, melted into the hot cream, enriches it, and the second, beaten in at the plastic temperature, builds the light, fat-continuous structure that lets it hold a shape. American buttercream has almost no water: the fat is loaded with fine sugar particles, so it is dense and very sweet, and its surface crusts as the little water there evaporates.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Butter in SMBC | 280 → 250 g per 100 g white | Lighter, sweeter, less stable when warm |
| Butter in SMBC | 280 → 320 g | Richer, firmer when cold, more heat-tolerant, more "buttery" |
| Sugar in SMBC | 160 → 180 g | Sweeter, slightly more stable meringue |
| Meringue type | Swiss → Italian | Slightly lighter and firmer; sharper piping |
| Salt | 0.2 → 0.4 % | Lower perceived sweetness |
| Butter in mousseline | 50 → 25 % of pastry cream | Softer, lighter, less firm in a cut cake |
| Finished cream temperature | 20 → 24 °C | Softer, glossier, closer to soupy; less piping definition |
