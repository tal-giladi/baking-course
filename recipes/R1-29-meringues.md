# R1-29 · French, Swiss and Italian meringue

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation built on established technique: the three classical meringues, all at the same 1 : 2 white-to-sugar ratio so you can compare them directly, plus a pavlova variant. Ratios, syrup temperatures and drying temperatures are standard professional practice. It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make French, Swiss and Italian meringue from 100 g of egg white each, to a stiff, glossy peak, with measured endpoints: bain-marie temperature for Swiss, syrup temperature for Italian, final temperature and specific gravity (SG) for all three. Pipe and bake French meringues dry and white at 100 °C, and torch Italian meringue.

## Expected result

- **French:** bright white, glossy, stiff peak; lightest of the three (SG about 0.15–0.22). Baked: white to faint ivory, crisp and dry right through, very light, lifts cleanly off the paper, 25–28 % weight loss.
- **Swiss:** very glossy, satiny, dense, stiff peak that stands straight (SG about 0.22–0.32); smooth, no grit between the fingers; holds piped ridges for hours.
- **Italian:** smooth, glossy, fine-textured, stiff; holds sharp piped detail (SG about 0.20–0.28); torches to an even golden brown without weeping.
- **Pavlova:** crisp, pale crust that cracks a little as it cools, and a soft, marshmallow-like centre.

## Formula

The base for the percentages is the egg white (white = 100 %). Separate the eggs cold and let the whites come to 18–22 °C. Large eggs give about 30–35 g of white each: about 3 whites per batch.

**French meringue (1 : 2)**

| Ingredient | % of white | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Egg whites, 18–22 °C | 100 | 100 |
| Cream of tartar | 1 | 1 |
| Caster (superfine) sugar | 100 | 100 |
| Icing sugar, sifted | 100 | 100 |
| **Total** | **301** | **301** |

**Swiss meringue (1 : 2)**

| Ingredient | % of white | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Egg whites | 100 | 100 |
| Caster or granulated sugar | 200 | 200 |
| **Total** | **300** | **300** |

**Italian meringue (1 : 2)**

| Ingredient | % of white | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Egg whites | 100 | 100 |
| Sugar, whipped into the whites | 20 | 20 |
| Sugar, for the syrup | 180 | 180 |
| Water, for the syrup | 60 | 60 |
| **Total (before evaporation)** | **360** | **360** |

Syrup at 118–121 °C: 180 ÷ 0.87 ≈ 207 g, so about 33 g of water boils off. Finished meringue ≈ **327 g**.

**Pavlova variant**

| Ingredient | % of white | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Egg whites | 100 | 100 |
| Caster sugar | 200 | 200 |
| Cornstarch | 5 | 5 |
| White vinegar (or lemon juice) | 5 | 5 |
| Vanilla extract | 2 | 2 |
| **Total** | **312** | **312** |

**Yield:** each batch is about 300–330 g of meringue, about 1.2–1.5 L of foam. French: about 60 kisses of 5 g, or 10 nests of 8 cm (about 25 g each) and a few kisses. Swiss and Italian: tops one 22–24 cm tart generously, or makes 60 piped kisses. Pavlova: one 18 cm disc, 5–6 cm high.
**Scaling:** ×0.5 (50 g white) whips poorly in a stand mixer; use a hand mixer. ×2 fits a 5 L stand mixer.
**Allergens:** egg.

## Equipment

- Stand mixer with whisk, or a hand mixer (for the Italian meringue a stand mixer is much easier: you need one hand for the syrup).
- Scrupulously clean, grease-free bowl (stainless steel or glass; wipe with a paper towel dampened with vinegar, then dry).
- Heatproof bowl and a pan for the bain-marie (Swiss); small heavy saucepan, 14–16 cm, with a pouring lip (Italian).
- Probe thermometer, calibrated ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md)).
- 100 ml measuring cup for SG, scale (1 g, 0.1 g for cream of tartar if available).
- Piping bags; 10–12 mm plain tip and a medium open-star tip.
- Two baking trays lined with parchment (a dab of meringue under each corner holds it down). Oven thermometer.
- Kitchen torch (for Italian).

## Mise en place

1. **Oven:** preheat to **100 °C (212 °F)** conventional (no fan, or fan at 90 °C) with an oven thermometer on the middle rack. Many ovens are unreliable at low settings; check the actual temperature.
2. Separate the eggs cold, one at a time into a small bowl before adding to the main bowl, so a broken yolk costs one white, not the batch. **Any trace of yolk will reduce the volume.**
3. Weigh whites and let them reach 18–22 °C (about 30 min out of the fridge).
4. For Swiss: bring 3–4 cm of water to a bare simmer in a pan on which the bowl sits without touching the water.
5. For Italian: weigh the syrup sugar and water into the saucepan; have a brush and cup of water ready ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md), syrup C).
6. Prepare trays, bags and tips before you start whipping; French meringue must be piped within 10–15 minutes.

## Procedure

### French meringue

1. Whip the whites with the cream of tartar on medium speed until **foamy**: large bubbles, translucent, no peak (about 1 min).
2. Increase to medium-high and whip to **soft peak**: opaque, the peak folds over (2–3 min in a stand mixer).
3. With the mixer running, add the **caster sugar** about 1 tablespoon (12–15 g) at a time, every 20–30 seconds (3–4 min in total).
4. Whip at high speed for 2–3 minutes more until **stiff and glossy**: the peak stands straight, the meringue clings to the inverted whisk, and a little rubbed between the fingers feels almost smooth.
5. **Fold in the icing sugar** in two additions with a large spatula, just until no streaks remain (10–15 turns each). Don't overmix; you will lose volume.
6. **Measure SG:** fill the 100 ml cup level, weigh, divide by 100. Record. Expect about 0.15–0.22.
7. **Pipe at once.** Kisses: 10–12 mm plain or star tip, 3 cm across, about 5 g. Nests: 8 cm spiral base with a double ring on the edge. Leave 2–3 cm between pieces.
8. **Weigh the full tray** (tare the tray and parchment first) and record.
9. **Bake at 100 °C:** kisses **60–90 min**, nests **1.5–2 h**. Do not open the door in the first hour. Colour should stay white or faintly ivory.
10. **Dry in the oven:** turn off the oven and leave the meringues inside with the door propped open 1–2 cm for **at least 1 h** (overnight is fine).
11. **Endpoint:** they lift cleanly off the parchment, sound hollow when tapped, and a broken test piece is dry and crisp right through when cool. **Weigh the tray: target 25–28 % loss.** Less than about 22 % means the centres are still wet: return them to a 90–100 °C oven.
12. Store airtight at room temperature, with a silica gel sachet if you have one, for up to 2 weeks. Never refrigerate.

### Swiss meringue

1. Put the whites and sugar in the heatproof bowl over the barely simmering water. **Whisk constantly** by hand, scraping the sides of the bowl, so the whites at the edge do not cook.
2. Heat to **65–70 °C**, about 4–6 minutes. For guests who are pregnant, elderly, very young or immunocompromised, heat to **71 °C (160 °F)**, or use pasteurized whites. **Endpoint:** the mixture is warm, slightly thickened, and a drop rubbed between your fingers has **no grains** at all. If you feel grit, keep heating and whisking.
3. Transfer to the mixer and whip at high speed. It will turn white and thick within 2 minutes, then gain gloss and stiffness.
4. Whip until **stiff, very glossy**, and the bowl feels cool to barely warm: **25–30 °C**, about 8–12 minutes. (For buttercream, continue to ≤25 °C; see [R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md).)
5. Measure SG and record. Expect about 0.22–0.32.
6. Pipe (it holds star-tip ridges very well) and bake as French meringue, or use as a topping. Baked Swiss meringues are denser and more candy-like in texture.

### Italian meringue

1. Put the syrup sugar and water in the saucepan and dissolve over medium heat, stirring. Once clear, stop stirring, wash down the walls, insert the thermometer and boil ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md), syrup C).
2. **When the syrup reaches 110–112 °C**, start whipping the whites on medium-high speed.
3. When the whites reach soft peak, add the 20 g sugar gradually and continue to **firm peak**. If the whites are ready before the syrup, turn the mixer to low to keep them moving; do not stop and do not overwhip.
4. When the syrup reaches **118–121 °C** (corrected; 121 °C for a firmer meringue that holds sharp piping), take it off the heat.
5. With the mixer on **medium-high**, pour the syrup in a **thin, steady stream down the side of the bowl**, between the whisk and the wall, over about 30–45 seconds. Do not let it hit the whisk.
6. Increase to high speed and whip until **stiff, glossy and cooled to 30–35 °C** (about 8–12 min) for piping or torching, or **≤25 °C** for buttercream.
7. Measure SG and record. Expect about 0.20–0.28.
8. **Torch test:** pipe or spread a spoonful on a plate and brown it with the torch held 5–8 cm away, moving continuously. It should brown evenly without weeping.

<div class="callout safety">

**Safety:** Italian meringue syrup is at 118–121 °C. Keep the pan handle turned inward, pour away from your body with your face back from the bowl, and keep a bowl of iced water to hand for burns (cool the skin under running water for 20 minutes). Raw egg: French meringue is raw until baked; Swiss meringue at 71 °C is pasteurized; the Italian method is not a validated pasteurization step. For uncooked uses (mousse, torched topping) served to vulnerable people, use pasteurized whites, which whip more slowly and benefit from 1 % cream of tartar.

</div>

### Pavlova variant

1. Oven at **120 °C (250 °F)**. Draw an 18 cm circle on parchment, turn it over.
2. Make a French meringue with all 200 g sugar added as caster sugar in step 3 (no icing sugar, no cream of tartar). Whip to stiff and glossy, 3–4 minutes after the last sugar.
3. Mix the cornstarch into the vinegar and vanilla, and fold into the meringue in 5–6 turns.
4. Spread into the circle, 5–6 cm high, with a shallow hollow in the top and rough ridges up the sides.
5. Bake at 120 °C for 10 minutes, then lower to **100–110 °C** for **75–90 min** more, until the crust is dry and crisp to the touch and pale ivory.
6. Turn the oven off and let the pavlova cool in the oven with the door ajar for at least 1 h. It cracks a little as it cools; this is expected.
7. Top with whipped cream and fruit just before serving; the cream softens the crust within 1–2 h.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fat-free equipment and whites | No yolk, clean bowl | Fat interferes with protein films; volume and stability drop |
| White temperature | 18–22 °C | Cold whites foam slowly |
| French: sugar timing | After soft peak, in 12–15 g additions | Early sugar slows foaming; fast addition deflates |
| Swiss: bain-marie endpoint | 65–70 °C (71 °C for safety), no grit | Undissolved sugar makes it gritty and weepy |
| Italian: syrup temperature | 118–121 °C, corrected | Lower: soft, less stable; higher: hard threads |
| Italian: syrup pouring | Thin stream down the bowl wall | Syrup on the whisk forms glass threads and lumps |
| Whip-to-cool endpoint | 25–35 °C depending on use | Warm meringue slumps; for buttercream melts butter |
| Oven temperature | 90–110 °C measured | Higher browns and cracks; lower does not dry |
| Drying endpoint | 25–28 % weight loss; dry through | Chewy, sticky centres otherwise |
| Storage | Airtight, dry, room temperature | Meringue absorbs moisture and softens |

## Scientific explanation

In all three methods, egg-white proteins unfold at the air–water surface and bond into elastic films around the bubbles ([09.2](stage-1/module-09/lesson-02.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). Sugar, dissolved in the water of the whites, turns the liquid between the bubbles into a thick syrup that drains slowly, which is what makes a meringue stable enough to pipe and bake. The methods differ in how completely the sugar is dissolved and how much heat the proteins see. French meringue has raw proteins and partly undissolved sugar: the most volume, the least stability. In Swiss meringue the sugar is fully dissolved and the proteins partly unfolded at 60–70 °C before whipping: denser, finer, glossier and stable. In Italian meringue the sugar arrives as an 87 % syrup at 118–121 °C that partly cooks the foam as it forms: fine, firm and the most stable. Baking at 90–110 °C removes water slowly; sugar becomes a glass supported by coagulated protein. Hotter ovens expand the air faster than the surface can stretch (cracks) and brown the sugar and proteins (ivory to beige). In the pavlova, cornstarch gelatinizes with the water kept in the thick centre, and acid stabilizes the foam, giving a soft set centre instead of a weeping one ([13.2](stage-1/module-13/lesson-02.md)).

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar ratio | 1 : 2 → 1 : 1 | Faster, lighter, less stable, less glossy; bakes less crisp |
| Cream of tartar (French) | 1 % → 0 | Narrower window before overwhipping; slightly less stable, less white |
| Italian syrup temperature | 118 → 121 °C | Firmer meringue, sharper piping; less supple for mousse |
| Swiss bain-marie temperature | 60 → 75 °C | Denser, heavier; risk of cooked specks above ~75–80 °C |
| Oven temperature | 100 → 120 °C | Faster, ivory to beige, risk of cracking and sticky centres |
| Piece size | Kisses → 8 cm shells | Longer drying (1.5–2 h+) |
| Humidity | Dry → humid day | Longer drying, softening within hours in storage |
