# R1-41 · Layer cake / simple entremet

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

An educational formulation built on established technique: a three-layer génoise cake soaked with a flavoured syrup, filled with Swiss meringue buttercream and fruit, crumb coated and finished on a turntable. The alternative is the same cake assembled in a ring with acetate and filled with crème diplomate and fruit, a simple entremet and the bridge to Stage 2. This is an **assembly recipe**: every component comes from its own recipe, linked below; this page gives the quantities for one 20 cm cake, the order of work and the control points at assembly. Weights are standard professional proportions, written for this course.

## Objective

Assemble a 20 cm layer cake that is level, straight-sided and sharp-edged, moist but not wet, and cuts into 12 equal slices with even, clean layers, by weighing the syrup into each layer, containing the filling, and controlling the buttercream temperature at each stage.

## Expected result

- **Whole cake:** about 1.5 kg, 20–21 cm across, 8–9 cm tall; level top (within 3 mm across), vertical sides, a sharp top edge; smooth finish with no crumbs or air pockets; a neat shell border and 12 rosettes.
- **Slice (1/12, about 125 g) at 16–20 °C:** three sponge layers of equal thickness (1.2–1.5 cm) separated by two even filling layers (about 1 cm) with fruit visible, and a 4–5 mm coat. The sponge is moist and tender, springs back, and does not drip when pressed.
- **Taste:** buttery, not greasy; sweetness balanced by the fruit; syrup flavour noticeable but not boozy or sugary.

## Equipment

- Turntable; 20 cm cake board (or 22 cm for a visible margin).
- Long serrated knife (at least 30 cm); toothpicks or two rulers as cutting guides; ruler.
- Large and small offset spatulas; bench scraper (straight, stainless, at least 10 cm tall).
- Pastry brush; scale (1 g); probe thermometer.
- Piping bags; 10 mm plain tip (dams), medium star tip (8–10 mm) for borders and rosettes.
- 12-portion cake marker (or a paper template); long thin knife and hot water for cutting.
- Option B: 20 cm × 6–7 cm cake ring; acetate strip 8 cm wide and about 65 cm long; flat board with parchment.

## Mise en place

- **Day −2 or earlier:** buttercream made ([R1-30](recipes/R1-30-buttercreams.md)); keeps 1 week refrigerated or 3 months frozen. Génoise may be baked and frozen.
- **Day −1:** génoise baked ([R1-18](recipes/R1-18-genoise.md)), cooled, wrapped, rested overnight at room temperature. Syrup made and cooled ([R1-28](recipes/R1-28-sugar-syrups.md) B). Option B: pastry cream made ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)).
- **Day 0, 1 h before assembly:** buttercream out of the refrigerator (or thawed overnight in the refrigerator) and re-beaten with the paddle to **20–22 °C**: smooth, silky, matt. If it looks curdled it is too cold: warm the bowl sides for a few seconds and keep beating. If it looks greasy and soft it is too warm: chill 10 min and re-beat.
- Fruit washed, dried on paper, raspberries halved or strawberries cut into 1 cm dice; kept on paper.
- Piping bags filled: 10 mm plain tip with buttercream for dams.
- Clear, level refrigerator shelf with 12 cm of headroom.

## Procedure

### Option A: turntable

**Split and soak**

1. **Level.** Génoise on the turntable (chilled 30 min if soft). With the serrated knife held level at the height where any dome begins, saw in short strokes while turning the cake against the knife; remove the dome. **Endpoint:** flat top; height 4.0–4.5 cm.
2. **Split** into three layers of 1.3–1.5 cm: mark with toothpicks every 1.3–1.5 cm at four points around the side, cut turning the cake against the knife, lift each layer onto a board. Weigh the layers (target about 130 g each).
3. **Board.** Put a teaspoon of buttercream on the cake board; set the **original top layer** (now the base) on it, cut side up. Press gently.
4. **Soak the bottom layer.** Put the board on the scale, tare, and brush **30 g** syrup from the edges inward, in two passes.

**Fill and stack**

5. **Dam.** Pipe a ring of buttercream (10 mm tip) just inside the edge of the layer, about 1 cm tall.
6. **Fill.** Spread **150 g** buttercream inside the dam, level with the offset spatula; press **100 g** fruit into it evenly, keeping fruit 1 cm from the edge.
7. **Middle layer.** Place it centred, press gently with a flat board to level. Soak with **35 g** syrup. Repeat the dam, 150 g buttercream and 100 g fruit.
8. **Top layer.** Place the **original bottom layer** (flat, pan side) cut side down. Press with a board; check level at eye height and correct by pressing the high side. Brush **40 g** syrup onto the top.
9. **Chill 15–20 min** until the dams and filling are firm.

**Coat**

10. **Crumb coat.** Spread about **120 g** buttercream thinly (1–2 mm) over the top and sides, filling gaps between layers; scrape smooth. Do not return any scraped buttercream to the main bowl. **Chill 20–30 min** until the coat is firm to the touch (≤ 10 °C).
11. **Final coat.** Re-beat the buttercream to 20–22 °C. Pile about **250 g** on top and on the sides. Spread the top outward past the edge. Load the sides from the bottom up.
12. **Smooth the sides:** bench scraper vertical, edge at 30–45° to the surface, resting on the board; rotate the turntable in one continuous movement with your free hand. Scrape the excess into a separate bowl. Fill holes; repeat 2–3 passes. **Endpoint:** vertical, smooth sides, a raised crown above the top.
13. **Top edge:** with a clean offset spatula held almost flat, pull the crown in toward the centre in short strokes, wiping the spatula each time. **Endpoint:** a sharp, square edge.
14. **Chill 20–30 min.** For a finer finish, make one final pass with a scraper warmed in hot water and dried.

**Decorate**

15. **Mark** 12 portions lightly on top with the marker.
16. **Pipe** a shell border at the base (star tip), then one rosette on each portion near the edge (total about **80 g**). Put a piece of fruit on each rosette (about **50 g**).
17. **Chill** at least 1 h (buttercream set) before cutting. Weigh the finished cake and record.

### Option B: ring and acetate

1. Line the 20 cm ring with the acetate strip, overlapping ends taped outside; set it on the parchment-lined board.
2. Level and split the génoise as in steps 1–2, then trim each layer 2–3 mm all round (removing the crust) so it fits inside the acetate with a 2–3 mm gap.
3. Bottom layer in the ring; soak with **20 g** syrup.
4. Make the diplomate now ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md) steps 12–16); it starts to set within 30–60 min. Pipe **100 g** diplomate into the gap and over the layer, press **125 g** fruit into it (1 cm from the acetate), cover with another **100 g** diplomate and level. The diplomate should fill the gap against the acetate.
5. Middle layer; soak **23 g**; repeat 200 g diplomate and 125 g fruit.
6. Top layer; soak **27 g**. Press gently level.
7. Chill at **≤ 5 °C** at least **3–4 h** (or overnight) until the diplomate is set.
8. Whip the stabilized Chantilly ([R1-27](recipes/R1-27-creme-chantilly.md)) to medium peak; spread **150 g** on top and level with a palette knife across the ring, or pipe it.
9. **Unmould:** lift the ring straight up, then peel the acetate in one continuous movement. Decorate the top with **60 g** fruit. Keep at ≤ 5 °C.

### Cutting and holding

18. **Temper:** buttercream cake out of the refrigerator **30–45 min** before cutting (buttercream 10–14 °C for cutting; 16–20 °C to eat). Option B: 10–15 min at most.
19. **Cut** with a long, thin knife dipped in hot water and wiped dry; one downward stroke, pull out at the base; clean between cuts. Follow the marks for 12 portions; weigh 3 slices and record.
20. **Hold:** Option A: covered, refrigerated, up to 3 days (2 days with fresh fruit inside); freezes well (freeze uncovered until hard, then wrap; thaw overnight in the refrigerator in its wrapping). Option B: **≤ 5 °C**, eat within **24–48 h**; do not freeze. Keep the cake in a box when it comes out of the refrigerator into a warm room, to limit condensation.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Génoise | Day-old, levelled, 3 layers of 1.3–1.5 cm, ±2 mm | Even layers = level cake and even slices; day-old crumb cuts cleanly |
| Syrup | Weighed: 30/35/40 g (Option A), 20/23/27 g (Option B) | Too little = dry cake; too much = soggy base that slides |
| Buttercream temperature | 20–22 °C for filling, coating and piping | Colder tears the sponge and drags crumbs; warmer slides and will not hold an edge |
| Dam | Continuous ring, 1 cm tall, inside the edge | Contains soft filling and fruit; prevents bulging |
| Crumb coat | Thin, chilled until firm (≤ 10 °C) | Locks crumbs away from the final coat |
| Level | Checked after stacking, before coating | A lean cannot be fixed after the coat |
| Diplomate (Option B) | Set ≥ 3–4 h at ≤ 5 °C before unmoulding | Gelatin gel carries the layers; unset diplomate bulges |
| Service | Buttercream 16–20 °C; cream fillings ≤ 5 °C until 15 min before service | Texture, flavour and food safety |

## Scientific explanation

The layer cake is a structure built from soft materials. The génoise is a set egg-and-starch foam with a low fat content: its open, connected cells take up syrup by capillarity, and the dissolved sugar in the syrup keeps the crumb moist (it lowers the water activity, so the water is held more tightly and evaporates more slowly) and tender (sugar plasticizes the cell walls) ([10.3](stage-1/module-10/lesson-03.md), [SC-01](stage-1/science/sc-01-water.md)). Weighing the syrup matters because the capillary uptake is uneven: the cut surfaces take syrup fastest, and over-soaked bottom layers collapse under load and drain onto the board.

Swiss meringue buttercream is a water-in-fat emulsion stabilized by the meringue proteins and the butterfat crystal network ([13.3](stage-1/module-13/lesson-03.md), [SC-06](stage-1/science/sc-06-colloids.md)). Its consistency is set by the fraction of butterfat that is solid, which changes steeply between about 15 and 25 °C ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)): at 20–22 °C enough fat is solid to hold a shape and enough is liquid to spread smoothly. This is why every stage of assembly has a buttercream temperature, and why the chilled crumb coat (fat mostly solid) can be coated over without moving. The continuous fat phase also makes buttercream a good moisture barrier: the coated cake dries far more slowly than a bare sponge, and the fruit in the filling wets the adjacent sponge but not the finish.

In Option B the structure is carried by gelatin: the diplomate's gelatin forms a thermo-reversible gel below about 15 °C that holds the whipped cream's air and the layers in place; it melts again near body temperature, which is what gives the soft, melting texture ([R1-26](recipes/R1-26-creme-patissiere.md)).

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Syrup per layer | 20 → 50 g | Drier, springier → moister, heavier; above ~45 g the base goes soggy |
| Syrup concentration | 30 → 50 °Brix | Less sweet, more watery → sweeter, moister-feeling, keeps longer |
| Filling | Buttercream → diplomate → stabilized Chantilly | Rich and stable → lighter, chilled, shorter life |
| Layers | 2 → 3 → 4 | More layers = more filling-to-sponge contact, more even moisture, harder to keep level |
| Coat thickness | 3 → 6 mm | Thinner shows layers through; thicker is rich and sweet |
| Assembly | Turntable → ring and acetate | Skill-dependent sides → guaranteed straight sides, visible layers |
| Sponge | Génoise → chiffon ([R1-19](recipes/R1-19-chiffon-cake.md)) | Chiffon is moister and softer; needs less syrup and is weaker under load |

## Experiment suggestions

- Syrup series: soak three 8 cm rounds cut from the same génoise with 15 %, 25 % and 35 % of their weight in syrup; sandwich each with buttercream; taste and press-test after 4 h and 24 h.
- Buttercream temperature: coat three small cakes (or blocks of sponge) with buttercream at 17, 21 and 25 °C; photograph the finish and count visible crumbs.
- Holding: hold slices at 4 °C and at 20 °C; score moistness and buttercream texture at 1 h, 24 h and 48 h.
