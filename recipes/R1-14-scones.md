# R1-14 · Scones (rubbing-in method)

<span class="tag educational">Educational formulation</span>

This is an educational formulation of a British-style scone, built on the established rubbing-in method as described in the professional texts cited below. Proportions sit inside standard professional ranges (butter 20–25 %, sugar 10–15 %, baking powder 5–6 %, liquid 50–60 %). It is not a reproduction of any published recipe.

## Objective

Make about 10 tall, lightly sweet scones by the rubbing-in method. Rub cold butter (4–7 °C) into flour to a mix of fine crumbs and pea-size pieces, bind with cold milk in under 30 s, build layers with 2–3 folds, cut straight down and bake hot. Measure fat and dough temperatures, handling time and rise ratio.

## Expected result

- 9–10 round scones (6 cm cutter), each ~75 g raw, rising from 2.5 cm to **5–6 cm** tall (rise ratio ~2–2.4×), with straight sides.
- Golden-brown tops and bases; pale, soft, slightly torn sides at the "waist" where they rose.
- A scone splits horizontally by hand along a natural layer.
- Crumb: soft, light, tender and slightly flaky; moist, not dry or cottony; no raw doughy centre.
- Lightly sweet, buttery, no bitter or chemical aftertaste.

## Formula

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| Plain (all-purpose) flour, ~10–11.5 % protein | 100.0 | 400 |
| Unsalted butter, cold (4–7 °C), 1 cm cubes | 22.5 | 90 |
| Caster or fine granulated sugar | 12.5 | 50 |
| Baking powder (double-acting or SAPP-based) | 5.0 | 20 |
| Fine salt | 1.0 | 4 |
| Whole milk, cold (4–8 °C) | 55.0 | 220 |
| **Total** | **196.0** | **784** |

**Egg wash (not in the formula):** 1 egg beaten with a pinch of salt; or milk for a softer, paler top.
**Yield:** ~784 g dough → **9–10 round scones of ~75 g** with a 6 cm cutter at 2.5 cm thick (one reroll of scraps), or **12 square scones of ~65 g** cut with a knife from a 20 × 14 cm slab (no scraps).
**Hydration:** 55 % milk ≈ 48 % water on flour, plus ~14 g water from the butter: a soft, slightly sticky dough.
**Scaling:** ×0.5 (200 g flour) makes 4–5 scones and works well; the recipe is easy to halve. ×2 (800 g flour): rub in with a food processor or in two batches so the butter stays cold.

**Variants:**
- *Egg-enriched:* replace 50 g of the milk with 1 egg (50 g). Richer, more colour, slightly firmer and taller.
- *Buttermilk:* replace the milk with 220 g cold buttermilk, reduce baking powder to **12 g (3 %)** and add **1.6 g (0.4 %) baking soda** to neutralize its acid ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)). More tender, slight tang.
- *Fruit:* add 60 g sultanas or currants (15 %) after rubbing in.

## Equipment

- Scale (1 g); 0.1 g scale for the baking powder and soda.
- Large, wide bowl (chilled 15 min if the kitchen is above 22 °C); fine sieve.
- Pastry cutter, or food processor, or box grater (if using frozen butter).
- Table knife or flexible spatula for mixing.
- 6 cm plain round cutter (sharp edge), or a large knife; ruler.
- Heavy light-coloured baking tray lined with parchment.
- Probe thermometer; oven thermometer; pastry brush; cooling rack.

## Mise en place

1. Cube the butter 1 cm and refrigerate **≥20 min**; probe a cube: **4–7 °C**. In a warm kitchen, freeze it 10 min instead.
2. Keep the milk in the fridge until use.
3. Oven rack in the middle. Preheat to **220 °C (425 °F)** for ≥25 min; confirm with an oven thermometer. Use a light-coloured tray; if yours is dark, double it with a second tray underneath.
4. Sift the baking powder with ~50 g of the flour; whisk with the rest of the flour, sugar and salt for 30 s.

## Procedure

**Step 1 — Rub in (about 2–3 min).** Add the cold butter cubes to the dry mix and toss to coat. Rub between fingertips and thumbs, lifting the mixture and letting it fall, or cut in with a pastry cutter, or pulse **4–6 × 1 s** in a food processor. Stop when most of the mixture looks like **coarse breadcrumbs** with some flattened **pea-size pieces** (3–6 mm) of butter still visible. Shake the bowl: larger pieces rise to the top; rub those once more if many are larger than a pea.
*Check:* the mixture feels cool and dry, not greasy; if it starts to clump or feels soft, refrigerate the bowl 10 min.

**Step 2 — Add liquid (≤ 30 s).** Make a well, pour in **210 g** of the milk (hold back 10 g). Mix with a table knife or spatula using a cutting, lifting motion until no dry flour remains at the bottom of the bowl, **15–30 s**. Add the last 10 g milk only if dry patches remain. The dough is **shaggy, soft and slightly sticky**, not smooth.

**Step 3 — Check dough temperature.** Probe the centre: target **10–15 °C**. Above 15 °C, cover and refrigerate 20 min before step 4.

**Step 4 — Fold-and-pat (≤ 1 min).** Tip onto a lightly floured bench. Pat into a rectangle ~2 cm thick; fold in half; pat out again to 2 cm. Repeat for **2–3 folds** in total. Use the minimum bench flour; brush off excess.

**Step 5 — Final thickness.** Pat to an even **2.5 cm** (use a ruler, or two 2.5 cm guides either side).

**Step 6 — Cut.** Dip the cutter in flour and press **straight down, then lift straight up — no twisting**. Cut as close together as possible. Place on the tray **~1 cm apart** for tall, soft-sided scones, or 4 cm apart for crustier sides. Gently press scraps together (do not knead), pat to 2.5 cm once and cut again; bake the reroll scones separately or mark them: they will be less tall and tougher. For squares: pat to a 20 × 14 cm slab and cut 3 × 4 with a floured knife.

**Step 7 — Rest and wash.** Brush **only the tops** with egg wash; wash on the sides glues the layers. If the dough has warmed above 15 °C, chill the tray **15 min**.

**Step 8 — Bake at 220 °C (425 °F) for 12–15 min.** Rotate the tray at 8 min if your oven is uneven. Lower to 210 °C (410 °F) if your bases brown too fast.

**Step 9 — Doneness.** Tops and bases golden-brown; the pale sides spring back when pressed; **internal temperature 93–97 °C**; a scone lifts cleanly from the parchment and sounds slightly hollow underneath.

**Step 10 — Cool.** Transfer to a rack. For soft crusts, cover loosely with a clean cloth. Serve warm or within a few hours. Freeze unbaked cut scones on the tray, then bag; bake from frozen at 220 °C for 15–19 min.

## Critical control points

| CCP | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Butter temperature at rubbing | 4–7 °C | Hard enough to break into pieces, not smear; pieces make flakes and steam |
| Rubbing endpoint | Coarse crumbs + pea-size pieces (3–6 mm) | All fine: cakey, less rise. Large lumps: greasy holes, uneven rise |
| Mixing after liquid | 15–30 s, shaggy dough | Each extra second develops gluten and warms fat: tough, lower rise |
| Dough temperature | 10–15 °C before cutting | Above ~15 °C fat smears and leaks: spread, flat, greasy |
| Handling | 2–3 folds, one reroll maximum | Layers without toughness |
| Cutting | Straight down, no twist | Twisting seals edges: lopsided, low rise |
| Oven | 220 °C verified; light tray | Fast melt and steam before fat soaks in; avoids burnt bases |
| Internal temperature | 93–97 °C | Below: doughy centre; long past: dry |

## Common failures

- Scones spread, flat, greasy → [scones spread or did not rise](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=scones-spread-or-did-not-rise)
- Tough, chewy, shrank after cutting → [tough scones](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=tough-scones)
- Dry, crumbly, stale within hours → [dry crumbly scones](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=dry-crumbly-scones)
- Dark bases, pale tops → [burnt bottoms](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=burnt-bottoms)
- Bitter or metallic aftertaste, yellowish crumb → [soapy or metallic taste](troubleshooting/quick-breads.md?id=soapy-or-metallic-taste)
- Scones lean to one side → twisted cutter, wash on the sides, or an oven hot side ([08.4](stage-1/module-08/lesson-04.md))

## Scientific explanation

Cold butter rubbed into flour ends up in two forms ([08.4](stage-1/module-08/lesson-04.md)). The fine fraction coats flour particles and limits how much gluten the milk can develop, which makes the scone tender. The pea-size pieces stay solid through mixing because they are at 4–7 °C, where butter is about half solid fat ([SC-04](stage-1/science/sc-04-lipids.md)); folding flattens them into flakes. In the 220 °C oven, the pieces melt, and their ~16 % water turns to steam, which inflates the space each piece leaves. At the same time, the baking powder's slow acid releases CO₂ ([08.2](stage-1/module-08/lesson-02.md)), and the dough sets around the expanded voids, giving lift and a layered, flaky crumb that splits horizontally. The high baking powder level (5 %) compensates for a stiff dough with no creamed air and a large surface through which gas escapes. Short mixing and few folds keep gluten development low. With a warm dough or a cool oven, the fat melts into the dough before steam can form: the scone spreads and bakes dense and greasy.

## Variables you can change

| Variable | Direction | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|
| Butter form | Cold cubes → frozen and grated | More uniform flakes, taller, flakier ([X1-23](experiments/X1-23-scone-fat.md)) |
| Butter form | Cold → soft (20 °C) | Cakey, tender, less rise, more spread ([X1-23](experiments/X1-23-scone-fat.md)) |
| Butter level | 22.5 → 30 % | Richer, shorter, more tender; slightly more spread |
| Liquid | 55 → 60 % | Softer dough, lighter and more open if handled quickly; stickier to cut |
| Egg | 0 → 12.5 % (replacing milk) | More colour, richer, slightly firmer and taller |
| Folds | 2 → 6 | More layers at first, then tougher and shrinking |
| Oven | 220 → 190 °C | Lower rise, paler, more spread, greasier |
| Placement | 4 cm apart → touching | Taller, straighter, soft sides |
